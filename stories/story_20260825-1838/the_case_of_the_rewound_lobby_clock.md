'No-surely, you can't believe-' The voice cracked and he pressed a fist to his mouth. 'I had no reason. No cause-' But the others were already loosening. Captain Hale clapped him on the shoulder with the heartiness of a man relieved it was not his own shoulder being clapped. It fitted. 'The evidence is circumstantial,' she said, gently enough, 'but the timeline compels us. Dr. Finch, until something further comes to light, the suspicion must rest with you. The clock kept its silence above them, authoritative, certain, and no more trustworthy than it had been an hour ago. Outside, the wind leaned on the glass. The lamp beneath the key cabinet guttered in the draught, and Beatrice Quill reached across without looking and steadied it.

## Chapter 7: Secrets Beneath Secrets

Beatrice Quill steadied the lamp beneath the key cabinet, holding her hand there until the flame caught and threw a wavering glow across the reception office. Behind the corridor was cold and hushed with dusk, the air edged with damp wool and the faint metallic tang of floor polish. Shadows ran long across the linoleum. Every small sound-the creak of the office chair, the far-off clatter of crockery in the scullery-came louder than it had any right to, the autumn dark pressing flat against the windowpanes. Eleanor Voss paused on the threshold, gloved fingers resting on the doorframe. Lamplight slid over her notes and tipped the neat columns of names and hours into uneasy relief. The wind worried at the sealed window, rattling it in its frame, and when it dropped the quiet that followed was not restful.

She had heard that particular silence before, in rooms where somebody was deciding whether to speak. The staff corridor and reception office kept the household's brisk routine by day; tonight it held other things. 'You make it sound as though I'm about to be hanged for a missing teaspoon,' Beatrice said as Eleanor came in, her voice brittle. 'The clock's always been temperamental,' she said, a shade too lightly. 'It sticks, it sulks, it wants coaxing. I wind it every night, as I have for years.

If there's oil, or a scratch, it's nothing sinister-it's the price of keeping the place ticking. ' Her glance went to the staff schedule on its nail and came away again. 'If you fancy I've been up to mischief, you'll want more than a smudge of oil. ' By the filing cabinet, Sylvia Trent cleared her throat. Lamplight caught the sheen of her starched blouse; her voice, when it came, was worn thin. 'Miss Voss, if you're hunting lapses, you'll find them. I-' She stopped, twisted her hands together, and pushed on. 'I altered the staff log for last Tuesday. By half an hour, no more. I was late back from town and I didn't want it standing on the record. It has nothing whatever to do with Mr.

Vane. ' The words came out in a rush, brittle with relief and fright both. 'I thought if you saw the discrepancy you'd take me for something worse. ' Eleanor's voice softened, though its measure did not change. 'Thank you, Miss Trent. That accounts for the oddity in the log. ' She let a moment pass. 'It's not the kind of lie that kills a man. ' Sylvia's shoulders came down an inch, and Eleanor turned back to Beatrice. 'The scratch, the oil, the key left out. Those are not the marks of ordinary routine. ' She looked at the schedule on its nail. 'A little before the chimes struck eleven until the last echo past midnight, and no name at that desk but yours. And the chime lagged. Beatrice's bravado went from a second time, and what stood in its place was not theatre but plain tiredness. 'I do what's needed,' she said, so quietly the words were nearly lost. 'If that makes me suspect, so be it. But don't pretend the rest of you have never bent a rule. ' Her glance jumped to Sylvia and away. 'We all have our reasons. ' Eleanor turned the evidence over and found it would not lie the way it had lain before. An oil smudge, a scratch, a key left out-she had written them down as maintenance, the small greases and abrasions of an old house keeping time. They read now as a hand deliberately at work. The schedule had been drawn up to assure the household of order; hung on its nail in the lamplight, it showed instead exactly when the passage had been empty of everyone but one woman.

And Sylvia's half hour, which had sat crooked in the log for two days, was only a woman who had missed her train. A clock that failed of itself, a routine broken by chance-that account had carried her this far and would carry her no further. Beatrice laughed, a brittle sound with more weariness in it than mockery. 'Darling, if only clocks could be trusted as easily as people say they can. But then, whatever would we talk about? ' The line hung there a moment, a small ironic mercy, and the silence shut over it. Beyond the window the dusk had thickened, and the lamp seemed to give less light than before. Eleanor gathered her notes. Somewhere along the corridor a door closed, and she found she was listening for the chime, and for how late it would come.

## Chapter 8: The Pattern Emerges

"Let's have all the clocks in one place," Eleanor said. The lobby had gone quiet since dusk, and the cold came up through the marble as though the sea were lying directly beneath it. Wind off the water pushed at the glass in the doors, one gust after another. Brass polish, the metallic tang of the key rack, a thread of baking bread drifting in from the kitchen passage. At her words the others came in a step, none of them meaning to. Captain Ivor Hale stood a little apart and snapped his lighter open. The flame showed his face for a moment while he got the cigarette going, and it showed too that his hand was not quite steady, a thing he would have denied to his last hour. Beatrice Quill kept her arms folded and one brow raised at Eleanor, which was the posture, but her eyes went up to the clock above the desk and came back, and went up again. Dr.

Mallory Finch hung at the edge of the group, watching the punch-card Eleanor drew from her coat. Sylvia Trent held her notepad against her chest as though the day might still be got through in the usual order. Eleanor laid the kitchen punch-card on the desk and put out her hand for the captain's wristwatch. He unfastened it briskly and set it down beside the card, the hands bright under the lamp at a quarter past twelve. "Let's compare them, shall we? " She said it mildly. She was not looking at the watch at all; she was looking at the lobby clock, whose hands stood at a quarter to twelve. A pan rang somewhere behind the kitchen door. Outside, the wind kept up its low argument with the doors. At Eleanor's nod, Sylvia carried in the kitchen's wall clock, a plain enamelled face with none of the gilt of the one above the desk.

It agreed with the captain's watch to the minute, and both agreed with the midnight stamp on the punch-card. Eleanor crossed to the grand clock and laid a gloved hand flat on the glass. "The punch-card, the kitchen clock and the captain's watch," she said. "Three sources, one time between them. This one is thirty minutes behind. " She spoke the way a woman reads out a laundry list. Nobody in the lobby breathed. Beatrice shifted her weight. "It's not unusual for the old thing to lose time," she said, and the airiness had gone tight at the edges. "Salt air, poor maintenance, careless staff-take your pick. " Her glance went to the staff schedule pinned behind the desk and came away fast. Eleanor saw it go, and said nothing at all, and let the nothing sit there. Hale let out a plume of smoke and narrowed his eyes at the dial. "I set my shifts by that clock. Every meeting, every watch.

If it's wrong-" He stopped. "Then everything we've counted on wants looking at again. " He sounded less angry than robbed. Dr. Finch cleared his throat. "If the hour is wrong, every alibi hung on it comes down with it. We're adrift, Miss Voss. " His fingers, which had been steady enough over a body that morning, tapped a small uneven rhythm on the desk. Sylvia spoke without quite meaning to. "Miss Quill is the only one who manages the clock. She has the key. She knows the winding. " A flush climbed her throat. "None of the rest of us would go near it. " Eleanor went to the schedule and ran a finger down the column of names. "Miss Quill at the desk from the half hour before eleven to the first chime of the new day. Nobody else logged for those hours. " She let her finger rest there and looked up. Beatrice had a fold of her skirt worked between two knuckles.

"There is also the scratch on the glass," Eleanor said, "the oil at the winding keyhole, and the key lying out instead of in its drawer. All of it fresh. None of it accounted for. " The captain's watch ticked away on the desk, and for a moment it was the loudest thing in the room. "Two of the staff remember seeing you at the clock last night," Eleanor went on. "More than once. They say you seemed anxious. Intent on the mechanism. " The mask went for a moment; Beatrice's chin came down. "If I was anxious, it's because this whole house runs on that clock, and when it's wrong it's my name they say. " The arch tone had left her entirely. "Don't stand there and tell me you've never bent a rule to keep a place running. " Hale ground out his cigarette in the sand tray, harder than the job wanted. "We all trusted the routine.

If the clock's been lying, so has everything we built on it. " He looked at Beatrice, then found somewhere else to look, and let the rest of it go unsaid. Finch reached for his own watch and his hand shook taking it up. "If the case against me rests on that dial, it rests on nothing. Someone wanted the hour to fall earlier than it did. " Eleanor gathered the timepieces and set them in a row along the desk where every one of them could see. Two faces and a punch-card said one thing. Above the desk, the gilt hands said another, half an hour behind, and went on saying it. Beatrice produced a smile with nothing behind it. "Well, darling, that's the end of improvisation. Eleanor drew her gloves off, finger by finger, and set them on the desk beside the clocks. "One more test," she said, "and then we'll know. " In the lamplight not a face in the lobby moved.

## Chapter 9: The Final Trap

Late that night, as the wind battered the glass doors and the lobby glowed with the dim, uncertain light of the desk lamp, Eleanor Voss drew the group together before the reception. The marble floor was cold beneath her shoes; the air held a faint tang of brass polish and the sharper scent of autumn rain that had seeped in every time the doors were opened. The storm's howl pressed at the windows, but inside, the only sound was the muted tick of the desk's small clock and the shuffle of feet as the suspects assembled. -still hung in the air, unanswered, as Beatrice Quill's fingers worked the hem of her skirt, her bravado flickering like the lamp flame. Eleanor's gaze went first to the grand clock above the desk, its hands stubborn at a quarter to twelve. She let her eyes drop to the winding key, left out behind the reception desk, glinting in the lamplight.

It should have been locked in the manager's safe, as every staff member knew. The key's presence here, exposed and accessible, was the first crack in the story the hotel had told itself. 'There is your proof,' Eleanor said, holding the card aloft. 'The lobby clock is thirty minutes slow. Every alibi anchored to it is a fiction.
