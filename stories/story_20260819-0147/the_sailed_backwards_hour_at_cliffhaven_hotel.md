## Chapter 7: Shadow on the Rooftop

Beatrice Quill's shoes clicked on the rain-slicked tiles as she stepped onto the Hotel Rooftop Terrace, the night air thick with mist and the distant sound of waves breaking against the cliff below. Dr. Mallory Finch followed, her gloved hand brushing the cold stone balustrade, the glow from the lobby's distant lamps barely reaching this high. The mechanical whir from the Main Lobby still echoed in her mind, the image of the minute hand sweeping past the fixed hour refusing to fade. A hush settled between them, broken only by the wind's restless push against the ironwork. Dr. Mallory Finch paused beside the clock's rear casing, the damp air beading on her spectacles.

She watched Beatrice Quill, who hovered near the pulley, her clipboard clutched to her chest as if it might shield her from the darkness. The faint scent of wet stone and the sharper tang of metal drifted up from the mechanism at their feet. For a moment, Dr. Mallory Finch allowed herself to listen: not just to the wind, but to the subtle creak of the wire as it shifted in the night air. "It's colder up here than I remember," Beatrice Quill said, her voice brittle, eyes darting to the shadows that pooled near the edge of the terrace. Beatrice Quill exhaled, a shudder running through her. "There's always someone prowling about when they shouldn't," she muttered, but the bravado in her voice had faded. She turned away from the maid, focusing instead on the mechanism. Dr. Mallory Finch watched her, noting the tremor in her hands as she adjusted the clipboard-a telltale sign of nerves rarely betrayed by the manager. "You seem unsettled, Mrs. Quill," Dr. Mallory Finch observed quietly, her words more observation than accusation. Beatrice Quill's reply was sharp, but the edge was dulled by fatigue.

"I keep this place running, Doctor. I don't have time for shadows and stories. But if someone was up here, near the pulley, then that's a matter for you, not me. " She hesitated, then added, almost to herself, "I suppose I've always believed in keeping the ship afloat, even when the hull's leaking. " The admission, so uncharacteristic, hung between them, and Dr. Mallory Finch caught the faintest glimmer of fear in Beatrice Quill's eyes-a fear not just of accusation, but of losing control. She recognized the set: the same type Hugo Vane had been seen carrying, the tools marked with initials scratched in hurried strokes. The presence of these implements, so near the site of the maid's shadowy figure, sent a fresh ripple of unease through her. Beatrice Quill bent to peer at the tools, her breath visible in the cold night air. "Those aren't mine," she said, voice flat, but her eyes lingered on the initials, her brow furrowing. "He's always leaving things about-never where they belong. I warned him, but-" She stopped, the words trailing off as if she'd revealed too much. The implication was clear: someone with access, someone with skill, had been here, perhaps at the very moment the clock's fate was sealed. Dr. Mallory Finch straightened, the ache in her knees a dull reminder of the hour. She glanced at the maid, who hovered at the threshold, uncertain whether to flee or stay.

"Thank you," Dr. Mallory Finch said gently. "You've been very helpful. " The girl nodded, then slipped away, her footsteps fading down the stairwell, leaving only the wind and the hush of the terrace. For a moment, Dr. Mallory Finch let herself rest against the balustrade, the cold seeping through her coat. She looked out over the dark sea, the horizon lost to cloud and mist. The evidence was mounting, but the pattern remained elusive-a shadow at the pulley, tools left behind, Beatrice Quill's brittle composure cracking at the edges. The pressure on the manager was plain now: not just the burden of order, but the fear that order itself might be a lie. She turned back to the mechanism, her mind racing. The locked door to the terrace, the length of the thin control wire-twenty-five feet, she recalled, exactly as measured earlier-its path vanishing into the clock below.

The tools, the maid's testimony, the tremor in Beatrice Quill's hands: each detail was a thread, and together they wove a pattern that threatened to overturn every prior assumption. Yet the final connection, the means by which the hour had been fixed at a quarter to three, still hovered just out of reach. A brief, ironic smile flickered across Dr. Mallory Finch's lips as she caught Beatrice Quill's eye. "At least we're not short of mysteries tonight. If only the clock were as reliable as the staff logs. " The attempt at levity was met with a wan smile from Beatrice Quill, who shook her head. "If only, Doctor. But some things-no matter how well you plan-refuse to run on schedule. " The chill deepened as the night pressed in, the terrace now empty save for the two women and the silent clock. Dr. Mallory Finch gathered her notes, her mind already cataloguing the new evidence.

The shadowy figure near the pulley, the tools abandoned in haste, Beatrice Quill's momentary vulnerability-all had shifted the investigation's balance. The theory that had guided her thus far was no longer sufficient. She would need to return to the mechanism, to test and retest, until the clock's secrets finally yielded. But for now, the only certainty was the darkness, the wind, and the knowledge that the truth was closer-and more dangerous-than ever before.

## Chapter 8: The Clock's Secret Revealed

"You're still at it, Doctor? " Hugo Vane's voice, low and edged with fatigue, drifted across the Hotel lobby near the grandfather clock. The late-night hush pressed in, broken only by the muted tick of the clock and the distant echo of a radio fading somewhere down the corridor. Dr. Mallory Finch did not look up immediately. Her gloved fingers hovered above the polished wood, tracing the faint outline of the panel, the scent of old varnish and the sharper tang of cold metal mingling in the air. The lobby's lamplight cast long, uncertain shadows, and the autumn chill seeped through the glass doors, settling in her bones. She could feel Hugo Vane's gaze on her, wary and restless, as she returned her attention to the clock's inscrutable face. The silence between them was not companionable. Dr.

Mallory Finch's focus narrowed to the mechanism itself, her breath misting faintly in the cold air. She watched the minute hand complete its slow, unhurried circuit, the steady click marking each passing moment. Yet the hour hand-so bold, so certain in its position at a quarter to three-remained stubbornly unmoved. She let the seconds tick by, her own wristwatch confirming the minute hand's fidelity to real time. The hour hand, however, was an island of stasis. Her eyes narrowed. She reached for the small lever inside the case, the metal cool and slightly oily beneath her glove, and nudged it with practiced delicacy. The minute hand continued its journey, untroubled, but the hour hand refused to budge. There was no mechanical resistance-only a deliberate, unnatural stillness.

Hugo Vane shifted his weight, the sound of his shoes scuffing against the marble floor a brittle counterpoint to the clock's measured rhythm. "You'll wear a groove in the parquet if you keep pacing like that," Dr. Mallory Finch remarked, her tone dry, but her eyes never leaving the mechanism. The attempt at levity was thin, a brittle shield against the intensity of the moment. Hugo Vane managed a humorless chuckle. "I suppose I'm not used to being under such close observation. You've spent more time with that clock tonight than the staff have in a month. " His hands, usually so steady, fidgeted with the edge of his jacket. Dr. Mallory Finch caught the flicker of anxiety in his eyes-the way he studied her movements, as if searching for reassurance or, more likely, a warning. She straightened, the lamplight catching the fine dust on her gloves.

The air was thick with the scent of old paper and the faint, persistent odor of tobacco that clung to the velvet ropes cordoning off the scene. Dr. Mallory Finch turned to Hugo Vane, her expression unreadable. "You've always maintained the clock was the pride of Cliffhaven, Mr. Vane. Yet tonight it seems determined to betray us all. If the clock's gone wrong, blame the war, blame the salt air, blame anything but me. " His voice was steady, but his eyes darted to the wire that vanished behind the clock's casing. Dr. Mallory Finch crouched once more, her skirt brushing the cold marble. She followed the path of the nearly invisible wire, tracing its ascent from the hidden lever inside the clock up toward the ceiling.

"If you don't mind, Mr. Vane, I'd like to conduct a small experiment. I'll need someone on the Rooftop Terrace to pull the wire at my signal. " Her tone was brisk, almost clinical, but she noted the way Hugo Vane's hand tightened on the back of a nearby chair. "You think that will prove something? " he asked, voice raw. "It will prove whether the clock is telling us the truth," she replied, her gaze unwavering. The lobby's silence deepened, broken only by the faint creak of the clock's gears and the distant rattle of a passing tram outside. Dr. Mallory Finch checked her own watch again, synchronizing the minute hand's progress with the clock's face.

She observed the minute hand complete a full cycle, the hour hand remaining fixed, unmoved by the passage of time. The contradiction was no longer theoretical; it was visible, undeniable, and chilling in its implication. She let her breath out slowly, her mind racing through the possibilities. The mechanism had been tampered with-of that she was certain-but the precise method, the why and the who, remained just beyond her grasp. Hugo Vane's composure faltered. He stepped closer, his voice lowered. "You're not going to find what you're looking for, Doctor. Sometimes things break. Sometimes they're just old. " His words were defensive, but his eyes betrayed a flicker of something else-fear, perhaps, or the desperate hope that she would abandon her line of inquiry. Dr. Mallory Finch met his gaze, her own steady and unblinking. "Old things don't break like this, Mr. Vane.

Dr. Mallory Finch steeled herself for the test to come, knowing that the next step would force the truth into daylight, whatever the cost. The night pressed in, cold and inexorable, as the clock ticked on-one hand moving, the other unmoved, and the truth waiting just beyond the hour.

## Chapter 9: The Discriminating Test

By the time dawn pressed pale light through the stained glass of the Hotel lobby, the air was sharp with the scent of salt and distant tobacco, and the soft echo of footsteps seemed to bounce between marble and chrome. 'Many of your alibis depend on the time this clock displayed.

'Beatrice, you were recorded in the Staff Basement, your presence verified by two staff signatures, every quarter hour from eight until nearly nine. The evidence clears you. ' Beatrice Quill's shoulders eased, the tension in her jaw softening as she let out a breath she'd been holding. She turned to Captain Ivor Hale. 'Captain, you dined with the Colonel and his wife from eight fifteen onward. The meal records, signed by the headwaiter, place you at the table through the critical window. Dr. Mallory Finch met her gaze. 'Sylvia, your presence was logged at the dining hall and kitchen throughout the evening. Staff and guest testimony alike confirm you never entered the lobby, nor did you approach the terrace. The witness notes from the headwaiter and Mrs. Quill corroborate every step. You could not have been near the mechanism at the crucial time. The alibi holds. ' Sylvia Trent's mouth curved in a brief, brittle smile, but she did not speak. At last, Dr. Mallory Finch turned to Hugo Vane. 'Mr.

Vane, your alibi places you on the Rooftop Terrace during the period the clock was fixed at a quarter to three-but you alone had the keys, and you alone possess the technical skill and tools to manipulate the mechanism. The staff logs do not account for your movements precisely, and the tools found near the pulley bear your initials. Dr. Mallory Finch gave a signal-a single, sharp nod. On the terrace, Sylvia Trent pulled the thin wire. All eyes fixed on the clock's face. Dr. Mallory Finch called out the time from her wristwatch: 'Eight fifty-six. ' The grandfather clock's face refused to acknowledge the passing minute-the hour hand, disengaged from its gear train, had been frozen by the wire's pull. The minute hand, unimpeded, cycled forward. The demonstration was irrefutable.

She let the tension hold for a breath, then spoke, her tone precise. 'This mechanism proves the hour hand could be fixed for forty-five minutes while the minute hand continued, creating the illusion of an earlier time. The only person with the means, opportunity, and mechanical knowledge to install and operate such a device is Hugo Vane. Beatrice Quill, Captain Ivor Hale, and Sylvia Trent are cleared-the records and testimonies eliminate them. But you, Mr. Vane, are left with no such defense. ' Hugo Vane's face tightened; his bravado slipped away, replaced by a raw, cornered look. Dr. Mallory Finch stepped closer, fixing him with a steady gaze.
