## Chapter 6: Carpet Wear Marks

The disturbance matches a ninety degrees swing—exactly what you'd expect if the panel pivoted here to open a passage three feet wide behind the wall." Marguerite Selwyn, standing by the window, drew the curtain aside and peered out into the darkness, the glass cool beneath her palm. "You're saying someone moved the panel—recently?"

"The wear marks on the carpet align perfectly with the pivot point of the rotating wall panel. Whoever used it, did so within the last day or so."

"You said the wear was recent," Marguerite commented. She stepped to the end of the bed and ran her hand along the embroidered coverlet. "How recent?"

Adela picked up a small brush and dusted the baseboard, collecting a sample of debris in a glass vial. "Recent enough that the dust has not resettled. The panel must have been moved after the last thorough cleaning—no more than a day or two ago."

Adela paused, studying the pattern of marks. "I have a method. The question is who had the knowledge and opportunity to use it."

Ottoline approached, stooping to examine the base of the panel. She pulled a small flashlight from her pocket, its weak beam glancing off the seam. "Could anyone have used it? Or only someone with inside knowledge?"

Adela answered. "The construction is not obvious. Unless you knew about the hinge and the precise place to lift the carpet, you would never find the pivot. It points to someone with detailed familiarity—either staff or someone with access to estate plans."

Marguerite crossed to the desk and picked up a letter opener, turning it over in her hand. She set it down with care, aligning it with the blotter. "There are only a few who would qualify, then."

A sardonic smile flickered across Harriet's lips. She picked up the lamp and shifted its beam, angling it to cast a long shadow across the floor. "If you're hunting for ghosts, you'd better bring a torch."

Adela replied. "I prefer footprints to phantoms." She followed the shadow to the far corner, where the carpet met the wall. The marks faded there, ending abruptly—a sign the panel's swing was limited by the width of the corridor behind.

Ottoline knelt, tracing the edge of the carpet with her thumb. "It's clever, I'll give them that. But it still doesn't tell you who moved it."

"Not quite," Adela agreed. She made another note, recording the angle and the position of the seam. "But it narrows the field. And with enough time and careful scrutiny, the one who left these marks will reveal themselves."

Marguerite watched her, a flicker of anxiety crossing her face. She reached for the desk lamp, adjusting its position so that its light fell on a framed photograph of Cecil Thorne—alive, unsmiling, and resolute. She lingered over the image. She set it upright.

Ottoline, seeing the gesture, stood and crossed to the wardrobe. She opened it, revealing a row of neatly pressed shirts and a battered suitcase. She lifted the case, weighing it in her hands. "He was a man of order," she said quietly. "He'd have noticed if something was out of place."

Harriet laughed, the sound brittle. "Order is a luxury the rest of us can't afford." She set the lamp back on the floor.

Adela closed her notebook with a snap. She knelt one last time. She pressed the carpet flat over the hinge, concealing the secret once more. "The evidence is here," she said. "Now we must match it to the only person who could have used it."

Outside the window, the night pressed in, the sounds of the circus drifting faint and melancholy across the grounds. Within the stillness of the room, the faint wear marks and the silent pivot beneath the carpet waited for their story to be told—one more secret in a house that had seen too many.

## Chapter 7: Suspect Interviews and Alibi Checks

The outcome of the investigation would determine not only justice for Cecil but the fragile coexistence between the manor and circus, central to her work and life. Ottoline Fairweather picked up a broom, sweeping the dust from under the bench.

The flickering oil lamp flames cast uncertain shadows, and the soft murmur of whispered conversations faded as Adela Halloway made her final notes. The rules of the Servants' Hall—limited to household staff, locked during formal hours—were enforced strictly, and the evidence was clear.

## Chapter 8: The Panel, the Passage, and the Proof

"Let's begin," Adela Halloway said, her hand resting atop the battered wooden case she had brought to Cecil Thorne's bedroom. The afternoon sun slanted through the tall sash windows, casting bands of pale light across the thick carpet, the carved stone walls, and the panel whose secrets had so long gone unnoticed. Close by, the precise seams and quiet geometry of the room waited, their silence ready to be tested. Harriet Bellamy, her foot tapping a nervous rhythm, stood beside the bed, while Ivor Yardley, solicitor, kept an immaculate distance near the window, his fingers tracing the edge of the curtain as if weighing the cost of each word he did not say.

"Why here, now?" Harriet asked, her voice edged with tension she did not attempt to hide. She leaned on her good leg, the slight limp from her old injury betraying itself only in moments of distraction.

"Because the proof is here, Miss Bellamy," Adela replied, setting down her notebook on the desk. She gestured to the wall panel, the carpet edge already drawn back to expose the faint seam. "The time has come to show how the killer moved unseen, and who among us alone could have done so."

Harriet's gaze darted from Adela to the panel, suspicion and hope warring in her eyes. "And if you're wrong?"

"If I'm wrong, the evidence will show it," Adela replied. She produced a folding ruler, snapping it out to measure the exposed base of the wall. "But I do not think I am." She pressed the ruler flat, reading off the precise fourteen inches, and made a note.

Ivor Yardley, his voice smooth, interrupted. "Surely, Miss Halloway, this is a performance for benefit only. The house is full of hidden quirks, and the panel is merely one of them." He adjusted his cuff.

"Not so," Adela answered, glancing up. "This panel is unique. Its edges align imperfectly with the wall, and a faint seam is visible beneath the carpet where the panel pivots. The geometry matches exactly the three feet width of the servant corridor behind it, and the wear marks on the carpet align perfectly with the pivot point of the rotating wall panel." She crouched and lifted the panel with a firm, practiced motion. The stone turned with a muted groan, swinging open at ninety degrees, revealing the darkness beyond.

Harriet drew back, a visible shiver running through her. "You mean someone could come and go, unseen, while the room was locked?"

Adela nodded. "Yes. The physical model and reenactment show the panel pivots under the carpet, opening a hidden passage. It is not a trick of light or age, but a deliberate device—used recently, as the wear proves."

Ivor's lips thinned, but he said nothing. His hand dropped from the curtain, fingers flexing at his side.

Adela gestured to the opening. "Adela's tone sharpened as she looked from Harriet to Ivor. "We will test who can operate the panel. Miss Bellamy, if you would attempt it first?" She stepped aside, letting the tension build as the others watched." She stepped aside, offering Harriet the place.

"I can't," she said. She brushed dust from her skirt, glancing away.

"Thank you," Adela said quietly, helping Harriet to her feet. She turned to Ivor Yardley. "Mr. Yardley, your turn."

"If you insist, Miss." He crouched without hesitation and moved unerringly to the concealed latch. With a single, practiced motion, he pressed, twisted, and the panel swung open again, smooth as a well-oiled hinge.

Adela's voice was cool and certain as she recorded the result. "Documents and testimonies confirm Ivor Yardley's exclusive familiarity with the panel's construction and operation. Access logs and skill assessments exclude all suspects except Ivor Yardley from operating the panel. Ivor's voice was flat, stripped of all pretense. "Formally noted, Miss Halloway." He rose, dusting his knees.

A hush fell. Harriet's voice, when it came, was barely above a whisper, but clear: "He was going to cut you off, wasn't he? From the estate, from Marguerite's accounts, from everything."

Adela's voice was cold. "And the cost would have been your legal reputation—your position, your place at Halloway Manor."

Ivor's gaze did not waver. "My standing in the county," he said simply.

Adela turned to Harriet and spoke for all to hear. ""Ivor Yardley entered Cecil Thorne's room through the rotating wall panel, moving by the servant corridor and leaving no trace behind." The opportunity lay in the window a quarter past nine to a quarter to ten—the very span the clock was meant to disguise."
