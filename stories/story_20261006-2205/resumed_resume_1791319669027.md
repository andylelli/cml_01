Her tone was arch, but her gaze slid toward the empty chair at the solicitor's usual place, unwilling to rest there.

The fire was unlit; the afternoon's chill had crept into the drawing room, and the only warmth came from the teapot Ottoline set with trembling hands at the centre of the table. Ottoline's movements were careful, her thumb tracing the rim of each cup before setting it down—an old habit, never remarked, born of childhood illness and the will to reassure herself of the world's solidity.

Adela Halloway, standing near the sideboard, recorded names in her notebook with her usual precision. "Harriet Bellamy—cleared. Marguerite Selwyn—cleared. Ottoline Fairweather—cleared." Her voice was steady, the verdict unornamented, and she closed the book with a snap that marked the end of suspicion as surely as the end of a chapter.

Harriet, hands finally still, flicked a glance at Adela. "And what does cleared mean, in practice? Will I find my name in the next village scandal sheet, or shall I content myself with the company's good opinion?" She let the question hang, one eyebrow arched, the performance just enough to draw a smile from Adela.

"Your name remains your own," Adela replied. She set the notebook aside, her fingertips brushing the spine, the gesture as final as her words.

Ottoline, pouring tea, looked up. "Is it really over?" Her voice did not quite steady; she held the pot with both hands, knuckles pale around the handle.

"Yes, Ottoline," Marguerite answered, her tone stretched thin but unbroken. "It is over for us." She reached for her cup, her hand resting atop the saucer, the porcelain cool against her skin as she let the reassurance settle.

Harriet leaned forward, arms crossed atop the table's edge. "You never said what you planned, Marguerite—if you had been found at fault. Would you have left the circus behind?" Her voice was casual, but her eyes held a flicker of genuine curiosity.

Marguerite's reply was quiet, four words only: "I would have vanished." Ottoline, hearing the certainty in those words, looked away, her attention drawn to the pattern of light on the wall.

"Some do not have that luxury, Marguerite," Ottoline said, her own voice a blend of apology and longing. She reached for a biscuit with the awkwardness of someone unused to taking before others, her hands betraying their weakness only slightly as the treat crumbled between her fingertips.

Harriet, not missing the tremor, watched Ottoline for a moment before speaking again, her words a little softer than usual. "You always wanted to care for the horses, Ottoline, and you do it better than any of us—whether or not you have the confidence to call yourself their master. If it matters, I never forgot the night you stayed up with the foal when the rest of us had lost hope, or the way you kept the fires burning when the frost took the pipes and half the troupe was ill. I doubt I ever said thank you, and it's probably too late to make it count, but—thank you all the same."

Ottoline's reply was gentle, gratitude and awkwardness twined together: "I tried my best." She set the biscuit down, her hands folded in her lap.

Marguerite turned, her gaze on Harriet, and spoke in a low tone that carried more weight than her words. "You always say less than you mean, Harriet." She let the moment linger, then reached for the sugar bowl, spooning two lumps into her tea with deliberate care.

Harriet gave a single, sardonic laugh. "I say what I can afford, Marguerite, and never a word more than required—at least, not since the accident." She tapped her knee, the old injury obvious in the way she shifted her seat.

Adela, eyes fixed on the window, broke the silence. "If the panel's findings have settled matters, so too are the accusations. Each of you is free to return to the life you made before the panel was found and the letter opener taken." She watched as a cloud drifted across the sun, the shifting light rendering the room strange and familiar in equal measure.

Marguerite's reply was as much a sigh as a statement. "Freedom is not always as simple as it sounds." She lifted her teacup, the thin porcelain ringing against the saucer as she set it down.

Harriet's gaze lingered on the empty chair, voice softer than before. "There will be paperwork—there always is—but the stories that matter will stay with us." She traced a circle on the tabletop with her forefinger, the act absentminded and oddly tender.

Ottoline, her cup now empty, began gathering plates, her hands efficient if not always steady. She carried the tray to the sideboard, pausing to adjust the stack so nothing tipped or rattled, her composure restored by the routine of service.

Marguerite, watching her, said, "You manage well enough without an audience, Ottoline. Perhaps that's the real secret to surviving all this."

Ottoline's answer was simple, unadorned: "I do what I can." She poured the dregs of the tea into the sink, rinsing the pot as quietly as possible.

Harriet's laughter rose, as brittle as the fading daylight. "It's a pity Yardley could never master the art of simplicity. Perhaps he'd still have his place if he'd remembered the value of a plain answer." Her eyes glinted, the edge of the joke never far away.

Adela straightened the chairs around the table, her hands smoothing the last of the creased linen—a gesture as much for her own reassurance as for the room's. "The legalities are settled. What remains is repair."

Ottoline, her tray now empty, moved to the window to close the shutters. She paused, eyes wandering to the lawns beyond, and for a moment her hands were still, the tremor quieted by the comfort of a task completed.

Marguerite, notebook in hand, flicked through the blank pages, considering the future as one might an empty ledger—filled only by what she chose to write next.

Harriet folded her scarf and slipped it into her bag, an echo of the performer's final act, and looked to Adela with a half-smile. "And what of you, Miss Halloway? Case closed, name cleared, scandal averted. Will you take up your next puzzle, or linger here in the ruins of ours?"

Adela's lips curved, her voice dry as always. "Curious, isn't that. The work, in the end, is never quite done." She tucked her notebook into her case, the click of the clasp the last sound needed to mark the close of one story, and the beginning of everything that must—by necessity—follow.

## Chapter 10: The Weight That Settles

"There's no escaping the laundry, you know." Ottoline Fairweather's voice emerged from the hall as she bent to gather a scattered bundle of sheets—work paused during the ordeal, now resumed out of habit and necessity. Ottoline moved quietly past the windows, her arms full of linens, while other staff members shuffled through the corridor, their hands working steadily as they brought life back into the emptied spaces of the manor.

Adela Halloway stood by the victim's bedroom door with her case propped atop Cecil Thorne's trunk, sorting through her own notes, the pressure of the closed case settling in her shoulders. She was methodical, method unchanged, each paper smoothed and replaced, each action suffused with a return to routine. The faint scent of starch and wax polish drifted in from the open laundry door, blending with the colder air of the unheated corridor.

"Will you remain long?" Marguerite Selwyn's voice came from the landing, her hand resting on the banister, the other lifting a folded letter—one she had delayed sending since the day of Cecil's death. She wore her composure as a shield, but her eyes lingered on the threshold as if uncertain whether to step forward or retreat. Adela met Marguerite's glance with a level reply, choosing her words with care, her tone not unkind.

"Not if I can avoid it." Adela returned a file to her case, her glance passing over the furniture with a silent inventory, the habits of inquiry yielding slowly to the habits of the house.

Harriet Bellamy, appearing at the end of the corridor with her limp more pronounced as fatigue overtook the evening's composure, carried a battered theatre script beneath one arm. She hesitated at the threshold, absently rubbing her ankle before moving on with determined steps. She flicked through the pages with one hand, stopping occasionally to smooth a corner. "Someone should write this up for the company newsletter," she said, her tone a blend of mockery and relief. "I'll even let Ottoline have the final joke, so long as she spares the rest of us the ending." Her gaze moved to the empty chair by Cecil's desk, the customary stack of reports now shifted out of sight.

Ottoline knelt to collect scattered buttons from the carpet, fingers trembling only slightly as she worked. "One day they'll say the circus put the law to shame, and the laundry waited patiently for its turn," she declared with a twisted smile, her tone half-defiance, half self-mockery. Adela, sorting the keys into a neat row, did not look up as she answered: "It never ends, Ottoline." The hallway, dimly lit, pressed around them all, ordinary enough to swallow secrets and the remains of scandal alike.

Marguerite stepped into the bedroom, her gloved hands tracing the edge of Cecil's writing chair—a thing she had avoided touching for days. She found the faint groove his ring had left on the wood, memory sharper than the chill that lingered. "Did you ever imagine you'd see the day when silence felt heavier than accusation?" she asked, her fingers lingering on the chair's worn arm.

"No," Adela answered, her focus on the ledger open at her side. She returned to copying the names of house staff into the margins, a final record before she would close the file for good.

The clock on the mantel, its hands reset to the correct hour at last, ticked its way through the deepening night, the steady rhythm a comfort in a house still learning to breathe again. As Adela listened, she let her hand rest atop the case for a moment, the weight of her own early failure—never spoken, never excused—tucked just beneath the surface, always present when the work slowed and memory threatened to fill the gaps.

"Are you sure you don't mind?" Ottoline's question broke the quiet as she held up a stained cuff, saved from the pile for mending. Ottoline's question was more than it seemed—she sought permission to resume, to repair, to return to what had been interrupted.

"Not at all," Adela replied, her voice carrying its usual steadiness. She watched as Ottoline moved to the window, where the moonlight made broken patterns across the glass, and began sewing with the quiet competence of one who had spent a lifetime making things serviceable again.

Harriet, leaning against the doorframe, let the script fall open in her lap. "Suppose we're meant to pretend none of this changed us," she mused, the words skating the edge of bitterness and relief. "Do we all go back to our roles as if the curtain never fell?" She closed her eyes, her thumb running along the worn leather spine.

Ottoline glanced up from her mending, a thread caught between her teeth. "We go back, but not the same," she said, her words half-muffled but clear enough. She tied off a knot, her concentration a shield against the uncertainty that flickered at the edges of the night.

Marguerite, setting the unsent letter on the desk, regarded the objects left behind—an inkpot, a brass stamp, the battered clock—each a remnant of Cecil Thorne's ordinary routines. She picked up the clock, weighing its cool mass in her hand, and for a moment the echo of his terse command—let's keep to the point—hung in the air. She set the clock gently down, her posture softening as she released it.

From the far end of the corridor came the sound of a door unlocked, hinges creaking as a staff member—unseen—resumed the rounds that had been delayed by crisis and inquiry. The rhythm of the house reasserted itself, cautious but persistent.

Adela, closing her case with its characteristic snap, turned to Marguerite. "The letter—will you send it?" Ottoline's question was matter-of-fact, but as she held up the cuff her gaze did not waver, and Marguerite, hands steady, met it with a nod.

"I will," Marguerite replied, slipping the folded sheet into her bag. She moved to the hallway, her footsteps as measured as her reply, leaving the room lighter for her absence.

In the hush that followed, Ottoline finished her mending and tucked the cuff into her apron pocket. "If you ever need a hand with impossible seams, Miss Halloway, I'm your woman," she said with a shy smile, the offer genuine beneath the jest. Adela's answer came, brief and dry: "I'll remember that."

The silence became companionable, broken only by the routine: Harriet shelving her script, Ottoline folding linen, Marguerite writing out a name on an envelope. The manifold tasks of the house resumed, each touching what Cecil Thorne had once governed with such certainty. The lamp's light cast long shadows along the wall, and the ordinary world pressed in, insistent and unstoppable.

In the final moments before midnight, Adela crossed to the desk, her hand pausing on Cecil's pen—unused, slightly ink-stained, the cap clicked shut with habitual neatness. She remembered his brusque instruction, the way he would gesture with the pen before setting it decisively atop a list of tasks. For a moment, she let memory fill the space, then set the pen back where it belonged.

Ottoline, arms full of linen, made her way down the corridor, her gait slow but steady, pausing only to glance through a half-open door at the sleeping animals in the yard. The routine—feeding, mending, writing, watching—reasserted itself with the quiet force of habit, and the house, for all it had lost, found its way again to what must come next.
