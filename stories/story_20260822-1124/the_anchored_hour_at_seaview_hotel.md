To question it now would mean questioning not only Beatrice's account but the memory the whole party had agreed to share. Her attention drifted to the side table, where the detective had laid out the battered candlestick beside Dr. Mallory Finch's wristwatch, its hands stopped at exactly nine o'clock. The watch sat near a stack of letters and a folded note, unremarked for now but present all the same. Eleanor looked from the watch to the clock and back, and something in the gap between the two hours would not settle. The others, meanwhile, seemed content to let the matter rest, their relief showing in eased shoulders and softened voices.

Sylvia, emboldened by the apparent resolution, offered a thin smile. 'We all want this to be over, Mrs. Voss. There's nothing to be gained by doubting every log and every clock. ' Hugo Vane, still turning his cap over in his hands, nodded, though his eyes kept flicking from Beatrice to the table and back. Captain Hale sank deeper into the armchair, the last of the tension going out of him as the group's agreement hardened into something like fact. Eleanor felt herself close to giving way, the pull to accept the tidy answer stronger than she liked. Yet something held her back-the memory of Beatrice's trembling hand, the answers offered a beat too quickly, the access record laid out as though to head off a question rather than answer one.

She cleared her throat and spoke, her voice steady but quieter than usual. 'It's tempting to believe in a tidy solution, especially when the evidence seems so obliging. But perhaps we ought to remember that even the most reliable clock can be made to lie. ' Beatrice's eyes flashed, and for a heartbeat the composure went entirely. 'If you're not satisfied, Mrs. Voss, you're welcome to keep digging. Just be certain you're not looking for guilt where there is only routine. ' The words came out sharp, but the polish returned almost at once, and she offered a brittle smile. 'After all, what would the guests think if the manager were accused of tampering with time itself? ' A thin laugh went round the group, the tension breaking for a moment on the absurdity of the idea.

Beneath it, though, Eleanor heard something else in Beatrice's words-not just the fear of being caught out, but the fear of what would follow after. The hotel's name, Beatrice's place in it, the whole fragile order of the evening: all of it leaned on the clock being believed. The fire cracked and threw a shower of sparks up the chimney, and the wind outside rattled the panes harder than before. Eleanor allowed herself a wry smile and let her tone soften as she addressed the room. 'One mustn't assume that the first solution is always the right one. Sometimes the truth takes a little longer to show itself. ' A murmur of assent went round, but it was relief, not agreement, that Eleanor heard in it. For now the matter seemed settled, and the room's will to believe in Beatrice's innocence outweighed whatever doubt lingered at its edges.

As the group began to disperse, Eleanor stayed by the mantel, her eyes drawn once more to the stopped clock and the silent watch on the table beside it. The false solution had taken root, fed by the very evidence that should have starved it. She felt the isolation of her position keenly-to press further would mean breaking more than an alibi. Yet as she watched Beatrice gather her papers, one thought would not leave her: whatever the room had agreed to believe, the truth was not finished with them yet.

## Chapter 7: Secrets and Lies

Eleanor Voss set her notes aside, the lamplight glinting off their battered corners as she pressed her palm to the smooth wood of the Seaview Hotel private office desk. The hour was late; the darkness outside pressed against the frosted glass with the weight of midwinter, and the wind's low moan found every crack in the old frame. The air in the lounge was thick with the scent of spent tobacco and the faint, acrid tang of burnt coal from the distant boiler. Somewhere in the lounge, a clock ticked-one of the small ones, not the great liar above the mantel. Eleanor's eyes drifted to the mantelpiece, where the stopped clock and Dr. Mallory Finch's silent wristwatch still sat, twin sentinels over a truth no one wished to name. She lingered by the mantel, the hush of the night settling around her like a second coat.

The group had begun to disperse, but Beatrice Quill remained, her posture rigid in the fire's glow. Captain Ivor Hale and Sylvia Trent hovered nearby, their conversation little more than a murmur, while Hugo Vane busied himself with the poker, sending up a brief shower of sparks that flickered in the dimness. The lounge's velvet upholstery caught the firelight, throwing long shadows across the walls, and the silence was broken only by the occasional creak of old floorboards. Eleanor's gaze lingered on Beatrice, noting the way her fingers trembled as she gathered her papers-more than mere nerves, Eleanor thought, something closer to dread. Beatrice's composure fractured when Eleanor quietly asked, 'May I see the correspondence you tucked away earlier? ' For a moment, Beatrice's eyes flashed with something raw-resentment, perhaps, or fear.

She hesitated, then slid a folded letter across the desk, her knuckles white. The paper was creased, the ink bold and unfamiliar. Eleanor unfolded it, aware of the others' attention sharpening, and read in silence. The words were unmistakable: veiled threats, demands for money, and a signature that belonged, unmistakably, to Dr. Mallory Finch. 'You're not the only one with secrets, Mrs. Quill,' Eleanor murmured, her voice low enough for only Beatrice to hear. The revelation shifted the air in the room. Captain Ivor Hale straightened, suspicion flickering across his face, while Sylvia Trent leaned forward, her notebook forgotten. Beatrice's lips parted, but for a moment no words came. When she finally spoke, her tone was brittle with exhaustion. 'She-Dr. Finch-knew things about the hotel's finances. About me.

She threatened to ruin everything unless I paid her. ' The admission hung in the air, heavy as the smoke curling from the fire. Eleanor watched Beatrice's hands, the way they twisted the hem of her sleeve, and realized that the nervousness she had read as fear of accusation was, in truth, the terror of exposure. Sylvia's voice was sharp, but not unkind. 'That's motive, not murder. ' Her eyes darted to the clock, then back to Beatrice. 'But it does explain why you'd want to control what everyone remembers about tonight. ' The implication was clear, but the accusation stopped just short of naming the crime. Hugo Vane, silent until now, let out a shaky breath. 'If you were being blackmailed, why not go to the police? ' he asked, his voice barely above a whisper. Beatrice's reply was a soft, bitter laugh. 'And destroy the hotel? Lose everything I've worked for? No.

I thought I could manage it-manage her. ' Eleanor turned the letter over in her hands, the paper rough beneath her fingers. She recalled the maintenance records she had examined earlier, Beatrice's signature granting her access to the clock's mechanism during her scheduled break. The old meaning-routine, authority, a manager's duty-fell away in the light of this new knowledge. Now it was evidence of opportunity, of skill, of someone with both the motive and the means to alter the timeline the hotel's fragile order depended upon. The logs and permissions, once mere formalities, became the scaffolding of a carefully constructed lie.

A hush settled as Eleanor laid the letter atop the stack of records. 'We've all been too eager to believe in the safety of routine,' she said, her voice steady, 'and too willing to let the clock tell us what to remember. ' The others shifted, unease rippling through the group. Captain Ivor Hale's jaw tightened, Sylvia's pen hovered uncertainly over her notebook, and Hugo Vane stared at his shoes. Beatrice, for her part, sat very still, her eyes fixed on the fire. For a moment, the only sound was the wind pressing against the glass, and the faint, persistent tick of a clock somewhere deep in the hotel-a reminder that time, once unmoored, could not be so easily anchored again.

Yet, in the midst of this tension, Eleanor allowed herself a brief, dry smile. 'Well,' she said, glancing at the battered candlestick and the stack of ledgers, 'at least we can be certain of one thing: the Seaview Hotel will never be accused of running on time. ' The remark drew a reluctant chuckle from Captain Ivor Hale and even a faint, rueful smile from Beatrice. It was a moment's relief-a signal, perhaps, that the truth, however dark, could be faced together. But as the laughter faded, the reality of what had been uncovered settled back over them, heavier than before. Eleanor's mind raced with the implications. The correspondence shifted her understanding of Beatrice's actions: what had seemed like a nervous adherence to routine was, in truth, a desperate attempt to maintain control over a narrative that threatened to unravel.

The permissions and logs, the maintenance access, the very schedule Beatrice had clung to-all now pointed not to innocence, but to a calculated effort to conceal guilt. The clues, once scattered and ambiguous, began to arrange themselves into a new, more troubling pattern. As the fire burned low and the night pressed in, Eleanor watched Beatrice gather her things, her movements slower, the mask of composure worn thin. The others lingered, uncertain, the boundaries between guilt and survival, between moral failing and criminal act, blurring in the dim glow of the lounge. Eleanor felt the weight of betrayal and ambition in the air, and knew that the next step would demand not only proof, but courage-the courage to name what everyone now suspected, and to accept the cost of seeing clearly at last.

## Chapter 8: The Discriminating Test

"Please, everyone, if you would gather round," Eleanor Voss said, her voice carrying through the Seaview Hotel Lobby, where gaslight flickered over polished oak and threw uneasy shadows against the fogged windows. Candle wax mingled with pipe tobacco in the air, and the ticking of the smaller mantel clocks seemed almost impertinent against the hush that had settled since Beatrice Quill's composure began to crack. The fire burned low, its restless light crossing the faces of Captain Ivor Hale, Sylvia Trent, Hugo Vane, and Beatrice herself, who stood stiff-backed near the reception desk, the ledger held against her chest like something that might still protect her. Outside, the fog pressed thick against the glass, muffling the distant crash of waves. The lobby, usually a stage for polite ritual, felt tonight like a cage-every movement watched, every breath counted.

Eleanor's gaze swept the group, her mind still turning over the letter she had read moments earlier: Dr. Mallory Finch's blackmail demands, the threat that had pushed Beatrice past whatever line she'd meant to hold. The others watched her, expectant and wary, as she crossed to the mantel where the lobby clock stood, its hands frozen at twenty minutes to eight at night. It was half past eight at night by the mantel clock. Eleanor set Dr. Mallory Finch's gold wristwatch beside it, glass still smeared faintly red, hands stopped at nine o'clock at night. Beside both she laid the candlestick, its base dulled with blood and brass. The fire cracked somewhere behind her; from the dining room came the murmur of voices that did not yet know what was happening here. "We have all relied on these clocks," Eleanor said.

"But tonight, time itself was made an accomplice. " She looked at Beatrice, who flinched but did not look away. "Every witness here remembers the murder happening at half past eight. And yet the lobby clock, the one we all glanced at without thinking, had already stopped-its hands aligned as if to fix that moment in all our minds. Dr. Finch's wristwatch says otherwise. Nine o'clock. The true hour. " She let the two faces sit side by side a moment longer than was comfortable. "If the clock was stopped before the crime, then whatever alibi rests on it rests on nothing. " She turned to Captain Ivor Hale. "The bar log has your name until nearly nine, and the bartender signed beside it. You couldn't have slipped away unnoticed, and you couldn't have reached the clock. " Hale's jaw tightened, but he held his gaze. "I did as the log shows," he said.

"No more, no less. " Next she turned to Sylvia Trent. "The dining room registry has you from half past eight onward, and more than one member of staff will say the same. " Sylvia's answer came quick, almost brisk, though relief moved under it. "Let's not linger on it, then. If I'm cleared, who does that leave? " Eleanor's gaze moved to Hugo Vane, who stood near the billiard room door turning his cap over in his hands. "Your signature is in the billiard room log at eight twenty-five, Mr. Vane, and there are guests who'll swear you were still there when the commotion started. " Hugo's voice came out barely above a whisper. "I never left that room. Not till it was all over. " Eleanor returned to the mantel and rested her hand between the clock and the watch. "There is only one person who had both the means and the chance to reach the clock's mechanism in that window.

The maintenance log, the access record, the staff schedule-all of it points the same way. Only Beatrice Quill could have opened that case and set the hands to twenty minutes to eight. The rest of us built our memory of the evening around a lie. " She studied Beatrice, whose knuckles had gone white on the ledger. "You stopped the clock, and then you struck Dr. Finch with that candlestick at nine. The watch on her wrist kept the truth you couldn't touch. " Beatrice's composure gave way at last. Her voice, when it came, was thin, none of its usual edge left in it. "She was going to destroy everything-my name, the hotel, all of it. I thought if I could buy myself time, I could fix things, that she'd relent. She laughed at me instead. " Her shoulders shook; the ledger slid from her arms and struck the floor. "I struck her.

I didn't mean-I only wanted her to stop. " Eleanor's tone gentled, though she did not look away. "You moved the clock's hands to fix our memories to a false hour. But the watch kept its own time, and it would not lie for you. " No one spoke. Captain Hale's face gave nothing away, but his hands unclenched at his sides. Sylvia Trent closed her notebook without ceremony, her expression somewhere between pity and grim relief. Hugo Vane looked at the floor, his own relief tangled with something like sorrow. Beatrice stood apart from all of them, the weight of it settling visibly onto her shoulders. Eleanor set the watch, the clock, and the candlestick in a line upon the mantel. "She was struck with this," she said quietly, "by someone frightened enough to risk everything rather than be exposed. The clock was a clever piece of work.

It only failed against the hour the watch had already fixed. " Beatrice's eyes filled, though she did not plead. "She left me no choice," she said, voice breaking. "I thought I could manage it. I was wrong. I only wanted to save what little I had left. " It was not an excuse, only an admission-exhaustion more than malice. Eleanor offered no comfort beyond the truth itself. "The cost of a lie is always steeper than we think when we tell it. You are not the only one paying for this tonight. " She turned to the others, her voice quiet now, worn thin with the evening. "Let us remember what's been lost, and carry what we must. " The lobby clocks went on ticking, indifferent, while outside the fog thickened against the glass-the illusion of a fixed hour gone, and only the plain, cold fact of the true one left standing in its place.

## Chapter 9: Suspect Clearances

By the time the fire in the Seaview Hotel lounge had burned down to a soft, flickering glow, the fog outside pressed thick as wool against the windows, muting the distant crash of waves and swallowing the last hints of evening. The air was heavy with the scent of damp wool and spent tobacco, and the hush that followed Beatrice Quill's confession seemed to deepen with every passing minute. Eleanor Voss, standing at the edge of the circle of chairs, felt the weight of the room's exhaustion-relief edged with something rawer, more uncertain. The clocks along the mantel ticked on, indifferent, while the grand lobby clock, still frozen at twenty minutes to eight at night, loomed over the aftermath. Eleanor's hand lingered on the stack of logs and correspondence she had assembled atop the side table.

The bartender's testimony, and the staff's, confirm you never left their sight. ' She paused, letting the truth settle. 'You could not have reached the lobby clock, nor left the main rooms unnoticed. Eleanor gathered the logs-bar, dining room, billiard room-and placed them in a careful row atop the mantel, each one a testament to the rituals that had, in the end, preserved more than they betrayed. ## Chapter 10: The Culprit Revealed
