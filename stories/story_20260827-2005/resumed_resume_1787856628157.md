## Chapter 7: A Late-Breaking Complication

"Let's try it again, Captain," Eleanor Voss said, her voice measured and calm, slicing through the hush that had settled over the Hotel Lobby. The faint glow of lamps reflected off rain-streaked glass, and the cold, damp air pressed in from the night beyond, making the marble floor slick beneath her sensible shoes. The hourglass, centerpiece of so much recent tension, sat on the teak side table, its brass frame glinting in the dim light. The silence was punctuated only by the steady tap of rain and the distant echo of a radio from the lounge, now little more than a whisper. Eleanor's fingers, cool and steady, hovered above the hourglass. The lobby's somber elegance-chrome accents, polished wood, the lingering odor of wax-felt at odds with the anxiety that prickled beneath her skin.

She could sense Captain Ivor Hale's presence behind her, the rigid set of his shoulders and the faint scuff of his shoes on the damp terrazzo. "If you insist, Miss Voss. But I fail to see what this will accomplish. " He moved closer, the lamplight catching the silver at his temples, his gaze fixed not on Eleanor but on the hourglass itself. "We are, after all, creatures of habit. Let's see if the sand agrees with your recollection. " She turned the hourglass over, watching as the first grains began to fall.

You claim you flipped the glass at a quarter to eleven, yes? " Captain Hale's jaw tightened. "That is correct. I have said so repeatedly. " His tone was flat, but his eyes flickered-just for a moment-toward the rain-lashed windows, as if considering escape. Eleanor noted the hesitation, the subtle shift in his stance. The demonstration had unsettled him more than he wished to show. They waited in silence, the only sounds the soft patter of rain and the occasional creak from the old hotel's bones. As the sand trickled, Eleanor's thoughts churned. The contradiction had gnawed at her for hours: the hourglass, meant to measure twenty minutes, had been half full at ten minutes past eleven.

Yet Captain Hale's claim-flipping it at a quarter to eleven-could not be reconciled with the physical evidence. She watched the grains fall, each one a silent rebuke to the official story. When the sand reached the halfway mark, Eleanor stopped the clock. She pointed, her voice low but clear. "There. Half full-exactly ten minutes by the clock. If the glass had been flipped at a quarter to eleven, as you insist, it would have been nearly empty by the time Dr finch stepped out of sight near ten minutes past eleven. " She let the implication hang, heavy as the damp air. "Yet the sand was half full. The evidence disagrees with your account. " Captain Hale's lips pressed together, a muscle jumping in his cheek. He did not answer at once. Instead, he moved to the hourglass, studying it as though it might yield a different truth under his scrutiny.

The lobby's silence deepened, broken only by the faint hum of the black-and-white television in the lounge. "Perhaps the glass is faulty," he muttered, but even he seemed to find the excuse thin. Eleanor's gaze sharpened. "You're the only one with the keys to the maintenance cupboard, the only one who signs the logs, the only one who handles the hourglass. No one else has the knowledge or the opportunity to alter its ritual. The staff confirm it, the guests confirm it. This is your domain, Captain. " She watched him, searching for a crack in his composure. A flicker of something-fear, perhaps, or guilt-passed across Captain Hale's face. He straightened, drawing himself up to his full height, but the effect was undermined by the tremor in his left hand. "You're making much of a trifle, Miss Voss.

There are more important matters than sand and brass. " His attempt at deflection rang hollow in the vast, dim lobby. Eleanor felt a surge of determination, tempered by the weight of her own doubts. She had been a confidante to Dr. Finch's family, had kept their secrets out of loyalty. Now, justice demanded a reckoning, and the cost was becoming clear. She pressed on, her tone gentle but unyielding. "Sometimes the smallest things are the most telling, Captain. A misplaced minute, a half-measure of sand. They reveal more than grand gestures ever could. " The demonstration had left the lobby colder, the air thick with anticipation. Captain Hale's unease was now plain to anyone who cared to look. He lingered by the hourglass, his eyes fixed on the slow descent of sand, as if willing it to erase the contradiction. But the evidence remained, stubborn and silent.

Eleanor sensed that the final trap was near, the last barrier between suspicion and truth about to fall. For a moment, relief flickered in the gloom-a brief, ironic counterpoint to the tension. From the far end of the lobby, Beatrice Quill's voice drifted in, sharp and wry: "If the sand could talk, Miss Voss, I daresay it would have its own list of complaints. Captain Hale's alibi had crumbled, his authority undermined by the very ritual he had claimed as his own.

## Chapter 8: The Discriminating Test

Late that night, as the rain battered the windows in relentless sheets, Eleanor Voss stood before the assembled group in the Hotel Lobby with the hourglass poised in her steady hands. The flat, diffuse light from the overhead lamps cast the marble floor in a shadowless pallor, and the hum of soft conversation faded to silence. Dampness clung to the air, mingling with the faint, waxy scent of polished wood. The lobby, usually a place of quiet refuge, now felt charged with anticipation. Captain Ivor Hale's rigid posture by the reception desk drew every eye, but it was the hourglass-its brass frame glinting dully-that held the room's collective breath. Eleanor's resolve, hardened by the earlier demonstration, was visible in the set of her jaw and the measured cadence of her voice. She began without preamble, her tone precise. Eleanor gestured to the wall clock, its hands marking the passage of time with impartial certainty. "Captain Hale has insisted, again and again, that he flipped the hourglass at 'a quarter to eleven. ' Yet when Dr finch was glimpsed departing from 'ten minutes past eleven,' the hourglass was still half full. The only sounds were the soft turning of pages from Hugo's notebook and the distant patter of rain. At the ten-minute mark, Eleanor stilled the hourglass. The sand in the upper bulb was, unmistakably, "half full. " She turned to the group. "This is what the hourglass shows after ten minutes.

Yet, by Captain Hale's account, it should have been nearly empty at ten minutes past eleven if he had flipped it at a quarter to eleven. The evidence disagrees with his story.
