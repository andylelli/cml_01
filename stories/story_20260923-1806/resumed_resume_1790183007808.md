Gwendolyn's lips quirked. "He'd sooner accuse the sun of shining too brightly."

Harriet moved to the window, her fingers tracing a pattern on the glass. "They see only half the story," she murmured, her breath clouding the pane.

Norbury crossed the room, retrieving a small notebook from his pocket. He opened it, revealing a neat column of times-eight forty-five, nine fifteen, a quarter to ten-and underlined a name. "The ledger was altered in the same intervals as the supposed alibi," he said.

Nora's reply was a shrug. "Patterns are easy to find if you're looking for them."

Bertram Norbury looked at her, his gaze steady. "And easy to create, if you know how."

A log shifted in the grate, sending a shower of sparks up the chimney. For a moment, the only sound was the gentle roar of the fire.

Theodora leaned in, her voice low. "Did you ever suspect someone would use the judge's compass habit to their advantage?"

Gwendolyn answered, her tone all understatement. "Not until it became inconvenient."

Nora's dry wit surfaced. "Funny how inconvenience brings out the best in people."

Norbury's eyes narrowed. "You had the means, the access, and the motive. Gaunt threatened to expose you, and you alone could manipulate both the ledger and the compass testimony."

Nora's lips twisted. "Anything else you'd like to pin on me?"

Harriet's voice broke the silence, gentle but edged. "Only if you insist."

Norbury thumbed through his notebook, recalling a moment from his army days-how a single, overlooked detail had changed the outcome of an entire campaign. He studied Nora, then the ledger on the table.

For no reason at all, Gwendolyn plucked a withered sprig of lavender from a vase, rolling it between her fingers as she listened.

Theodora's voice was soft. "We can hope it doesn't come to that," she said, gathering her scarf as she stood.

Bertram Norbury closed his notebook, his face set. "Tomorrow, we test the compass. The truth is in the details."

Nora watched him, her face unreadable, as the fire burned lower and the room filled with the hush of secrets waiting to be revealed.

## Chapter 8: Compass and Ledger

"Let's run the test." Bertram Norbury's voice was even, his words carrying through the stillness of the late evening hotel office. Nora Quayle stood across the desk, arms folded, the battered compass set carefully between them.

The room was empty but for their voices and the faint tick of the clock-a clock whose hands had marked both the hour of death and the slow crawl of revelation since. Norbury turned the compass so its worn side faced north and called Nora's attention to the needle's drift.

"Fifteen degrees off," she said, not bothering to hide her impatience.

Norbury took the compass, holding it level. The needle swung and settled, this time pointing true. "Quarter to ten and nine o'clock-if the judge took his reading with the casing tilted, that's the offset in position."

Nora's reply was curt. "Obvious, once seen."

Harriet Kestrel entered quietly, her key jangling on a thin chain. She crossed to the desk, her hand brushing the edge of the ledger. "Staff saw me cleaning rooms from ten minutes to nine to a quarter past nine," she said. "That's my alibi-nothing to do with compasses or ledgers."

Bertram Norbury nodded. "Corroborated by two separate staff and the linen log. You were clear of the office the entire time."

Harriet's lips curved in a polite, savage smile. "If only they knew, all the things people do to avoid a little honest work."

Norbury watched Nora's hands, the knuckles white where she held the back of the chair. "The ledger entries-eight forty-five to a quarter to ten-were altered in ink and order. Only someone with exclusive access and knowledge of the routines could manage that without arousing suspicion."

Nora's answer was a single word: "Perhaps."

The wind rattled the windows, sending a spray of sand against the glass. For a moment, the only movement was the flicker of candlelight on the polished surface of the desk.

Norbury set the compass level again, gesturing for Nora to try. She complied, her hands steady, but the needle drifted as soon as she allowed the casing to tilt. Nora watched the needle drift, frowning as understanding dawned. Her grip tightened on the compass, and she glanced quickly at Norbury, the lines of suspicion deepening between them.

"Montague Gaunt is cleared, for obvious reasons," Norbury said, voice gentle. "The dead have no alibi to confirm, nor motive left to pursue."

Harriet glanced at the compass, then the ledger, her eyes lingering on the subtle variances-each a clue, now impossible to ignore. She folded her hands atop the desk, the gesture as precise as her work.

Bertram Norbury outlined the sequence: the false alibi constructed on a faulty compass reading, ledger entries manipulated to fit the fabricated timing, all orchestrated by someone with both technical expertise and an urgent motive.

Nora watched him, her face unreadable, the silence between them weighted by everything that had gone unsaid.

For no reason at all, Harriet straightened a crooked photograph on the wall, her fingers lingering on the frame as she listened.

Norbury's eyes narrowed. "It was never the judge's error alone-it was the exploitation of his habit, by someone who knew exactly how the trick worked."

Nora did not move, her gaze fixed on the compass as though it might still offer her a way out.

"Let's start with what we know," Norbury said, his words final as the click of the closing ledger. "The time, the method, the motive-all lead to one place."

The test was complete. The result was undeniable. The silence lingered, the implications settling on the room, with only the final accusation yet to be spoken.

## Chapter 9: The Shape of the Truth

The lounge was hushed, the lamps casting long shadows across the polished floor as Bertram Norbury faced the assembled group: Gwendolyn Vance, Harriet Kestrel, and Theodora Lachlan. The surf beyond the windows was a persistent murmur, a reminder that the world outside still turned, indifferent to the secrets uncovered within.

Theodora Lachlan stood at the window, her silhouette framed by the moonlit dunes. "Is it over?" she asked, her voice soft.

"No," Harriet replied, her hands folded on the back of a chair. The tension in the room was palpable, each person caught in the final moment before the truth emerged.

Bertram Norbury placed the compass and the open ledger on the table, symbols now of a plan laid bare. He spoke with measured authority. "Nora Quayle killed Montague Gaunt," he said, his voice carrying to every corner of the room.

Nora's lips parted. For a moment, she said nothing. Then, in a voice stripped of bravado, she answered: "Torque says otherwise, but I can't argue the proof."

A taut, electric silence followed, the revelation sparking a current of fear and disbelief through the room. Outside, the tide crashed louder, as if echoing the shock within.

Theodora turned from the window, her hand lingering on the sill. "What happens now?" she asked, her words barely above a whisper.

"That's for the authorities," Norbury replied. He closed the ledger with deliberate care.

Gwendolyn Vance's voice was a brittle echo. She nodded, her eyes shining in the lamplight. "So it ends."

Harriet Kestrel's gaze lingered on Nora, her voice gentle but edged with a savage finality. She gathered her apron, her voice low. "Some things will never be spoken," she murmured, stepping into the corridor, the sound of her footsteps fading into the hush.

For a moment, no one moved. The room held its breath, suspended between the ending and whatever would come next.

Theodora's hand tightened on the window latch, her silhouette steady against the shifting light. She drew a slow breath. "No one will sleep easily tonight," she said, and the night pressed on.

## Chapter 10: Dunes Resuming Their Silence

"Tea, Mr. Norbury?" The kettle whistled as Harriet Kestrel set the tray on the polished office desk, the clink of china a quiet signal that life, in some measure, had resumed. The proof still rested in the closed ledger beside the battered compass, but no one reached for either.

Bertram Norbury accepted the cup, his hand steady as he stirred in a spoonful of sugar. He glanced at the window, where the moonlight silvered the dunes and the sea beyond, then turned to the pile of correspondence he'd left unfinished the night before. He began with the mail: sorting the bundle, the ribbon binding it cool beneath his fingers.

Gwendolyn Vance entered, her steps measured, a sheaf of new invoices in hand. She placed them atop the desk, their edges perfectly aligned, and retrieved the hotel's bankbook from the locked drawer. The sudden collapse of her family's estate finances-never spoken of-had left her with a new, sharper attention to every line and sum, a habit that would not soon leave her.

Theodora Lachlan, draped in a fresh silk shawl, leaned against the window frame. She watched the staff move about the lounge, her posture relaxed for the first time in days. There was less strain at her eyes; the threat to her inheritance, at least for now, had been quietly dissolved.

Nora Quayle was not present. The office was emptier for her absence. In the garage, a row of spanners hung beside a battered oil can, the half-repaired stopwatch resting on the bench where Nora had left it the night before. The scent of machine oil lingered. A mug with a lipstick mark, still half full of cold tea, sat beside the tools. No one had moved it. The space felt suspended, as if waiting for her to return and finish what she'd started.

Bertram Norbury, his work here concluded, prepared to leave. He tucked his notebook into his case, the familiar weight a reassurance. Tomorrow, he would dispatch his report, the matter closed, and turn to the next inquiry-his professional reputation intact, if a shade more battered.

Gwendolyn, for her part, had assumed direct control of the hotel's accounts. Guests still gossiped about the scandal, but her authority was rarely questioned now. She paused to answer a query from a junior clerk, her words brisk yet not unkind.

A memory of Montague Gaunt lingered, unconnected to his death: a flash of wild laughter at the wheel, hair windblown, defying the limits of both engine and fate. Even now, his bravado echoed in the way the hotel's racing trophies caught the light, undimmed.

Harriet Kestrel, newly promoted to head housekeeper, moved through the corridors with a lighter step. She paused to adjust a crooked painting, her hands steady, her gaze lingering on the scene outside-the dunes, the sea, their silence undisturbed.

Theodora excused herself, her voice a model of understatement. "That would be unfortunate, if the scones are burned," she said, her laughter trailing behind as she went to the kitchen.

Harriet's answer was soft, four words only. "They never are, ma'am." She straightened the tea tray, aligning the cups with characteristic precision.

Norbury, his case in hand, offered a final long speech-words of thanks, advice, a gentle reminder that the world would keep spinning no matter the scandal or sorrow. Harriet listened, her answer brief and final. "You're needed elsewhere, sir."

As the clock struck midnight, the hotel office emptied. The dunes outside, for all their secrets, resumed their long, silent watch, the moonlight smoothing the last traces of violence from their shifting face. Down the corridor, the bell for the night porter rang; a guest's laughter carried up the stairwell. In the office, the page of the ledger turned, and the distant clatter of crockery drifted from the kitchens. The case was closed. Life at the hotel, in all its quiet routines, pressed forward.
