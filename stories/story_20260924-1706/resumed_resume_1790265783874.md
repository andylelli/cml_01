"You chose yourself over the sanctuary, over the truth. I hope it was worth the price." She did not wait for a reply, but stepped away, her boots silent on the rug. Cecil Rutherford let out a breath, the sound rough in the quiet. He ran a hand over the battered cap in his pocket, shaking his head. "All that for this?" The words fell flat, and the room seemed to shrink around them, the air thick with the residue of accusation and confession. Ottoline Orme moved to the door, her answer flat. "I'll fetch the constable." She did not look back as she left, the sound of her footsteps fading into the corridor. Frances Underhill remained by the hearth, her hand resting on the mantelpiece.

Around her, the spring morning crept in through the window-cool, damp, and indifferent to human schemes. The clock ticked on, its chime now honest, marking the end of one story and the uncertain beginning of another.

## Chapter 10: The Price of Truth

By late morning, the hush in the drawing room was of a different quality-less the silence of suspense, more the weight of something finished. The fire had burned low, leaving only a faint warmth on the air, and the scent of damp wood mingled with the sharper tang of lavender polish. Outside, the spring mist still clung to the glass, but inside, the world had shifted: Gerald Thorne was gone, taken into custody after the confession he had signed, and the household's order-such as it was-had begun to unravel and re-form around the absence. Frances Underhill remained by the hearth, her hand resting on the mantelpiece where she had steadied herself before, the smooth wood cool against her palm. The clock above ticked on, its chime now honest, the hour unremarkable but for the knowledge of what it had once concealed.

The others had not dispersed; they lingered, as if some unfinished business still held them in orbit. Marguerite Ashgrove stood near the window, arms folded, gaze turned outward to the pale light. Adela Bellamy, upright and composed, busied herself with a stack of field notes, her posture rigid-shoulders drawn back with the same brittle dignity Frances had seen after a humiliating critique at a botanical conference, a memory now visible in the set of her jaw. "What happens now?" Cecil Rutherford's voice broke the quiet, rough as gravel. He stood by the door, cap in hand, boots still carrying the scent of wet earth. No one answered at once. The question hung between them, as much about the village as the estate, about the future as the past. "We do what must be done," Ottoline Orme replied, her tone steady.

She moved to the sideboard, gathering the letters and ledgers that had been left in disarray. Her hands were precise, her movements economical, but Frances saw the tension in her shoulders-Ottoline's duty had always been to the house, but now that duty was heavier, the lines of responsibility redrawn. She did not look at the others as she spoke. "The constable will send word to the market town. The trustees will be notified. The sanctuary will need a new steward." She paused, the faint echo of the clock's chime filling the space where Gerald Thorne's voice might once have answered. Marguerite Ashgrove turned from the window, her gaze sharp. "There's a torn page by the barn," Marguerite said, holding it up-a scrap of the birdwatching journal, the handwriting unmistakable. "No one's claimed it. I suppose it's evidence now, or just another thing for the constable's pile."

She let the page fall to the table, blunt as ever. "At least the birds don't care who keeps the books." Adela Bellamy's reply was quiet, almost a whisper. "The birds care more than you think, Marguerite. The sanctuary was meant to protect them. Now-" She stopped, her gloved hand trembling as she pressed it flat atop her notes. She did not finish the thought. Instead, she looked to Frances, her eyes searching for something-absolution, perhaps, or simply a reason to believe the work would go on. "We have surveys to complete. I'll see to the aviary myself." "You'll manage," Cecil Rutherford said, a sardonic edge in his tone. "Always do. The rest of us will muddle through." He shrugged, but the gesture lacked its usual defiance. He turned to Ottoline Orme. "If you need help with the accounts, I'll lend a hand. No sense letting the books rot while the lawyers argue."

A faint smile flickered at the corner of Ottoline Orme's mouth, gone almost before it appeared. She nodded once. "That will do." She returned to her sorting, the letters making a soft, steady sound as she stacked them. The routine of the house, battered but unbroken, began to reassert itself. Frances crossed to the table, her footsteps muffled by the thick rug. She picked up the torn journal page, her thumb tracing the edge where it had been ripped. The handwriting was careful, the ink faded. She set it aside for the constable-a relic of Gerald Thorne's presence, now only a piece of the record. She glanced at the clock, its face reflecting the grey daylight, and allowed herself a single, dry observation. "It seems the clock will keep honest hours now, at least until someone else decides to improve upon them."

No one laughed, but the tension eased, the remark a small release after so much restraint. Marguerite Ashgrove snorted. "If the next steward tries, I'll break the minute hand myself." She did not look at Frances, but her bluntness drew a faint, genuine smile from Adela Bellamy-a brief moment of shared relief amid the wreckage. The drawing room's order resumed by degrees. Ottoline Orme opened the windows, letting in the vernal air and the distant sound of a birdcall. Adela Bellamy gathered her field notes, her hands steadier now, and left for the sanctuary. Cecil Rutherford lingered a moment longer, then followed Ottoline Orme to the study, the business of the estate calling them back to ordinary life. Marguerite Ashgrove remained by the window, her gaze fixed on the hedgerow, as if searching for something she could not name.

Frances lingered, alone at last, her thoughts circling the man who had been lost. She remembered Dr. Jardine's habit of humming under his breath as he catalogued a new species-never quite in tune, always persistent. It was not a detail that mattered to the constable or the trustees, but it mattered to her. She straightened the chair he had favoured, the one by the fire, and set his spectacles on the side table, a small act of restoration amid so much loss. Frances Underhill's eyes settled on a note tucked among Dr. Jardine's papers-a terse warning about restricting access to the farm. The phrase was abrupt, but its implication clear: Jardine had threatened to limit entry, a fact that now seemed crucial. Frances folded the note carefully and looked up, her eyes narrowing. "Jardine's warning about restricting access to the farm-it changes everything," she said softly.

"If he was trying to keep people out, it suggests a motive tied directly to the farm itself, and that premeditation wasn't accidental." The household's rhythm resumed, but nothing was quite as it had been. Gerald Thorne's name would not be spoken easily, not for a long time. The sanctuary would survive, but the cost of its preservation was written in the silence that followed each honest chime of the clock. Outside, the mist began to lift, and the first sunlight of the day crept across the floorboards, pale and uncertain. Frances watched it for a moment, then turned away, the price of truth settling quietly into the bones of Blackmoor Sanctuary.
