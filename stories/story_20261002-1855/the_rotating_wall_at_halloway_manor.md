"You'll find quiet again." Marguerite offered a wry, almost rueful smile. She let her hand drift to the small vase on the desk, adjusting its position by a fraction.

Adela watched Harriet and Ottoline move around the room—one light with the release of tension, the other quietly grateful. She recorded the moment in her mind, knowing how quickly relief could be replaced by the next set of demands or disappointments.

Marguerite Selwyn cleared. She smoothed the lace at her wrist and stepped closer to Ottoline, offering a hand that trembled only a little. Ottoline accepted, pressing her palm to Marguerite's with a gentle squeeze, her voice caught in her throat.

Harriet Bellamy cleared. She lifted her cane and tapped it gently against the hearthstone, her glance to Adela full of something like camaraderie. She picked up her cup again, this time holding it with both hands.

Ottoline Fairweather cleared. She let go of the window latch, returning to the hearth to retrieve her brush, her hands moving with greater steadiness, the tremor eased by certainty at last.

Adela, standing at the centre of the room, surveyed the scene: the letter opener laid back in its drawer, the paperweight at rest, the lamp's light steady on each of their faces. The clock above the mantel ticked on, marching towards half past eight with tranquil indifference.

Outside, the first stars showed against the dusk. Inside, the drawing room filled with the small, deliberate movements of people returned to themselves—not as suspects, but as women bearing the aftermath of relief, of apology, of gratitude. Ottoline gathered the ashes into her dustpan, Harriet drained the last of her tea, and Marguerite folded her gloves, her eyes meeting Adela's just once before looking away. The days would go on now, the ordinary tasks resumed, and the case—at last—was cleared.

## Chapter 10: Ordinary Turns Again

"Is there more we must see to, Miss Halloway?" Ottoline Fairweather asked, her voice hushed but dusted with her familiar self-mockery as she hovered in the doorway. Adela Halloway barely looked up from her notes, her pen held precisely over the margin where a case once lingered unfinished, the lamplight glancing off the edge of the page.

"No. We're finished." "No. We're finished." Her hand did not stop its steady movement as she checked final lines on her pad and tucked it back into the battered folder that had seen too much use and one notorious disgrace.

Ottoline nodded, relief softening her face. She shifted her grip to the broom handle she had absent-mindedly carried with her, then glanced toward the corridor where the morning's sweep awaited. She glanced at the doorway, thinking of the kitchen staff and the scullery maid, and smiled faintly—she would join them soon. The ordinary sounds of the manor—soft voices and the muted clang of a pail from the scullery—tugged them back to the routines that had been left to tangle during the investigation.

Ivor Yardley stood in perfect stillness beside the heavy stone panel, his hands folded before him, each finger precisely aligned. The corridor beyond was quiet, the hush broken only by the distant reverberation of a door closing somewhere near the stables. His composure was glassy, eyes fixed on the pattern of the carpet and the faint outline of his own shoe where the dust had yet to be disturbed by others.

At the foot of the bed, Adela returned the letter opener to its evidence envelope and slid it into the valise the constable would collect. She moved with the careful economy of someone keenly aware of what a misstep can cost—her mind unbidden to an earlier case, a ruined reputation, and a letter never sent.

"Miss Halloway, what now?" Ottoline asked, her nerves prompting her to fuss with the hem of her apron, the bristles of her broom trailing across the stone flags.

"Life resumes," Adela said, her tone even. She watched as Ottoline straightened, set the broom aside, and smoothed her palms over the place where the carpet edge had been pried up so many times in recent days.

Harriet Bellamy's shadow drifted past the frosted glass in the corridor, her cane tapping softly in counterpoint to the clock in the hall. She paused just out of sight, a performer's instinct holding her back from a scene no longer hers to command. In the kitchen, a kettle hissed: the day's first cup of tea at liberty from suspicion.

Ivor shifted, his voice emerging with the brittle courtesy that did not quite mask bitterness. "Will you have an inventory drawn, Miss Halloway?" His line, cool and brief, hung in the air.

"Yes, by tomorrow." Adela's response was precise. She handed the inventory form to the constable, who tucked it under his arm and nodded, the task now in official hands.

Ottoline moved to the fireplace, gathering the stray matchsticks left from the constable's earlier search. She stooped carefully, the tremor in her right hand only faint now, and dropped each piece into the ash bucket she carried—familiar, humble work, a grace not granted to those who lived only in the extraordinary.

Outside, the lamps were being checked for the night. Marguerite Selwyn's silhouette passed the window, her posture composed as she directed the gardeners to restore the gravel path—each instruction clipped, her hands gloved and gestures economical. She paused, glanced at the house, and then returned to her papers in the drawing room, a letter half-written and waiting.

The door to the corridor opened, the constable entering quietly to retrieve the valise containing the murder weapon and Adela's bundle of notes. He paused, offering a respectful nod to Adela, who handed over the evidence without ceremony. The constable paused, then left with the valise and bundle of notes, his boots echoing softly as he stepped into the corridor and closed the door behind him.

Ottoline, lingering by the hearth, caught Adela's attention with a question about the state of the kitchen chimney. "If you ever tire of cases, Miss Halloway, there's always soot and mischief to clear—though I suspect the soot's more forgiving." Her words, a smile beneath, earned a rare upward quirk from Adela.

"I'll keep that in mind." Adela's reply was as brief as her humour allowed, eyes turning to the window where dusk gathered itself.

The room settled quietly. Ivor, having answered all that the law and Adela required, looked up as Ottoline remarked on the peculiar shape of closure—the slow, anticlimactic return of the everyday after a week of secrets and accusations. "It's odd, Mr. Yardley, how normal feels so strange, now the questions are done and the brooms are out again and the dinner bell rings as if nothing ever happened—though I suppose you might say it's the ordinary that's always waiting just out of sight, ready to sweep us up, whether we like it or not." She finished her winding sentence with a pause, watching him for any sign of reaction.

"Resumption is not redemption." Ivor's reply, six words, sliced the air cleanly; he tugged at his cuffs with fingers that trembled just a fraction.

In the corner, Adela inspected her own coat, which had hung untouched in the wardrobe since the day of her arrival. She checked the pockets with slow deliberation, her hand brushing the monogrammed handkerchief—an unwanted reminder of her first public humiliation, never spoken of, always at hand.

The constable's footsteps faded, and for a moment the room was its old self: stone, linen, and the persistent echo of work resumed. Adela noted the time on the mantel clock, its hands moving without protest past the hour that had once been stopped by human interference—an honest measure of passing, now.

Near the bed, Ottoline picked up Cecil Thorne's comb, left behind on the dressing table. She turned it over, thumb running along the teeth, recalling how he had used it each morning, hair parted with the same precision that marked his days. Ottoline set the comb gently beside the bucket, her eyes closing briefly in silent thanks as she stood alone by the bed.

Adela crossed to the window, watching the lights of the kitchen flare as Harriet and Marguerite returned to their evening tasks—receipts tallied, letters posted, dogs let out for their run on the south lawn. She registered each with the satisfaction of order restored, her own presence withdrawable now the crisis had passed.

Ottoline's broom swept the hall with slow, deliberate strokes; the clock ticked, and voices drifted from the kitchen, each sign of routine returning as the last question faded. In the hall, Ottoline's broom brushed the stone with steady strokes. The clock on the mantel ticked, its hands deliberate, marking the unremarkable passage of time—a world resumed, missing one, but closing round the absence with the work that every day requires.
