Montague, having set the foil aside, weighed it in his hand, feeling along the guard and balance point. He set it down again, glancing at Desmond, and then picked up the duplicate key resting among the other effects. The key was old, its surface marked by careful filing, and it slipped easily into the waiting handkerchief Montague offered, as if the gesture had been rehearsed.

Bertram Vance, standing by the bookcase, angled a lamp for better view. "A solicitor with architectural blueprints, a hollow foil, and a key to a death-trap—how very modern," he drawled, his voice smooth as he slotted a book of legal maxims back into place.

Montague Norbury ignored him, pocketing the duplicate key, and turned to Neville. "Would you care to confirm, Mr. Lachlan?"

"Fits the description," Neville answered, his gaze steady on the evidence. He set the blueprints aside, watching Desmond as he did so.

For a time, the room took on the hush of a library after hours—ink drying, clocks ticking, the rasp of a match as Montague lit the desk lamp to better examine the mechanism of the foil. In the yellow pool of light, the hollow inside the weapon gleamed. The room's stillness was broken only by Desmond's movements as he gathered spare paper clips from his tray, arranging them by size.

"One key, two blueprints, and a solicitor's knowledge of tides," Montague said, turning the key over. "That's a precise set of advantages, wouldn't you say?"

Desmond's reply came in four words: "Standard preparation. Legal necessity." Bertram smirked, shifting his weight from one foot to the other, the movement drawing attention to a loose floorboard beneath the carpet.

Neville, returning to his seat, let his hand rest atop the fencing foil, the cold metal unyielding beneath his palm. He spoke at length, his tone measured, each word carrying the deliberate weight of experience: "The trick of a duplicate key is knowing when not to use it. I've taught fencing here for twenty years, and in that time, I've seen more locks forced by impatience than by any thief's cunning. Whoever handled this foil last understood the value of precision—it's not just the blade that kills, but the hand that guides it, and the judgement to choose the moment. We all have our reasons to be cautious—some of us to keep our places, others out of simple pride. But this—" he gestured to the hollow foil and key, "—isn't the work of chance. It's the mark of someone who prepared their ground, long before the tide receded."

Desmond's answer was sharp: "Theory, not proof." Neville left it at that, shifting his cane and watching Bertram over the rim of his glasses.

Montague Norbury laid the hollow foil and key side by side with the blueprints, then drew a line through Hector Gaunt's name on his suspect list. He turned to Desmond, voice steady as he recited the clearance. "Hector Gaunt's alibi places him in the billiard room from two fifty to three twenty-five—witnessed by three guests and no way to reach the victim's room through the cave or the locked doors. No physical evidence connects him to the act."

Across the room, Hector Gaunt, who had entered quietly, adjusted his cufflinks with deliberate care. The gesture was precise, almost ceremonial—his hands lingered a moment too long, as if recalling the humiliation of a public disgrace. He said nothing, but the set of his jaw spoke for him, and he turned to the window, eyes on the darkening horizon.

Montague's pen ticked on, striking through Neville Lachlan's name next. "Neville Lachlan's presence in the fencing hall is confirmed from three o'clock to a quarter to four, by both staff and pupils. No time, no means, no evidence of handling the murder weapon at the hour in question."

Neville inclined his head, the briefest nod, and set his walking stick squarely in its stand. "Thank you, Norbury," he said, his voice quiet, hands folded in his lap. The movement was understated, but a trace of relief unknotted the tension in his shoulders.

Bertram Vance's turn came. Montague's tone was implacable. "Bertram Vance is accounted for in his private quarters, on maintenance duty—his work unrelated to the murder's timing, and corroborated by both staff logs and his presence near the coastal windows, which explains the salt corrosion but nothing more."

Bertram's smile was brittle. "A fine mess, but not mine." He flicked a stray paper scrap from his sleeve with exaggerated courtesy.

Montague paused, letting the clearance settle, and then regarded the silent photograph of Katherine Quayle atop Desmond's desk. "Katherine Quayle is cleared by virtue of being the victim," he said, and set the pen down. The photograph caught the lamplight, the sharp lines of Katherine's fencing jacket at odds with the gentle cast of her smile.

An exchange, separate from the investigation, passed between Montague and Desmond. Montague, folding the blueprints, mused aloud: "One day we'll run out of hidden keys." Desmond met his gaze, the flicker of a smile at the edge of his mouth, and replied, "Unlikely, in this house." Montague chuckled, the sound brief but genuine, and handed the duplicate key to Neville for safekeeping.

The night pressed against the window. Outside, the surf fell silent for a moment, as if holding its breath, while inside the lamp shed a circle of clarity on a world grown suddenly more orderly. Each man, in turn, touched the evidence—foil, key, blueprint, photograph—before stepping back into the corridor, the matter of guilt not yet settled but the matter of means grown sharper.

Montague Norbury lingered at the threshold, his hand on the doorframe. "Keys and secrets, gentlemen. In the end, it's the one you keep that matters." He closed the door softly, the click lost in the hush that settles just before the truth comes out.

## Chapter 6: Alibis Under Scrutiny

"Let's have it in turn—where were you, Hector, when the clock struck twenty minutes past three?" Montague Norbury asked, his pen poised above a fresh sheet in his notebook as the four men assembled in the hotel billiard room. The heavy velvet curtains had been drawn back, letting in the last yellow of the day, and the green baize of the table bore silent witness to the gravity of the hour.

"I was here, Montague," Hector Gaunt replied, his tone flat as he tapped his knuckles once against the edge of the billiard table—cool, deliberate, as if confirming the fact for himself as well as for Norbury. Montague wrote without looking up, the pen tracing a line across the page. The room itself carried the hush of old rivalries and private stakes, the balls in the rack untouched since midday.

Neville Lachlan sat with his back to the wall, cane resting against his thigh, his hands folded tight as he regarded the grain of the table's edge. "He was in this room from two fifty until three twenty-five. I crossed the corridor at three and again at three twenty—saw him both times. No doubt about it," Neville offered, his gaze not shifting from a faint scratch in the table's finish. The old injury in his knee pressed him to shift in his seat, and the movement was as precise as a fencer's parry.

"Did anyone else see him?" Montague pressed, closing his notebook with a soft snap. In response, Bertram Vance, leaning against the doorframe and toying with a chalk cube, replied, "I passed the lounge twice collecting window keys for maintenance—Hector was in plain view, frowning at the billiard balls and not much else." He flicked the chalk into the pocket of his jacket, the gesture almost too casual.

Montague's voice levelled. "I'll want you to state your time in the fencing hall, Mr. Lachlan. Exact, if you please." Neville's answer was succinct: "Three o'clock to a quarter to four." Across the hall, the clang of metal against metal filtered through the doorway, the faint echo of a day's practice just concluded.

Bertram, with his usual careful enunciation, began a small speech. "I was in my quarters—private, not by choice but by rota—between ten minutes past three and twenty minutes to four. The chambermaid and the second coach checked the windows with me, and if you ask the laundry maid she'll confirm the salt corrosion on the latches, which is all I managed to repair before the business with the tide distracted us all." He finished with a small bow of the head, drumming his fingers on the door panel to punctuate his point.

Montague's reply was dry: "That covers it." He rose, running a palm along the rim of the billiard table, and motioned for the men to follow as he led them through the hall to the bright-lit fencing room where the timetables and staff logs lay open.

"Let's set it against the record," Montague said, collecting the attendance ledger from the wall hook and flipping the stiff pages. "Three o'clock: fencing session commences. Present, Neville Lachlan, two students, and the staff secretary. Three forty-five: session ends. That places you, Mr. Lachlan, under observation for the entire window." He set the ledger on the desk, fingers resting on the corner as he reviewed the signatures.

Hector, from his place near the rack of foils, watched Montague's careful inventory with something close to bemusement. "So I'm cleared by clocks and company, not by opinion," he drawled, his hand idly tracing the carved initials on the cue rack. Montague, expression unwavering, turned the record toward Bertram.

"You, Mr. Vance—your whereabouts at the time?" Montague asked. Bertram's lips curled in a small, precise smile. "In my quarters, as I said, reviewing maintenance lists and failing to avoid the smell of brine. The housekeeper caught me at it, and the maid can state the same. I did not leave until I was summoned by the noise in the south corridor."

"Thank you," Montague replied, barely a motion in his voice. He pressed the pen to the log and drew a tick beside Bertram's name.

"Does no one find it odd that all our alibis are so conveniently overlapping?" Hector asked, his tone measured as he chalked a billiard cue with brisk, methodical strokes. Neville, adjusting his grip on his cane, looked up, a small twist to his mouth. "We're always overlapping here. The only thing that doesn't is the truth," he said, turning his gaze to the rack of fencing masks hanging on the wall.

"Let's have it spelled out," Montague said, aligning a stack of index cards on the desk. "Hector Gaunt, seen in the billiard room from two fifty to three twenty-five. Neville Lachlan, in the fencing hall from three o'clock to a quarter to four. Bertram Vance, in his quarters from ten past three to twenty minutes to four." His voice rose slightly as he addressed the room, the gravity of the pattern settling over them.

Bertram, undeterred by the seriousness, leaned closer, his tone light but unyielding. "You can check with the kitchen staff if you like, Norbury. I had the windows open for the salt to dry and the door locked behind me. If I'd gone out by the rocks, I'd have left half my trousers behind." He straightened, smoothing his lapels with a self-satisfied air.

Montague, eyes flicking from Bertram to Hector, and then to Neville, spoke with a crisp authority. "Multiple witnesses confirm all three alibis. The times match the staff logs and witness statements. That leaves Katherine's room inaccessible—locked from within, window secured, and only the sea offering a route, for ten minutes at the lowest tide."

Hector, his hand on a billiard ball, let the silence settle before replying. "Then you've boxed yourself in, Norbury. If the only entry is the cave, and none of us could have used it, you've described an impossible crime." He sent the ball rolling down the green felt—smooth, unhurried, and wholly without purpose.

"Or I've described a trick we haven't seen through yet," Montague answered, sliding the staff log back into its drawer and closing it with a decisive click. The sound echoed in the high-ceilinged hall, and for a moment, everyone listened for something more—a slip, a confession, a forgotten detail to surface.

"Is this the bit where you tell us it was the ghost of fencing past?" Bertram asked at length, his tone so gentle it nearly masked the cruelty of the remark. "Or are we to wait for the tide to turn up the answer as well as the weapon?"

Neville's reply was simple: "Tide's not that reliable." He steadied himself as he stood, pressing a hand to his knee, and reached for the cleanest of the foils to hang it precisely back in its place.

Montague Norbury, his hand resting on the polished wood of the record desk, turned the matter over in his mind, matching the alibi intervals to the timetable of the tide. "We have from twenty minutes past three to half past three for the cave's exposure," he said aloud. "Three o'clock to a quarter to four for your fencing hall session, Neville. Ten minutes past three to twenty minutes to four for Bertram in his quarters. Two fifty to three twenty-five—Hector in the billiard room. The overlap makes it difficult—on paper, none of you should have had the time unaccounted for, but the window is there all the same. No sign, though, that any of you could have left your post for the minutes required to commit the crime."

Hector's gaze never left Montague's face. "Quite the predicament," he said, and Montague's pen stilled on the page, the ink drying in a black pool above the word 'alibi'.

The room's tension faded neither with the evening nor with the certainty of their innocence. Bertram Vance, unable to resist, balanced a billiard ball on the edge of his palm, spinning it once before setting it back in the rack with exaggerated care.

For a moment, the only sound was the faint whirr of the electric fan overhead, stirring the air and the papers Montague had so carefully arranged. Hector Gaunt's eyes dropped to his cufflinks, fingers running the engraved initials as though reading a history too bitter to voice.

Neville Lachlan, having hung the foils, ran his thumb along the length of the rack, testing its sturdiness—a senseless, habitual gesture, but one that comforted after the violence of the day. Bertram, for his part, produced a deck of cards from his jacket and began to shuffle, the soft slap of the pasteboards a counterpoint to whatever regret lingered unspoken in the room.

Montague Norbury, his hand returning to his cane, regarded the three men, one after the other. "We are left, gentlemen, with only two possibilities. Either the murderer is not in this room, or the route and timing of the act are not what we suppose."

"Or," Bertram said, his voice as gentle as glass, "the tide will show you a path you've not yet seen—if you're willing to look beneath the surface." He cut the deck with a flourish, the cards sliding together as the last light of day settled over the billiard table, illuminating nothing more than the orderly chaos of suspicion suspended.

In the brief hush that followed, Neville straightened his coat and collected his cane, the limp in his step more pronounced as he moved toward the door. Montague Norbury, left at the desk, pressed his palm once more to the cool wood, the imprint fading as quickly as the day's certainties. And the room, once host to accusation and defense, returned for a moment to its old, comfortable purpose—a place for games that mattered less than those just played.

## Chapter 7: Lies and Distractions

"Suppose we try candor for once," Montague Norbury said, his hands resting lightly on his cane as the group gathered in the dim-lit reading room. The dark wood gleamed with the sheen of recent polish, and the brass lamp on the side table threw a soft glow onto the heap of notebooks, tide charts, and blueprints Montague had arranged before him. The only sound was the faint rasp of Bertram Vance's thumb flicking across the edge of a deck of cards, shuffled more from habit than hope.

"I always do," Hector Gaunt replied, his tone as short as the list he kept of his own vices. Bertram, arms folded, tapped a card flat on the table, his gaze flicking sideways to Desmond Kestrel, who sat at the edge of the light, quietly turning a silver pen between long fingers.

Neville Lachlan, seated near the old stone hearth, noticed the thin trail of cigar ash on the lace doily—a detail so trivial it bespoke a world preoccupied by anything but murder—and brushed it gently away before he spoke. "Everyone's very sure of their part these days," he said, the smallest hint of amusement in his voice. He settled his walking stick against the fender and adjusted the cuff of his jacket with a practiced precision, watching as Desmond set his pen aside on the blotter.

Montague Norbury, never one to waste collected silence, reached for a folder thick with correspondence. "I want to see your notes, Desmond," he said, sliding the folder across the table. Desmond met his gaze and opened it, the rustle of paper sharp in the quiet, and passed several sheets to Montague: letters, receipts, and a sheaf of typed instructions, each with dates and initials in Desmond's neat, angular hand. Montague glanced briefly at the first page, then laid it aside, scanning for items that might illuminate motive. "Let's focus," he said, "on the instructions and Hector's correspondence—what did you actually do, Desmond?"

"There's a great deal here about the will," Montague observed, leafing through the papers and pausing over a page with Hector's signature. "And several notes on the cave's access times. Why so thorough, Desmond?"

"Legal necessity," Desmond answered, voice as level as the edge of the envelope he now aligned with his thumb. Montague circled a line in the margin with a pencil, glancing at Hector, who was studying the bookshelf, his attention elsewhere but his posture too careful to be truly distracted.

Bertram Vance reached to collect a stray card from the floor, the gesture easy, but the set of his mouth gave him away—a man who had lost heavily and was here to bluff until the next deal. He did not look at Neville, but his tone carried: "Curious that everyone's papers are in such perfect order, except the finances. Funny how chaos only ever lands where there's money to be made, or lost." His fingers worried the edge of the card for a moment, then tucked it back into the deck.

Neville's answer was brief: "Nature of the game." Montague made a note and shifted his gaze to Hector.

Hector Gaunt, who had moved to the drinks tray, poured himself a measure of whisky with the measured efficiency of a man redistributing his nerves. He turned to Desmond, glass in hand, and spoke at length, his words measured and cold as he addressed the small assembly: "It is a tedious thing, being made the villain in a house where one's only real crime was ambition. Yes, I forged the signature—no, not Katherine's, but my own, and not for theft, but for expedience. The books are a maze, gentlemen, and if you'd spent half as long in the academy's offices as I have, you'd know that every man in this room has cut a corner or two to keep his place. The will was changed: Hector's signature, in black ink, stood out beneath Katherine's on the revised page Montague held aloft. "I went to Desmond," Hector continued, "and showed him the date. I asked him to delay the submission. He nodded, but his hand hesitated over the envelope." Not because I wanted the old woman dead, but because I still hoped she'd forgive me before the ink was dry."

Neville's response was delivered without looking up: "That's your affair." He took up a poker from the stand and shifted the embers in the grate, the iron scraping softly across the brick.

Across the room, Bertram replaced his cards in their battered box, hands steady for the first time that evening. The cards' corners were scuffed—evidence of long nights spent courting luck and finding her absent—and he set them on the desk beside the folder of correspondence. The lamplight caught the shine on his cufflinks, and he adjusted one absently.

"Let's talk about the stains," Montague said, nodding to Neville, who rose—slowly, favoring his injured knee—and collected the fencing foil from the mantel where it had been resting. He carried it to the table and, with the careful touch of long practice, set it where the lamplight revealed the fresh stains and the telltale pattern of handling on the guard.

"These are new," Neville said, tracing the marks with the pad of his thumb. "Not old rust, not training residue—this is tonight's work. And these scratches—look, here—" he rotated the hilt for Montague to see, "—they fit Desmond's gloves exactly. The cross-hatch on the palm, the point of pressure just there. Not a fencer's mark, but the grip of a man who's spent more time in a clerk's office than on the piste."

Desmond, watching the examination, answered with the ghost of a smile: "Documentation never lies."

Montague Norbury, for his part, made a note in his book. "So you handled the foil, Desmond. When?"

"Earlier today, after fencing had concluded and the weapon was returned to the rack," Desmond replied, voice uninflected. He touched the folder at his elbow, aligning its corners with those of the ledger beside it.

Bertram, never missing a chance, interjected: "The staff logs show the foils were checked at three forty-five, and Desmond was seen in the hall after that—alone. If anyone had the chance to—how shall we say—prepare a weapon for more than display, it was him." His tone remained courteous, but the accusation was not.

Desmond's reply was as mild as the rain: "Circumstance, not certainty." Bertram looked away, smoothing the lapel of his jacket, the gesture betraying a restless energy he could not quite suppress.

Montague, now intent on detail, took up the blueprints once more, his hand steady as he traced the line from the cave's entrance to the window beneath Katherine's room. He paused over the notations, the tip of his pencil resting on the crucial segment. "There are instructions here, Desmond, in your hand, and a note from Hector. They speak plainly of the cave, the tides, and the need to secure the window locks. Were you, or Hector, planning to alter the locks, or only to delay the will?"

Desmond's expression did not change, but a flicker passed through his eyes. "Both, as required," he said. He gathered the pages, stacking them in a precise pile.

Neville Lachlan, watching the scene as an outsider might, noticed the faint smudge of blood on the foil's guard, hardly visible unless the light struck it just so. He held the foil at arm's length, considering the absurdity of murder by fencing weapon in a house devoted to the art. "All this—" he said, gesturing with the foil, "—for a school, a title, and a handful of ledgers. Money comes and goes, but pride—well, that's harder to clean up."

The remark hung in the air for a moment, unchallenged. Bertram reached for the whisky decanter and poured himself a drink, the liquid catching the lamp's gold. He raised his glass in a silent toast to the room, then drank deeply, setting the glass down with a faint clink.

Montague Norbury, gathering up the evidence, spoke with the calm of a man who knew the storm would break soon enough. "We have Desmond's notes, correspondence with Hector, fresh handling marks on the murder weapon, and a route through the cave only accessible for ten minutes at low tide. Tomorrow, we will reenact the passage. But tonight, I want each of you to look at what's missing—from the ledger, from the tide charts, and from the stains on the foil. Find what none of us have admitted. The next question may not be so easy to answer."

Hector Gaunt, unmoved, finished his whisky and set the glass precisely at the tray's edge. "Ask the right question, Norbury. You may find the answer less clever than you hoped." He left the room, the door closing behind him with a click.

Montague, setting his cane against the table, watched the others as they drifted apart: Bertram lingering to shuffle his cards one last time, Desmond collecting his pen and papers with mechanical efficiency, Neville testing the weight of the fencing foil for balance before returning it to its case.

A log snapped in the fire. Neville, noting the sound, murmured, "Even the smallest things come undone, if you watch long enough." He settled back in his chair, hand resting on the cool metal of his cane, and let the evening close around him. The night outside pressed close to the windows, and the sea's dull roar below was the only sign that time—and tide—waited for no man.

## Chapter 8: The Corridor Revealed

"Gentlemen, we will begin," Montague Norbury announced, cane pressed to the floor, as five men clustered in Katherine Quayle's room, the afternoon light slanting across the patterned carpet and the cold fencing foil set neatly atop the sheeted bed. The window—lower sash open a bare inch—framed the jagged rocks below, where the sound of the sea was sharper, the tide at its lowest. Hector Gaunt crossed to the sill and looked down, brows drawn, as the slap of waves against stone marked each passing second. Desmond Kestrel stood by the wardrobe, hands folded, his eyes tracing the blueprints Montague unrolled across the bedside table.

"Let's have the test," Bertram Vance said, his voice gentle as he held out the hollow foil, the hilt cool in his glove. Montague, moving with cautious precision, accepted it and set the foil on the desk beside the duplicate key and the tide chart, the paper crinkling as he pressed it flat. They watched as Neville Lachlan, steady despite the old injury in his knee, checked his watch and nodded toward the clock on the mantel. The minute hand showed twenty minutes past three—coincidence or calculation, the hour at which everything turned.

Montague nodded to Hector Gaunt. "Hector, if you would—demonstrate the passage from the cave." Hector, without hesitation, crossed to the window, his hand braced against the sill. Montague and Bertram led the way downstairs and out across the rocks, shoes crunching on rough stone as the wind picked at their sleeves. Desmond watched from above, arms folded, while Neville remained by the desk, tapping his cane in a slow rhythm along the edge of the blueprints.

Hector descended the slippery path to the mouth of the cave, boots scraping against the barnacled lip. He bent, fingers testing the damp rocks, then crouched low to enter the arch, his shoulders hunched to clear the ceiling. Montague, waiting at the base, peered into the dim corridor, the air thick with salt and the reek of seaweed. "Proceed, if you can," Montague called, his voice carrying between the stone walls.

Hector moved a yard into the gloom, his hand trailing along the wet surface, but stopped short. "Impossible," he called up, voice echoing, "the passage narrows. I can't reach the foundation, not without the proper key or a means to unfasten the window above." Montague gestured for him to return, and Hector emerged into the sunlight, brushing grit from his hands. Bertram offered a crisp, "Try again with a skeleton key, if you like," but Hector shook his head, the answer clear.

Upstairs, Neville watched Desmond Kestrel with the wary attention of a man waiting for a move yet unplayed. "Suppose you demonstrate, Desmond," Neville said, his tone almost bored, as he placed the duplicate key on the window ledge. Desmond, composed, accepted the challenge in silence. He picked up the fencing foil, weighing it in his palm, and approached the window. Without haste, he set the hilt against the sill and twisted the cap, revealing the hollow shaft. The brass key slid into his hand, and he inserted it into the concealed lock at the base of the sash.

A distinct click sounded, followed by the smooth glide of the window opening a full span—precisely as the blueprints promised. Desmond stepped onto the ledge, then, with the measured movements of a man who had practiced this, climbed through the window frame, his shoes scraping stone, and dropped to the rocks below. Montague, observing from the shore, noted the route as Desmond ducked into the shadowed mouth of the cave, the foil in hand. Within seconds, Desmond reappeared in the low corridor, his back to the wall, the foil's tip steady as a compass needle.

The group watched in silence as Desmond traced the length of the cave, then scaled the interior wall and passed through the window once more, returning to the room as quietly as he had left. Bertram gave a low, ironic whistle. "I'll concede—elegant, if not admirable. And if I may say, that's the neatest murder method I've seen this side of the ledger."

Desmond's response was as brief as it was unflinching: "Noted." Bertram set his back to the writing desk, thumbing through a sheaf of notes, the gesture slow and deliberate.

Montague Norbury, cane steady, faced the group and spoke with the weight of certainty. "The fencing foil found in this room carries fresh stains and distinct handling marks—marks that match Desmond Kestrel's glove and grip, and no one else's. By that evidence, Desmond Kestrel entered through the tidal cave, killed Katherine Quayle, and locked the window from within."

A hush fell. Desmond's hands, now empty, smoothed a crease from his sleeve, the movement as precise as any courtroom ritual, and said, "She was about to register the new will." For a moment, the only sound was the far-off echo of the tide beginning its return.

Montague's voice cut through, firm and clear as an order at sea. "Desmond Kestrel killed Katherine Quayle." The sentence, once spoken, changed the air in the room. Desmond's face did not alter, but his hand found the back of a chair, knuckles pale. "It would have cost me the position—Quayle Academy, in its entirety," he said, each word delivered as if reading a verdict aloud.

Neville stepped forward, cane tapping the carpet, his gaze sharp. "If the access ran only from twenty minutes past three to half past three, then the route was open no longer than ten minutes. That's all the time required, if you know the mechanism and the way." He looked to Montague, who nodded, confirming the sequence as the clock's minute hand slipped forward.

"Let's be clear," Bertram said at length, his tone courteous but the knife beneath every syllable. "You used the cave, the concealed key, and the lowest tide. A passage only possible in that ten-minute span—impossibly clever, unless you already planned to be the last man standing."

Desmond's reply was low. "I planned for the worst," he said, fingers tracing the seam of the blueprints where the corridor's line met the room above. He looked up, meeting Montague's eyes, but no plea came.

Neville's attention drifted to the fencing foil, where the stains had dried to a dull brown. He picked it up, holding it at arm's length, then set it back in its oilcloth case. "That's the route: cave to window, window to cave. Only a man with the key, the knowledge, and the motive could have done it—and only then, when the tide gave him time."

Bertram replaced the stack of notes on the desk, his hand lingering on the topmost page. "I suppose we'll be needing a new solicitor," he said, the smile never reaching his eyes.

Montague Norbury took up the duplicate key, weighing it in his palm as though it held the balance of justice. The scene held, the air charged with the knowledge that the truth had finally emerged, while outside, the tide began to rise, closing the illusory corridor that had, for ten minutes, turned the course of fate.

## Chapter 9: The Lie Unravelled

"Norbury, what now?" Hector Gaunt asked, his hands folded on the arm of the leather chair as the late afternoon sun slanted through the tall windows of the hotel lounge and caught the edge of the low glass table. The billiard cues leaned haphazard in the corner where the day had left them; Katherine Quayle's portrait on the wall threw a poised, distant challenge over the gathering. Montague Norbury, his cane steady between his knees, regarded each man in turn before replying.

"Desmond Kestrel is to be taken into custody," Montague said, his voice quiet but implacable as he made the notation in his small black diary. Hector's eyes flicked once to Desmond, then back to the chess set on the table, his movement betraying nothing.

Desmond Kestrel sat very upright, his fingers interlaced atop the battered attache case resting on his lap. His reply, when it came, was unhurried: "As per the documentation." Montague's left hand, affected by a mild but permanent tremor, adjusted the inkwell just so, as though reaffirming the order in a world newly shipwrecked.

Neville Lachlan, one arm draped over the back of the settee, watched Desmond with a neutral gaze. "It had to happen," he said, voice low, as he tapped the knot of his tie—loosened at last after a day's siege. Bertram Vance, always keen to reclaim advantage, busied himself with the remnants of a bridge hand abandoned at the call of justice, the cards slipping through his fingers in silent commentary.

Hector Gaunt cleared his throat. "I suppose the will goes as originally intended, barring further surprises?" His tone was clipped, every syllable weighed for irony. Montague glanced up, weighing the ledger between thumb and forefinger. "That is correct. The legal position remains unchanged until the proper authorities review the documents and the succession is—formally—adjudicated."

Neville turned his cane over in his grasp, the silver tip gleaming faintly. He addressed Desmond with the gravity of a man who had seen too much: "All this for a school and a title. Was it worth it?" The question hung, and Bertram, with the cruelest gentleness, supplied the afterthought: "What will you remember, Desmond? The moment you locked the window, or the first day you thought it possible?"

Desmond's answer was brief, almost absent: "Neither." Hector, disinclined to linger on sentiment, rearranged the chessmen by colour, not rank, as though order could be forced from the pieces.

For a time, the room filled with the small, necessary sounds of men resuming something like ordinary occupation. Montague Norbury, with a sigh, replaced the cap on his pen and closed the diary, his thumb tracing the groove worn by years of anxious habits. He leaned his cane against the table and folded his hands atop Katherine Quayle's monogrammed handkerchief, brought down from her room a final time. His voice was soft, but the words clear: "Hector Gaunt, cleared." Hector's hands stilled, a pawn halfway to the board's edge, before he set it firmly in place and looked to the window, his thoughts sealed behind a glassy stare.

"Neville Lachlan, cleared." Montague's tone was steady. Neville's fingers drummed briefly on the head of his cane as he met Bertram's gaze for a rare, silent accord, the past and its enmities momentarily set aside.

"Bertram Vance, cleared." Bertram let out a breath he had been holding, his knuckles whitening on the card deck before he tucked it back into his pocket, the gesture as practiced as the mask he wore for the room.

Desmond Kestrel did not move for a long moment. "That's the record," he said, the words carrying the same measured certitude with which he had once announced victory or defeat in the courtroom. Montague Norbury stood and, with a slight tremble in his hand, placed Katherine's handkerchief on the table as a kind of benediction.

Montague's first exchange was a question to Desmond, quiet, almost rhetorical: "You have nothing further?" Desmond's answer came in four words: "All matters are settled." Bertram, at the sideboard, poured a measure of sherry and nudged the glass toward Montague, who declined with a shake of the head, returning his attention to the matter at hand.

Neville, not quite ready to let the ledger close, addressed the group at length, his voice steady but coloured by the fatigue of old battles, new and old. "One can only hope we've seen the last of this sort of thing—a house divided by money and pride, all of us fencing shadows with nothing gained but loss and suspicion. I'll see to the academy's students, try to preserve something of what Katherine built, and perhaps, in time, the place will return to its proper work—teaching discipline, not fostering intrigue. For now, I'll accept your thanks, and hope you mean it as you say it, not as you wager it on the next hand."

Desmond's reply was simple: "As you wish." Neville nodded, letting his cane rest against his knee, and for a moment, the room was still save for the ticking of the longcase clock and the hush that follows a final answer.

Montague Norbury took up the portrait of Katherine Quayle from the mantel, his fingers brushing the gilt frame, and for a time the dead woman's eyes regarded them all with that same dry wit she'd shown in life. "As it should be," Montague said, echoing her, before restoring the picture to its place of honour.

Hector Gaunt, hands now empty, rose to face Desmond. "If you'd played openly, you might have kept the academy in trust. Now it's only a question of what's left to salvage." He turned away, the words half apology, half warning.

Bertram Vance, unwilling to abandon the field, set the sherry glass precisely on a coaster, aligning it with the edge of the table. "Well, that's a fine mess." He offered the line with a flicker of a smile, the old bitterness muted, and Neville responded with a shrug, neither assent nor contradiction.

Desmond Kestrel regarded each man in turn, then reached for his attache case. With the calm of ritual, he passed the folder of legal documents to Montague Norbury, his hand steady and voice unshaken: "For your review, Norbury." Montague accepted the transfer, nodding once as he placed the folder with the others, their edges sharp and orderly in the failing light.

A silent figure in black entered to remove the last vestiges of the day's drama: empty glasses, a stray glove, the remains of a game unfinished. Katherine Quayle's body, now absent, returned only as memory—a presence felt in the alignment of the silver on the sideboard, in the hush that came whenever her name was spoken, and in the way the sea beyond the glass resumed its indifferent rhythm.

For a long moment, no one spoke. Montague Norbury's hands, faintly trembling, folded over the small diary he carried everywhere—record of fact and folly alike. The sun dipped low, its last beams catching the dust in the air, and the room's shadows stretched across the carpet, enfolding the men and their secrets as the tide covered the cave below.

A bell sounded faintly in the corridor, the signal for tea. Neville Lachlan reached for his cane and stood, the limp more pronounced now that the business was over. Hector Gaunt straightened his jacket with a jerk and moved to the window, gazing out at a world already recalibrating itself. Bertram Vance pocketed the cards, hands moving with practiced skill—one more trick, one more day.

Montague Norbury, hand on the back of his chair, looked once to the portrait of Katherine Quayle, then to the men around him, and finally to the closed door. "As it was before," he murmured, and the room breathed out the tension that had held it tight for so many hours.

Desmond Kestrel, gathering his things, paused at the sideboard, his fingers gliding over the wood, then left the room, the echo of his footfalls fading into the corridor. Montague's tremor stilled as he closed the diary, and for the first time that day, the hotel lounge felt like a place one might rest, if only until the next tide.

## Chapter 10: The Sea, the School, and the Balance Restored

"Steady as she goes," Montague Norbury said, turning from the window where the evening tide foamed quietly against the rocks below, and settling into the deep armchair nearest the fire. The hotel lounge had resumed its intended purpose: laughter drifted from the dining room, the faint clink of decanters punctuated the hush, and the chairs grouped in twos and threes made islands of privacy on the faded floral rug. Montague's hand came to rest on the arm, his cane balanced across his knees, as he watched the ordinary world take back its own.

Hector Gaunt stood at the sideboard, the decanter in his hand, eyes fixed on the steady measure of amber poured into a single glass. He did not drink, but set the glass square in the middle of the silver tray and placed the stopper with deliberate care beside it. His cufflinks caught the lamplight—a small, ruined crest visible for a moment—before he turned toward the hearth, his mouth a flat line.

Neville Lachlan, cane propped against his leg, shuffled a neat stack of academy announcements, his fingers deft even as the firelight danced over old scars. The low conversation in the lounge swirled around him, unnoticed, as he checked off student names and session times. His pen paused above the list, lips pressed thin, before he tucked the register away and glanced once—almost involuntarily—toward the window, as if expecting some interruption that did not come. Bertram Vance, seated beside him, flicked through a deck of playing cards, the pasteboards whispering between his hands as he sorted them by suit, his eyes never quite meeting Neville's.

"Norbury, do you suppose the sea will keep its secrets now?" Hector asked, his tone clipped, as he placed the decanter back in the rack, the gesture precise as a measured retreat. Montague glanced at him, then at the shifting reflections in the window, where the tide was neither rising nor falling in that uncertain twilight.

"Remains to be seen," Montague replied, his hand resting on the cane as he adjusted the fold of his handkerchief, the initials—faded now—barely visible in the firelight. Across the room, a waiter straightened a vase of daffodils, their yellow heads bobbing with the barest of drafts, and the scent of salt and polish mingled in the air, less intrusive now that suspicion had lifted.

Hector's presence at the window had become a habit since the case's close. He stood, hands folded behind his back, his reflection tall and angular in the glass. The memory of public disgrace was in the set of his shoulders—he did not hover over conversations, but let his hand settle briefly on the back of a chair as he passed, before moving on.

Neville, after a glance at the clock—its hands just past nine—cleared his throat. "Let's hope the academy runs to schedule, now," he said, his tone wry as he tapped the stack of announcements into line. Bertram's reply was soft: "If only the tides did as well." Neville allowed a brief smile, his gaze on the flame, before continuing to align the sheets.

Montague Norbury, having folded the last of Katherine Quayle's correspondence into a slim envelope, pressed his seal to the flap and set it atop the mantel. The gesture was unhurried, the wax cooling quickly beneath his palm as he spoke: "No more hidden keys, at least for a while." Hector, standing nearby, offered only a short, "That would be best." The envelope made a faint crackle as the wax hardened, and Montague slid it into the drawer of the writing desk, the action signaling a closing of matters.

Bertram Vance, who had watched the ritual with a half-smile, spoke at length, his voice pitched for all to hear. "You know, Norbury, in most houses, the worst thing likely to be found in a fencing foil is an overlooked receipt or an unpaid bill, not a key to the family's undoing. I suppose we are all grateful, in our way, that the tide has left at least one or two stones unturned. As for myself, the next time I'm asked to check the windows at low tide, I'll do so with both feet firmly planted on the carpet and my mind on the next round of cards. One must keep to one's strengths, after all." Montague's reply was gentle: "A sound principle."

The routine of the hotel reasserted itself with small, telling gestures. The waiter removed the last of the unused glasses, his hands deft and unhurried, and straightened the silver tray with a soft click against the marble. In the far corner, a lamp was lit and a newspaper folded into a neat rectangle, its headlines now irrelevant. The ordinary business of hospitality and comfort replaced the drama of accusation, and the silence meant for rest returned.

Montague Norbury's own life shifted by a shade: the sealed envelope containing the final evidence was delivered to the local constable, his duty as amateur detective completed at last. He felt lighter, almost, for the first time in months—his health holding steady, the tremor in his hand no worse than it had been before, and the prospect of quiet evenings with a pot of tea and the sea beyond the window more appealing than any fresh scandal.

Hector Gaunt returned to the academy after a discreet meeting with the trustees. Not as master, but as steward: the brass nameplate on his office door newly engraved, his keys exchanged for a smaller ring, and his posture dignified as he collected ledgers from the bursar's desk. He accepted the changes without visible complaint. He spent more hours in the library, his hands busy with sorting old records and repairing ledgers, the work a penance and a restoration both.

Neville Lachlan, as promised, resumed his classes—his voice steadier, his manner less guarded, the loss of Katherine Quayle a wound he tended best by example. He stood before the youngest class again, chalk in hand as he drew the lines of a salute on the board, pausing to correct a timid stance or offer a word of reassurance. If his patience seemed deeper, it was in the way he let a silence settle after a question, or watched the children leave with a quiet nod, his gaze lingering on the empty doorway.

Bertram Vance, with debts called in but not gone, shifted his ambitions: the prospect of inheriting the academy was gone, but he found small satisfaction in the order of maintenance, the turn of a well-oiled lock, the smooth close of a sash. Each day carried less bitterness, and his jokes, though still sharp, grew less cruel as the ordinary returned.

The memory of Katherine Quayle lingered, not in the drama of her death, but in the annual photograph pinned above the piano—a moment captured from a summer picnic, her eyes half-laughing as she poured tea for a guest unseen by the camera. That image, not the one in the portrait, became the one the survivors touched in passing, or paused before when the day's work was done.

As the evening deepened, the place resumed its customary rhythm: the fire banked, the chairs rearranged, the scent of the sea mingling with tobacco and the faint sweetness of beeswax polish. Conversation drifted back to commonplaces—schedules, weather, the slow repair of trust. The tide, indifferent as ever, sounded its retreat and return beyond the window, leaving the stones and the school as it had found them—waiting, enduring, and at peace.
