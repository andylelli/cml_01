## Chapter 1: Corridor Mirror Dust

"Cut to the chase." Harriet Bellamy's voice slipped through the low-lit corridor. She stared at the glimmering brass lamp—a trick of light on the polished oak panels showed her own reflection multiplied in gold. She leaned against the wall, gloved hands folded over her arms, a studied nonchalance in every line of her posture. The hush of a crackling hearth-fire from the drawing-room seemed to anchor the hush that followed.

Ivor Yardley, standing a measured four feet from the heavy door to Cecil Thorne's room, adjusted his cufflinks and allowed himself a glance at the angled mirror flush with the corridor wall. The mirror, set at forty-five degrees, met his inspection with nothing but the pale reflection of painted wainscot and the edge of the banister. He clutched his notepad, fingers pressing hard into the leather, as if the act of writing could ward off the unease flickering in his eyes.

"Ottoline, fetch another sheet," Marguerite Selwyn murmured, her words smooth as cream but edged with impatience. She watched Ottoline Fairweather cross the corridor briskly to a side table, retrieve a linen cloth, and kneel beside the threshold, her practical hands careful as she pressed it to a faint patch of dust.

"Were you here last night?" Ivor's inquiry landed on Adela Halloway, who had just entered with a gloved hand pressed to the pocket of her tailored skirt, notebook already in her grasp.

"No," Adela replied, her voice flat as the hallway runner beneath her feet. She glanced at the dust outline Ottoline revealed, kneeling to inspect the border traced in gray along the mirror's edge. "But whoever was here made a point of leaving nothing but questions."

The air in the corridor was heavy with the scents of polish and smoke from the dying embers. Harriet bent, her eyes following Adela's gesture. A clear dust outline traced the mirror's perimeter, so sharp it could have been mapped with a ruler.

Marguerite's fingers tightened on her pearl necklace as she caught Harriet's eye, the memory of old wounds flickering between them. Harriet straightened, chin lifted in silent challenge. For a moment, the corridor belonged to the past as much as the present.

Adela reached for a folded tape measure from her pocket and held it against the paneling, marking the two feet depth behind where the mirror sat. "The dust has not been disturbed beyond this line. That is too deliberate to be accidental." She ran a fingertip along the undisturbed edge, her touch pausing at a faint set of scratches below the wooden frame.

"Did you hear anything unusual?" Ottoline's question, directed at Marguerite, was soft but edged with something sharper. Marguerite looked away, her hand resting on the balustrade.

"Nothing, except the usual after six o'clock. We keep the corridor locked after dinner." Marguerite's voice came out carefully measured. She turned her watch over in her palm, the gold case catching the light, and then set it down on the console table.

Ivor, careful as always, produced a handkerchief and, using it, picked up the hunting knife lying near the settee. The blade bore slight scratch marks that caught the lamp light, a constellation of minute lines along the steel. He held it out for Adela to see.

"These marks—" Adela began, eyes narrowing as she examined the knife, "—are not recent, but they are specific. Ottoline, would you know if this matches anything from your greenhouse?"

Ottoline's lips pressed together, her gaze fixed on the blade. "I might," she said, not quite committing.

Harriet, too, stooped to look at the knife, her gloved finger not quite touching the hilt. "He always did like to keep things sharp," she muttered, words as much for herself as anyone. Her hands trembled, but she kept them folded tight.

"Do you believe the knife was brought from the kitchen?" Ivor asked Marguerite, who shook her head, the pearls at her throat giving the only answer she allowed.

A sudden movement—Marguerite's hand darting to close the door to Cecil Thorne's room—revealed the anxiety that pulsed beneath her calm. The body, still and formal in death, lay on the bed with a puncture mark over the heart, the torn fabric stained in a spreading pool. Adela watched as Ottoline, expression unreadable, laid the cloth over the wound.

"Does anyone recall what time he was last seen?" Adela's question lingered. The silence that followed was filled only by the tick of the hall clock, loud in the hush of morning.

"Six o'clock." Ottoline's answer was quiet, but certain. She smoothed the cloth across the Colonel's breast, her hand pausing at the edge of a medal still pinned to his dressing gown.

Harriet, glancing at the clock and then at the old man's still form, stepped back to the wall, one hand pressing unconsciously to the place on her wrist where, years ago, she'd worn a circus bangle. There was defiance in her stance, but a longing too—as if the corridor might open to a path she had never been allowed to take.

"I do," Harriet replied, her voice clipped to two syllables. She reached for the edge of the door, but did not close it.

Marguerite, hands clasped tightly, surveyed the gathering with practiced poise and a flicker of an old rivalry. "You seem rather determined to be involved, considering your—circumstances."

Harriet's reply was swift: "I have as much right." Marguerite turned away, fussing with a vase of lilies, their petals bruised by some earlier hand.

Adela, returning to the mirror and its precise outline in dust, held her notebook open, pen poised. "You say the corridor was locked after dinner. Yet the dust tells another story." She straightened, her expression unreadable, and set the notebook back into her case.

The glimmering brass lamp's warm glow played across Harriet's face, softening the sharpness of her features as she stared down the length of the corridor, as if searching for an exit not yet permitted her.

"Has anything like this ever happened here before?" Ottoline's question hung in the air, her hands busy with a discarded cufflink from the hearth-rug.

"No," Adela replied, voice as quiet as the closing of a ledger. The silence that followed belonged to the living, not the dead.

Harriet's gaze lingered on Marguerite, a shared history passing between them in the space of a heartbeat. With deliberate care, she set a battered suitcase—her own, scratched and faded—beside the wall, a silent assertion that she did not intend to leave again.

A draft from the window made the lamp flame shiver. Adela, with a final note written in her careful hand, closed her notebook and watched the dust resettle in the corridor, already anticipating the questions that would follow.

## Chapter 2: Mirror Alcove Contradiction

The firelight flickering on leather spines in the distant library lent a warmth at odds with the frost in the air. The reflection shows only the corridor wall, not what might be behind it." Ottoline's hands trembled slightly as she worked, but she said nothing of it. Harriet watched her, arms crossed, lips pursed as if weighing a question.

"Do you often clean this part of the corridor?" Adela asked, her tone measured. She flicked the edge of the tape measure once more, noting the two feet depth behind the mirror's mounting.

Adela waved her off with a quick shake of her head; the investigation mattered more than the outside world.

Marguerite, after a long pause, said, "Mirrors are for looking, not hiding." She flicked a speck of lint from her skirt, as if to dismiss the entire line of inquiry.

Ottoline, quietly, pressed her palm to the wall beside the mirror, testing for a loose panel. "If there's space, it was meant for more than spiders," she muttered, almost to herself, and then glanced at Adela for confirmation.

Adela's mind ran through the possibilities—if the mirror concealed a passage, it would have allowed passage from one end of the corridor to the victim's door without being seen, explaining the undisturbed dust and the lack of footprints on the visible floorboards. Ottoline set the dusting cloth aside and knelt again, this time peering beneath the mirror's edge. "Not unless they knew how to open it," she replied, and her tone carried the weight of experience.

Marguerite's lips thinned as she turned away, tapping her heel against the baseboard.

At the far end of the corridor, Adela's hand hesitated as she made a final note in her pocketbook, her mind turning over the possible consequences of pursuing the truth. Adela's hand trembled slightly as she paused, her pen hovering over the notebook. She remembered the day she'd left the legal world, the accusations whispered in wood-paneled corridors, and the weight of the forged document in her coat pocket. Even now, a stray glance from Marguerite made her heart lurch, as if the past might be uncovered with a single careless word. She set her jaw, recalling the Colonel's trust and the need to prove, to herself as much as anyone, that she would not walk away this time. Her decision now might finally set right what had gone so wrong.

Ottoline, gathering up the dusting cloth and smoothing the carpet where it had shifted, paused to adjust a brass doorstop with her foot. "We can tidy all we like," she said, "but some things don't want to be swept away." She placed the doorstop back into its groove, the metallic sound echoing off the stone.

"Has anyone here actually measured the depth behind this wall?" Harriet asked, fixing Adela with a stare that invited, or demanded, action.

Adela obliged, running the tape from the skirting to the mirror's edge—two feet, exactly. She wrote the number in her notebook. Harriet caught her eye and gave a brief, knowing nod, her lips pressed together—both of them aware that something was being concealed, though neither said so aloud.

The firelight from the library danced along the corridor's floor, highlighting every uneven board and every secret the morning air had yet to reveal. Harriet's arm brushed against the edge of a velvet curtain as she leaned into the alcove, searching for a sign—any sign—that the secrets of Halloway Manor might finally be drawn into the light.

## Chapter 3: Greenhouse Knife Scratches

"Mind your step; the gravel's loose." Ottoline Fairweather's warning drifted over her shoulder as she led Adela Halloway across the flagstones that separated the manor's south terrace from the greenhouse. She held the door open, the frosted windowpanes blurring the winter afternoon into a wash of white and silver. Damp earth clung to every breath and step; the scent was thick, grounding. Adela entered, careful not to brush the rows of potted seedlings lined along the bench, her gaze as unbending as the cold light.

Inside, every surface bore some sign of Ottoline's order—a trowel hanging from a nail, string coiled with geometric precision, a watering can upended to dry. Adela set the hunting knife on the worktable, its blade naked in the waning sun. The scratched steel caught the light, glinting against the wood, and for a moment it seemed a thing out of place in this haven of green and quiet industry.

"Did you ever see this knife before?" Adela asked, her hands folded over her notebook, the spine pressed flat to the table's scarred edge.

"No," Ottoline replied. She kept her eyes on a cluster of geranium cuttings, pinching away a yellowed leaf before it could invite further scrutiny.

Adela turned the knife, slow and deliberate, letting the blade's marred surface catch each new angle of light. Faint, parallel scratches crossed the blade near the base, some overlaying deeper gouges as if the knife had scraped against something harder than earth. She reached for a magnifying glass, its handle cold and heavy, and held it above the steel.

"Would you say these scratches could be made by a greenhouse tool?" Adela's words came measured, each syllable clipped and direct.

"Possibly." "Possibly." Ottoline pressed the earth atop a nearby pot, her fingertips working the soil with brisk, practiced motions.

A hammer on the workbench drew Adela's attention. She lifted it, examining the head—scored with the same rough pattern as the knife, the grooves matching in both depth and spacing. She set it beside the knife, side by side, and let the comparison speak for itself.

"Looks like a match," Adela observed, her tone flat.

Harriet Bellamy's voice filtered in from the open doorway, sharp and brisk. "If we're looking for who handles sharp things, maybe start with the people who keep things growing here." She stepped inside, boots leaving faint prints on the damp floorboard, her hands jammed deep in her coat pockets.

His voice remained steady, but the accusation in his words was unmistakable.

"Not my doing," Ottoline replied, her voice nearly drowned by the clatter of a watering can as she busied herself with the rosemary.

Marguerite Selwyn appeared at the window, her silhouette blurred by condensation, watching the proceedings with the careful attention of someone who did not intend to be caught unawares. She withdrew, the swish of her skirt audible even through the glass.

Adela reached for a notepad, her pen moving efficiently. She noted each scratch, each correspondence between knife and hammer, the minute residues of soil embedded in the hilt's crevices. "This is consistent with use in this greenhouse," she said quietly, her words more for the record than for the room.

A gust rattled the panes, drawing Ottoline's gaze to the frost-feathered pattern blooming across the glass. "We had a delivery late last night," she said, more to herself than anyone present, "and the railway's been no help—delays, cancellations, parcels all muddled." The unreliability of the winter train service, always a thorn in her side, had left her short-handed and irritable.

Ivor moved to the table, setting down his case with a little more force than required. "Miss Bellamy, perhaps you would care to explain your presence in the kitchen at the relevant hour, given your enthusiasm for—intrusion?"

Harriet's retort was immediate: "I was hungry." She picked up a trowel and tossed it from hand to hand, the metal clinking against her rings.

"Miss Bellamy, your interest in these proceedings is, if you will forgive me, remarkable given your lack of standing in the matter and your evident discomfort among the family, not to mention the unspoken but quite present tension that has persisted since your arrival, which has not gone unnoticed by even the most casual observer," Ivor continued, voice velvet-wrapped but sharp beneath.

"Better honest than polite," Harriet replied, setting the trowel down with a thud. Marguerite, outside, turned away and pulled her coat tighter, a gesture of withdrawal more than warmth.

Adela lifted the knife again, this time aligning the scratches beside a gouge on the wooden table leg. She marked the resemblance in her notes: the pattern was unmistakable, the evidence undeniable. "There is a dust outline, too, matching the edges of the mirror seen yesterday," she mused, recalling the earlier investigation. "It suggests deliberate concealment—someone knew the value of careful work."

Ottoline watched as Adela pocketed her notes, her own hands wiping soil from her palms with a battered rag. There was resignation in the gesture, but not defeat; she glanced once at the window, then at the door, as if measuring her escape.

"I suppose you'll be wanting to look at the study next." Ottoline's voice carried no hesitation as she gestured toward the hall, already half-turned to lead the way.

Adela nodded, gathering her things. "Best to cross every threshold," she replied, stepping into the cold air that rushed through the opened door.

In the study, a jumble of correspondence awaited—unopened letters, wax seals broken and rejoined, a case of gardening shears resting atop a stack of estate ledgers. Ottoline moved to a windowsill, picking up a clay pot and tilting it to check the roots. It was an old habit, soothing in its repetition, and for a moment the world outside—the frost, the murder, the relentless questions—seemed to recede.

Harriet leafed through a pile of seed catalogues, her fingers pausing on a page advertising rare orchids. She did not comment, but her silence was pointed, her eyes meeting Adela's for a brief acknowledgement.

Ottoline met her gaze without flinching. "Greenhouses grow more than flowers," she said, her tone quiet, "but not everything blossoms as it should." She set the pot down and wiped her hands, turning away to tend a row of seedlings.

Ivor, standing by the door, adjusted his case and watched Adela with the cool distance of a man who knew the power of a well-laid record. "This will all be in your report, I presume?" His words sounded polite, but the question pressed sharply.

Adela's reply was simple: "Of course." She tucked the knife and her notes into her bag, the investigation moving on as the light outside began to fade.

Harriet, as she passed Ivor by the threshold, looked back into the greenhouse, her eyes tracing the rows of seedlings. "Funny, isn't it, how something cut down can grow back again, given time." Her words lingered, a final note as the door closed softly behind her.

Ottoline, alone now, gathered her tools, arranging each with care. She paused over a seed packet labeled "Sweet Peas—Spring Planting," running her fingers along the edge. The world beyond the frosted panes waited, as did the next question, the next answer, and the next step in a pattern only just begun.

## Chapter 4: Wall Clock Discrepancy

"Duty first, always." Cecil Thorne's words cut through the hush of the study like a blade through silk. He stood with military rigidity beside the broad mahogany desk, its surface littered with estate ledgers and a silver inkstand. The evening gloom pressed at the windows, and the only light came from a standard lamp beside the velvet curtain, making the folds seem bottomless. Ottoline Fairweather, standing three paces away in her plain housekeeper's black, held a battered ledger in both hands, its spine worn from decades of use.

"I did only what was necessary," Ottoline said, her voice tight, her grip on the ledger white-knuckled. She met the Colonel's gaze and did not flinch. "You punished more than the thief, sir."

"Discipline is its own reward," Cecil replied, his jaw set. He turned from her, locking a drawer with a brass key and pocketing it, the movement final as a closing gate. Ottoline watched the motion—her own eyes flickering with the memory of that summer years ago, a friend dismissed, a trust broken. Outside, muffled footsteps on thick carpet signaled the approach of another evening's duty, and Ottoline's hand lingered on the ledger, thumb pressed to a name on the list, before she turned and left the room.

At a different hour, the library door was closed, and Ivor Yardley stood beside the billiards table, legal folio in hand. Cecil Thorne, coat exchanged for a loose cardigan, paced the patterned rug with a purposeful stride.

"You have consistently ignored my recommendations regarding Harriet's interest," Ivor's words sounded raw, the restrained bitterness barely veiled by formality. He set the folio down, fingers splayed across its edge as if ready to defend or destroy it. "It would have been simpler if you'd—trusted my judgment."

"I trust results, not intentions," Cecil replied, pausing to chalk his cue, the action a deliberate dismissal. "We do what is required to keep the estate intact." He lined up a shot he did not take, waiting for Ivor to push the matter further.

"You cannot keep order by omission," Ivor said, voice brittle, the sunlight on the green baize harsh and unforgiving. He retrieved the folio, tucking it beneath one arm, and Cecil, unmoved, returned to his silent calculations, the moment unresolved between them.

The present overtook the past as dusk settled over Halloway Manor. In the main hall, Adela Halloway stood beneath the ornate wall clock, her gaze fixed on the dial. The hands pointed to quarter to seven, the old mechanism ticking with the steady stubbornness of tradition. At her side, Harriet Bellamy fingered the strap of her travel bag, restless, her eyes never straying far from the grand staircase.

"Look at this." Adela gestured towards the clock, then produced the victim's wristwatch, its leather strap weathered, the crystal cracked. "The Colonel's watch stopped at quarter past seven. The clock here reads quarter to seven. That's a difference of thirty minutes—significant, wouldn't you say?"

"Depends who set it," Harriet replied, her tone blunt as she tossed the bag onto the marble-topped console. The gesture rattled a china bowl, drawing a sharp look from Marguerite Selwyn, who stood at the foot of the stairs, smoothing invisible wrinkles from her silk blouse.

"Why would the manor clock be wrong?" Ivor asked, his voice measured as he approached with a sheaf of notes, the paper rustling beneath his thumb. He hovered near the velvet curtain, one hand flicking the fabric back and forth, lost in thought.

"Accident or intent?" Adela replied, her focus never wavering from the clock face. She carefully set the wristwatch beside the ledger, watching as the second hand, dislodged by some impact, pointed unwaveringly to its impossible time.

Marguerite said, "He always wound both himself." She tapped the banister rail, the habit so ingrained it seemed almost involuntary.

Adela's gaze traveled from the wall clock to the watch, measuring the gap between quarter to seven and quarter past seven. "The time of death depends on which is right. One of these clocks was altered."

"Who had access?" Harriet's question was direct, her fingers drumming a restless tattoo on the hall table.

Ivor, voice low and courteous, replied, "Anyone with an interest in the will." The words dropped into the silence with all the subtlety of a closing safe.

Marguerite, after a moment's calculation, asked, "You're suggesting someone set the clock forward—" Her voice trailed off, the rest left to be filled by the room's hush.

Adela met her eyes. "The difference between quarter to seven and quarter past seven could mean the difference between guilt and innocence." She set down her notepad, the pencil rolling until it nudged the china bowl.

A sharp rap of footsteps drew attention as Ivor crossed to the hearth, picking up a poker and turning it in his hand. "You suppose Ottoline might have adjusted it? She is often about in the evening—sweeping, setting fires, tending to those little things none of us notice until they're left undone."

"No." Adela's answer was simple, and the discussion moved on.

Marguerite, her voice betraying nothing, drew close to Adela and asked, "What are you implying?"

"Only that time is slippery," Adela replied, "and someone here has taken advantage of it."

Ivor, voice crisp after Marguerite's slow and circuitous reasoning—"The estate has always observed strict routines, the clocks wound every evening, yet we find them conflicting on this most unfortunate night, which does rather strain the boundaries of coincidence, don't you think?"—replied, "Coincidences cluster around trouble." Marguerite reached for a handkerchief, dabbing at her temples, clearly unsettled by his brevity.

Harriet wandered to the threshold of the victim's room, her eyes falling on the hunting knife, which lay in its evidence bag on a side table. "That knife—does it really matter where it came from if the clocks don't agree?" She turned the bag over, watching the steel glint in the last of the light.

Adela lifted the knife, turning the evidence over in her gloved hands. "The scratch marks here are consistent with those found in Ottoline's greenhouse. It's not proof, but it is a path."

Ivor's hand hovered over the billiards cue rack, indecision painting his features. He reached for one, then paused, considering the consequences of action versus restraint, the thin line between duty and ambition. Ivor weighed memory against ambition, recalling the old arguments with Marguerite as he hesitated by the cue rack.

Marguerite, ever watchful, caught Adela's eye. "We spoke of such things before, you and I. Some matters refuse to stay buried." She smoothed her skirt, her movements precise, as if every crease could be ironed flat with enough will.

"Quite so." Ivor's reply was soft, his glance at the window betraying more than he would say aloud. He set the cue back, unreadable.

Adela looked from the hunting knife to the hall clock, her thoughts lining up with the evidence at hand. "The clock reads quarter to seven. The watch reads quarter past seven. Only one matches the body's state. The timing is the key."

The absence of any digital clocks or mobile telephones—modern conveniences that might have made such discrepancies impossible—was keenly felt in that moment, a relic of tradition now serving the cause of confusion. Marguerite, searching her bag, withdrew a slim volume of poetry, her thumb running absently along the gilt edge as if the answer might be found in verse.

Ivor turned to the window, looking out at the falling dusk, the last embers of sunlight lost behind the walled garden. He kept his hands clasped behind his back, a posture of waiting—for the next accusation, the next revelation.

Harriet, restless, paced the width of the hall, her boots scuffing the edge of the rug. "Are you suggesting Ottoline did this?" she asked, her voice sharp.

Adela set the knife down, folding her hands atop her notebook. "I'm suggesting that whoever understood the clocks understood more of this house than most. Manipulation of time requires access and intent."

Marguerite glanced at the velvet curtain, one finger tracing a fold, her thoughts hidden. "You always did have a taste for melodrama, Miss Halloway," she said, her words as cool as the marble at her back.

Adela said nothing. The tolling of the manor clock sounded more ominous than before.

## Chapter 5: Floor Misalignment

"It's a wonder these boards haven't given up entirely." Harriet Bellamy's voice, pitched low and dry as the November wind outside, fell across the corridor as she crouched near the skirting by the angled mirror, fingertips grazing the worn grain. The corridor was caught in the blue shadow of dusk, fine dust rising as Ottoline Fairweather swept a thick-bristled brush in broad strokes near Adela Halloway's feet. Each soft footfall upon the thick rugs echoed with the restraint of the house; the only other sound was the faint clatter of fire irons from a room beyond, muffled further by distance and heavy oak doors.

Adela knelt, squaring her notebook on the floor as she pressed a slim steel ruler against the gap beside the mirror. "If you look here," she said, "the floorboards are slightly out of line. They dip, then rise again, close to the frame." Her gloved hand traced the edge, pausing at a fine seam where the floorboard's varnish caught the last light. "There's disturbance here, recent enough for the alignment to matter."

Marguerite Selwyn, posture taut as ever, peered over Adela's shoulder. "Perhaps the house is settling. These old places are never quite still." Her words were clipped, but she knelt beside a patch of rug, smoothing it with one palm before straightening, the pearls at her throat shining dully. "Is it necessary to scrutinise every creak and misfit?"

"It is if it moves," Adela replied, her tone even, standing and brushing dust from her skirt. She watched Ottoline, whose own hands worked the brush steadily, bracing herself with one foot against the baseboard for leverage. Ottoline's brush moved in steady, deliberate strokes, her grip firm and her gaze focused. Each sweep left the dust in neat lines, and her hands paused every so often to examine the bristles, checking for stray threads or hidden debris. She glanced over her shoulder once, ensuring no one watched too closely, before returning to her task.

A faint, abrupt sound—a muffled scraping, low and metallic—rose from behind the mirror, just beyond Adela's reach. She stiffened, one hand pressing instinctively to her notebook as she held her breath, mind racing through the possibilities. ## Chapter 6: Kitchen Glove Smudge

"Does this look like murder to you?" Adela Halloway held the hunting knife in her gloved hand, the blade catching an orange slant of gaslight from above the kitchen range. Across the battered wooden table, Harriet Bellamy watched with folded arms, her fingers drumming a staccato rhythm that seemed more challenge than nerves. In the kitchen, the day's last coals glowed sullen behind the cast-iron grate, their warmth fought by the cold stone underfoot, while the faint tang of wood smoke crept beneath the door and mingled with the scent of lemons and onions left for morning.

Adela did not look up, instead setting the knife carefully down upon its cloth.

The kitchen clock chimed the half hour, its battered brass hands pointing to times already in question.

He brushed a speck from his sleeve, awaiting contradiction.

"Not my window, not my knife," Harriet replied, her voice a flat line, eyes on the table. Ivor moved to the sideboard, examining the decanter of sherry with idle distaste.

Adela laid the hunting knife flat upon a clean white cloth, its blade a silent witness. She studied the slight scratches along the hilt, recalling the greenhouse tools from earlier, and weighed the evidence as she pressed her thumb against the smudged leather of Harriet's glove. See here—the mark is smudged, and the way it stretches along the hilt shows it was made slowly, not in a hurry or with force."

Adela's answer was a silent shake of the head as she placed the knife back in its evidence envelope.

"Let's address the timeline," Adela continued. "Harriet was in the kitchen; Ivor was observed in the stables; Marguerite was seen in the walled garden. The dust along the corridor is smooth except for faint scratches—Ottoline's boot makes them, and there's her heel print by the door."

Ottoline folded the last of the towels, her hands smoothing each edge with habitual care, before she moved to the corridor, pausing to check that the mirror's glass was clear. "I don't like shadows, Miss Halloway," she said. "They make housework twice as long and the nights twice as cold." She turned back to the kitchen, her face impassive.

Ivor, hearing Marguerite's elaborate account—"With all due respect to the household's routines, I cannot see how the presence of the staff in their proper places and the adherence to the established order of the evening can leave any room for doubt, particularly since I myself confirmed the stables were locked and the horses fed at precisely the hour in question, as is my custom whenever I am at the manor, which is, I might add, often enough to be considered a reliable observer in such matters"—replied, "Stables were locked; I was there." Marguerite turned her gaze to the window, her lips pressed thin.

"Evidence from the corridor, the mirror, and the dust outline points elsewhere," Adela said, her tone as direct as ever. She brushed a hand over the evidence bag, her eyes narrowed in calculation. "Specific scratch marks and dust disturbance in the corridor match Ottoline's known patterns. The mechanism for the mirror is known only to her and those who maintain it."

The kitchen lamp flickered, the old wiring straining against another winter. Harriet, restless, crossed to the window, the cold breath of stone sill against her palm. In the faint reflection, her face was set, her jaw tight with something old and unspoken, the same resolve that once propelled her from home at fifteen, suitcase in hand and future unwritten.

Adela set her notebook down, flipping through the last few pages. "We have evidence of blackmail correspondence between Cecil and Ottoline. The means—the passage behind the mirror, the knowledge of its working—leads back to her. No one else had both opportunity and access."

Ottoline moved to the hearth, steadying herself on the iron poker. She watched Adela with an unreadable stillness, her lips pressed together. "A house keeps its secrets," she said, "but sometimes, secrets come out in the wash." Her hands, busy with the poker, trembled only slightly.

Social expectation pressed in here, unspoken but powerful, limiting Ottoline's movements and dictating who could pass freely through doorways and who would always be seen as trespasser. She set the poker down, her back straightening.

Harriet returned to the table, lifting her cup for a last cooling sip. "You're saying the only one left is Ottoline. Is that the line?" Her voice was frank, her gaze unwavering.

Adela nodded, her tone remaining measured. "The facts, as they stand, do not accuse you, Harriet. You were here, Ivor in the stables, Marguerite in the garden. The evidence supports every word. The passage, the mirror, the dust: they tell a different story, and we follow them where they lead."

Outside the kitchen's warmth, the corridor lamp cast a thin gold on the floorboards. Adela passed into the shadow, her mind racing through every detail, every question answered and unanswered. Behind her, Harriet gathered her coat, her movements deliberate, the battered suitcase at her feet a quiet promise: she had left once, and returned to find the truth this time—on her own terms, and no other's.

## Chapter 7: Suspect Alibis Confirmed

"Name the hour again." Adela Halloway's voice was crisp in the night air as she led the way over the crisp crunch of gravel underfoot, every step ringing clear between the frost-glittered hedges. She paused at the old iron gate to the stables, her notebook tucked into her gloved palm and her gaze steady on Ivor Yardley.

"Seven o'clock to eight o'clock," Ivor replied, referencing the alibi established the previous night. He adjusted his scarf with two fingers, the motion neat as the rest of him, and surveyed the stable doors, which gleamed faintly in the lantern-light. The horses inside shifted, their movement a muted reassurance.

Adela turned to the stable hand, a solid fellow with straw stuck to his boots. "You saw Mr. Yardley here?"

"Yes, miss. He checked the feed room and counted the tack. Didn't leave until after the church bell struck eight o'clock." The stable hand's voice was low but certain, his hands busy gathering a bucket, the handle clanging against the side as he set it down at Ivor's feet.

Harriet Bellamy's voice called from behind them, where she stood with her arms folded against the cold. "If the horses could talk, we'd be done by now." She handed the stable hand a carrot, her mouth twitching in a brief, sardonic smile.

Adela wrote in her notebook, her pencil moving with deliberate slowness. "Multiple witnesses. No possible interval unaccounted for." She closed the book with a snap and looked up at Ivor. "You're cleared."

Ivor let out a breath—whether of relief or annoyance, it was hard to say—and adjusted his gloves, his hands smoothing imaginary creases from the leather. "I expected as much." He turned his attention to the frost on the stable window, tracing its edge with the tip of his cane.

The crunch of boots on gravel marked Marguerite Selwyn's arrival as she emerged from the shadow of the yew walk, her coat swishing around her ankles, a gloved hand trailing lightly along the frost-glittered hedge. She glanced toward the walled garden, where the dark mass of earth and brick met the moonlight.

"Miss Selwyn, a moment," Adela called, approaching her with notebook poised. "Your location between a quarter past six and eight o'clock?"

"In the walled garden. I took the long path after supper—needed air. The gardener saw me; so did the kitchen maid crossing with the waste." Marguerite's tone was smooth, but her eyes flickered sideways to Harriet, who watched with open curiosity.

"Most evenings," Marguerite replied. She hesitated, then added, "It calms me." Her hands, though, clutched her bag a shade too tightly, knuckles pale.

Adela's pen scratched along the page, the sound sharp in the stillness. Adela recalled Marguerite Selwyn's whereabouts: the garden route, as confirmed the night before. Cleared by two witnesses." She looked up, her gaze lingering on Marguerite's shuttered expression before moving on.

Marguerite eyed Harriet, then Adela, the corners of her mouth barely rising. "Quite so." She turned away, her shoes crunching the gravel as she disappeared into the shadow between house and wall.

Cecil Thorne's absence loomed, yet for the formality of it, Adela checked her notes, marking the times the Colonel was last accounted for—every sighting pointing to the dinner hour, nothing beyond. His own routines were as fixed as the clocks he wound, and the flicker of memory from a housemaid—last seeing him by the mantel at a quarter past six—carried the ring of habit.

Adela paused beside a cold stone bench, her breath visible in the lamplight. "Cecil Thorne's routine, confirmed by staff—last seen alive at the appointed hour. Accounted for until the moment in question." She glanced at Harriet, who leaned against the bench, her eyes narrowed.

The tensions of inheritance and kinship pressed at her, shaping every movement.

Marguerite's face tightened, but she answered only, "Perhaps." With a flick of her wrist, she slid the letter back into her pocket and turned toward the house.

A stray tabby cat darted across the path, pausing to brush against Ivor's shoes. He stooped to touch its head, his hand lingering for a heartbeat longer than might be expected, then straightened with a composure that masked any sentiment.

The rule of the place pressed in—gates locked at dusk, family wandering only in permitted spaces, staff keeping to shadow and edge. Marguerite's passage through the walled garden had been sanctioned by time and tradition; even her rebellion was contained within the order of things.

Inside the kitchen, the glow from the range threw long shadows across scrubbed boards. Adela stood by the chopping block, the hunting knife resting safely in its envelope now. Adela pointed to Harriet Bellamy's glove: the smudge, she said, matched earlier kitchen work—the same fact noted yesterday. Harriet Bellamy stood by the stove during the critical hour, as confirmed by staff testimony." She tucked the knife away, her hands steady.

Adela glanced at her, lips pressed together in a fleeting smile. "It's the principle."

Marguerite hesitated at the doorway, glancing back once—her ambition and her need for belonging warring in the set of her shoulders. She caught Adela's eye, then said, "The estate is all I have." Her voice was low, but steady.

Adela returned to the entrance hall, her notes gathered, her mind sifting through names and alibis, the light from the staircase throwing her shadow long across the parquet. In the hush between questions, the truth moved closer, step by deliberate step.

Ivor lingered at the stable door, his eyes fixed on the distant lights of the village. In a motion almost too small to see, he pressed a folded letter flat inside his coat, the act meant for no one's notice. Whatever disappointment had marked his career, tonight it was folded away with the rest.

Cecil Thorne, in memory, stood by the study fire—his discipline and distance still ordering the house. The ledger he'd last handled now rested on the side table, untouched but for the notes Adela had added.

Behind closed doors, Harriet Bellamy paced her bedroom, Marguerite Selwyn sat rigid by her window, and Ivor Yardley stared at the ledger on his desk; outside, frost sharpened on every blade of grass, the estate looming above their heads. The facts were clear. The circle had narrowed to its final line.

## Chapter 8: The Passage of Shadows

"Let us assemble here." Adela Halloway's calm directive echoed along the corridor outside Cecil Thorne's room, where the wall lamps cast narrow golden shafts that failed to reach the shadowed corners behind bookshelves. The hush of early night pressed close, disturbed only by the quiet ticking of a brass clock somewhere past the bend. Marguerite Selwyn stood poised at the corridor's heart, her hands smoothing invisible creases from her skirt, her chin raised in practiced defiance. Ivor Yardley hovered nearby, legal case clutched close as a shield, his gaze fixed on the mirror's vacant reflection.

"Must we stand for this?" Marguerite's impatience shimmered, her voice thin against the hush as she drew a lace handkerchief across her palm, the gesture as empty as her smile.

"Yes," Ivor replied, voice clipped. He adjusted his spectacles with a surgeon's precision and waited.

Ottoline Fairweather, summoned at Adela's request, stood with her hands folded around a battered set of keys. The lamplight caught the silver at her temples, her expression one of weathered endurance. Harriet Bellamy lingered at the edge, arms crossed, one boot tapping a measured beat against the floor.

She let her hand rest flat, drawing attention to the clean break in the dust, a path cut by more than housekeeping.

"Why test the staff at all?" Marguerite's words were barbed, but her shoulders stiffened as Adela's gaze lingered.

"My name, my place—Halloway Manor," Ottoline replied, the shame and pride wound tight together.

All around, the others shifted—Marguerite smoothing her dress with trembling hands, Ivor straightening his case, Harriet folding her arms in a posture equal parts defense and relief.

No one moved to leave. The corridor, lit and chill, held them in its grip a moment longer—secrets spent, judgment hanging in the hush before the ordinary world could begin again.

## Chapter 9: The Drawing Room Reckoning

Adela Halloway's voice, calm and unwavering, carried through the drawing room, where shadows lay long across the Persian rug and smoke tendrils curled above the hearth as the night pressed on. She stood by the marble-topped sideboard, her hand flat on a ledger, eyes steady on those gathered. Even now, I am not quite sure who I am supposed to be angry with—the Colonel, Ottoline, or myself." She fiddled with her bracelet, each link cold and bright under the lamplight.

Harriet's reply, to Marguerite's exhaustive reflection—"It's just that when you consider everything, the letters, the inheritance, the way we were all circling each other these past few days, it's almost as if the house itself demanded that one of us bear the weight, and now that Ottoline has, I almost feel sorry, but then again, maybe not, given what she did and why she did it,"—was simply: "Truth's its own punishment." Marguerite bit her lip, eyes fixed on the dying fire.

Adela closed the ledger, setting it atop a neat stack of correspondence awaiting the morning post. "Harriet Bellamy is cleared," she said, her voice quiet but sure. Harriet leaned back, her hands tucked behind her head, letting the tension drain from her shoulders.

Marguerite, smoothing the velvet cushion beside her, glanced at Adela. "And me?" Her tone was almost hopeful, but guarded.

"Marguerite Selwyn is cleared," Adela replied. Marguerite's breath left her in a shudder, and she laid her hands flat against her knees, fingers splaying in silent gratitude.

Harriet watched Marguerite, then Ivor. "You're still keeping the family together, then?"

"I suppose I must," Marguerite said, her voice thin. She reached for a letter opener and ran her thumb along its edge, absorbing the solidity of the metal.

The tick of the mantel clock seemed louder now, each second marking the slow return to order. Ivor moved to the sideboard, selecting a decanter and pouring a measure of sherry for Adela, then for himself. He raised his glass, a silent acknowledgment, and Adela accepted the gesture with a nod.

"Do you want to stay?" Marguerite asked, her eyes fixed on Harriet.

"Not sure yet," Harriet replied, her voice as blunt as the poker resting in the hearth.

A draft from the corridor stirred the smoke above the hearth, sending it curling into new shapes. Marguerite gathered her shawl tightly around her shoulders, her eyes reflecting both relief and uncertainty.

Adela drew a stack of receipts from her bag, smoothing each slip flat with careful fingers. The habit—old, persistent, born of a need for order and proof—reminded her of a different life, one where redemption was measured not in confessions, but in ledgers balanced and accounts settled.

The distant sound of voices—servants, freed at last from the strain of secrecy—brought the room's attention to the reality waiting beyond its walls. Marguerite stood, smoothing her skirt, her hand lingering on the chair back as if hesitant to let go. Harriet, meanwhile, stretched and stood, hands tucked in her coat pockets, eyes darting once to the window and the world outside.

Ivor, glass in hand, joined Adela at the fire. "I believe you have restored more than order, Miss Halloway," he said, his tone carrying both acknowledgment and a hint of envy. Adela cracked a rare smile, subdued but genuine.

The drawing room, hushed now but for the occasional snap from the hearth, stood as witness to the night's reckonings. Each person, cleared and changed, lingered over the small rituals of the hour—letters handled, glasses raised, cushions smoothed, and, above all, the slow, deliberate return to the ordinary business of life.

## Chapter 10: Night Restored

"Shall I relight the fire?" Ottoline Fairweather's question, soft but clear, cut the hush in the drawing room as she reached for the worn brass poker on the hearth. The embers glowed sullen in the grate, their warmth fading; outside, the wind pressed at the windowpanes but did not enter. A police cap, forgotten, rested on the side table beside Cecil Thorne's battered pipe, its bowl still faintly scented with last week's tobacco. The formality of proof and process had receded, replaced by the slow return of household order: Ottoline knelt, prodding the coals with steady hands, her movements careful and practiced.

"No need. It's late," Adela Halloway replied, her tone as measured as always as she gathered the last of her notes. The worn leather of her notebook felt lighter now, the pressure of secrecy finally eased. She moved to the desk where letters—halted for days by the investigation—lay in a neat stack, her hand lingering before she selected one marked for the estate's solicitor. With deliberate precision, she smoothed the envelope, addressed it anew, and set it in the outbox for morning collection.

"Did you ever think it would end like this?" Harriet Bellamy's voice was low but direct as she leaned against the window frame, the velvet curtain cool beneath her palm. The battered suitcase she had kept by her side for days sat open at her feet, half-packed but untouched. She glanced down at Cecil Thorne's old service revolver, now locked away in a drawer, and her features softened for a moment—the shadow of loss mingling with relief.

"It had to end some way." She closed her notebook and set it on the mantel, beside a photograph of the Colonel in uniform, the silver frame polished to a dull gleam. She watched Harriet kneel to repack the suitcase, the motion practiced from years on the road, and felt the weight of her own departure from the life of a solicitor's assistant pressing at the edges of memory—an old scandal not spoken of, but quietly shaping every decision.

Marguerite Selwyn crossed to the sideboard, her posture more composed than before but her hands restless as she reached for the letter opener. She examined the blade, then set it aside and pulled from the drawer a pale blue notecard. She wrote a brief line, sealed the card, and placed it atop the morning's correspondence. Her glance met Adela's for a moment, something close to gratitude in her eyes.

Ivor Yardley, standing by the window with his case at his feet, looked out into the darkness where the stables lights shone faintly. He cleared his throat. "Will you remain, Miss Bellamy?" Ivor's words seemed to linger as he waited, glancing between Harriet and the rest of the room.

"Time will tell," Harriet replied, her voice soft. She snapped the suitcase shut and straightened, running her thumb along the battered handle, the leather worn smooth by years of travel. Her eyes flicked to Marguerite, who lifted her chin in acknowledgment, then to Adela, who was gathering up the scattered receipts from the desk.

Ottoline straightened, brushing the soot from her skirt. "No use crying over coals," she said, her mouth twisting in a wry smile as she set the poker back and reached for the old logbook that hung by the hearth. She wrote the hour in the margin—an old habit, marking the day's work done—then closed it with a snap.

"Miss Halloway, what will you do now?" Marguerite's question was tentative, her hand resting on the back of a velvet chair. The firelight gleamed on her signet ring, the metal warmed by her touch.

"Begin again," Adela replied, her words a quiet certainty. She moved to the window, checked that it was latched, and let the curtain fall behind her, obscuring the night.

Harriet, catching the end of Ottoline's earlier quip—"You know, I've always thought the house would collapse if you ever left, and I suppose now we'll see what all those years of keeping us in line were really worth, especially since you've managed to do what none of us dared"—smiled faintly. "I suppose you warned us." Ottoline's laugh, low and brief, carried a note of resignation as she collected the discarded fire tongs, her hands steady despite all that had changed.

Ivor's reply to a servant's hushed question—"Sir, shall I bring the car out now, or will you and Miss Selwyn go in the morning? The road is clear, though the frost is coming down fast and it might be slippery, but if you prefer to wait I can have everything ready at first light, with rugs and the thermos in the back seat as usual"—was only, "We'll wait for morning." The servant nodded, reaching for the keys on the entry table.

Marguerite lifted Cecil Thorne's old signet ring from beside the ledger, turning it over in her hand. She remembered, unsummoned, the Colonel at the breakfast table—reading telegraphs, pouring tea, his hand steady as he signed the day's orders. She set the ring down, careful not to let it roll.

Ottoline moved to the door, pausing there with her hand on the knob. She looked back, her eyes sweeping the familiar curves of the wainscoting and the pattern of light across the rug. "One last sweep before bed," she said, half to herself, and disappeared into the shadowed corridor.

In the sudden quiet, Marguerite resumed her seat, smoothing her skirt and pulling the correspondence basket closer. She selected a letter and began to read, eyes moving line by line, her concentration returning to the mundane, the ordinary tasks that defined her world. Ivor, meanwhile, set out a sheaf of estate documents on the writing desk, aligning each page with the edge—a ritual as precise as it was calming.

Adela crossed the room to the fireside, picking up the battered pipe that had once belonged to Cecil Thorne. She held it for a moment, recalling the Colonel's dry, clipped remarks as he filled it in the hush before dinner, the scent of tobacco mingling with the polish of old wood. She set the pipe gently in a drawer, closing it with care.

Harriet stood and hefted her suitcase, moving toward the door. She paused, glancing once more at the assembled company. "Tomorrow's another show," she said, the words unadorned, and slipped into the dim hall beyond.

The mantle clock ticked, unhurried, marking the slow, necessary return to order. The house, briefly unmoored, settled into its own familiar rhythm once more—letters written, fire tended, the work of inheritance and routine resuming on the far side of loss. Adela watched the last of the embers glow, her hand still upon the drawer, as the quiet closed in around them—steady, unbroken, and, for tonight, at peace.
