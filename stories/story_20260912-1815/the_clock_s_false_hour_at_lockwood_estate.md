Percival Thorne's charm came back, though it sat on him now like a coat held up against weather. Ambrose Halloway felt the room narrow around him and take nothing off his shoulders for it. Three names struck out and the mechanism still keeping its own counsel. He stayed by the hearth a while longer with his hand on the oak. Outside the rain kept on and the estate kept its hours; the case had turned, and he would follow it where it went, though he would not be trusting the hour again. The lamp guttered and steadied. The silence that came after was heavier than the one before it.

## Chapter 7: A Glove and a Growing Puzzle

The fire in the clock room had burned down to a flicker, and the rain still worked at the leaded glass. Ambrose Halloway stayed by the hearth with one hand on the smooth oak of the mantel, less for warmth than for something solid to hold. Shadows swelled and shrank along the walls as each gust took the panes. Burnt wood, damp wool-and under it the ticking, patient and regular, which was precisely the trouble. He looked across at the mechanism, its brass gears picking up what light the fire had left. The lamp guttered and steadied. Beyond the window the estate went on keeping its hours; Ambrose had stopped believing in them. "Inspector. You're looking for something." Ottoline Dunmore had come in without a sound. Her bias-cut skirt brushed the rough boards, and the sleeves of her blouse were turned back, wrists pale in the gloom.

The cotton gloves she was seldom without were nowhere on her. Her hands moved instead with a curious care, as though everything in the room might mark them. Ambrose noted the composure and thought how thin it lay, no deeper than varnish. Above the wainscot the canal charts jumped and settled in the firelight. Her eyes went to the clock mechanism, stayed a moment, then came back to him and gave him nothing. He knelt beside the case and ran his fingers along the brass edge. In the narrow gap between the housing and the oak shelf lay a scrap of cotton, frayed along one side, dark with oil, and stitched at the corner with the unmistakable ridge of a glove's seam. Ambrose lifted it out and turned it slowly in the lamplight. It was the same practical stuff as the pair he had seen lying abandoned in the Lock-Keeper's Cottage-the pair she had worn earlier that day.

He held it up and said nothing at all. "You keep the manor clock," Ambrose said at last, evenly. "Few on the estate could. Fewer still would know how to touch the striking train and leave it looking untouched." He let the fragment lie open in his palm, the fire finding its ragged edge. "Routine is a comfortable sort of fiction, Miss Dunmore. Clocks are less obliging." "I keep the estate's timepieces in order." Her voice held, though she folded her hands and the folding was not quite steady. "The canal schedule depends on it. If you're suggesting I altered the manor clock, you'll find my fingerprints on every part of it. I maintain it. That is the routine, and the routine is mine to keep." Her eyes went again to the torn cotton. "And if you mean to accuse me, Inspector-I was at the cottage from four to half past, as I have already told you. The tools were there. Nothing interrupted me."

"I am accusing no one," Ambrose said, the dry London edge coming up in the words. "It is only that the evidence grows less obliging in your company." He set the fragment down on the shelf beside the mechanism, where neither of them could pretend to have mislaid it. "The striking train is out. It sounded the hour at twenty-five minutes past three-the face was showing a quarter to four. a gentleman's pocket watch stopped at ten past three." He let that sit. "Somewhere in there is a gap I cannot fit a routine into." Her composure slipped then, not much, but he saw it go. She was still looking at the scrap of glove. "Sometimes the smallest cog puts out the whole clock," she said, and it did not sound as though it were meant for him. Ambrose watched the flicker in her-a woman worn thin by other people's carelessness, and now standing in the middle of a suspicion she had not arranged.

He was aware of doubt in himself, and aware too that it had come too late to be any use. The firelight moved over the brass gears, over the torn cotton, over both their faces. Rain went on at the windows. Outside, the estate kept its hours as it always had; inside, no one in the room would be trusting them again until they knew who had taught the clock to lie.

## Chapter 8: The Timed Clock Test

It was the gray dawn, cold and damp, that found Ambrose Halloway still in the Manor clock room, the fire reduced to a thin line of embers and the air thick with the odor of burnt wood and oil. The rain had slackened but not ceased; its persistent patter against the window mingled with the soft creak of wooden beams overhead. He stood before the brass mechanism, the scrap of cotton glove from the night before lying beside the clockmaker's report, and let the silence stretch. Ottoline Dunmore entered quietly, her bias-cut skirt brushing the rough boards, the sleeves of her blouse turned back as though she expected her hands to be needed. She paused, eyes on the clock, and said nothing. "You see where we are," Ambrose Halloway began, his voice measured, the faint London in his vowels pressing through the hush. "Routine is a comforting fiction. The clock's truth is less accommodating."

He gestured to the torn glove, then to the clockmaker's report-its pages detailing the timed sequence of the manor clock's striking train, the victim's pocket watch, and the lock-keeper's cottage clock. The flat light from the canal filtered through the dusty glass, shadowless and pale, as he laid out the three timekeepers on the oak desk. "Inspector, the estate's routine depends on the clock," Ottoline Dunmore said, her composure brittle. She folded her hands, fingers working against her palm. "If you're suggesting I altered it, you'll find my fingerprints everywhere. I maintain it. That is the routine, and the routine is mine to keep." Her gaze lingered on the glove fragment, then on Ambrose Halloway's face, searching for reassurance and finding none. "We will settle the matter now," Ambrose Halloway said, picking up the victim's pocket watch-its hands frozen at ten minutes past three.

He set it beside the lock-keeper's cottage clock, whose mechanism had never been tampered, and then turned to the manor clock. "The theory is simple: if the manor clock struck at twenty-five minutes past three, but its face showed a quarter to four, then the chime was advanced by twenty minutes. The victim's watch stopped at ten minutes past three. The lock-keeper's cottage clock, untouched, will chime in sequence with the true hour." He wound the manor clock, careful to align the hands precisely as they had been found. The mechanism's detent lever, misaligned but not broken, gave a faint resistance as he set it. The lock-keeper's cottage clock ticked steadily in the background, its gears smooth and unhurried. Ambrose Halloway waited, the air heavy with expectation. Ottoline Dunmore's composure slipped as he counted aloud, the seconds stretching.

"Now," he said, and the manor clock struck-its chime echoing at twenty-five minutes past three. The lock-keeper's cottage clock, by contrast, struck only when the true hour arrived, its sound clear and unforced. The victim's pocket watch, stopped at ten minutes past three, lay between them as a silent witness. "The manor clock's chime was advanced by twenty minutes," Ambrose Halloway stated, his tone dry but unyielding. "The striking train was altered. The face showed a quarter to four, but the chime sounded at twenty-five minutes past three. The victim died at ten minutes past three, as the pocket watch proves. Percival Thorne was in the library only after four o'clock-his alibi stands. Josephine Rutherford was in the kitchen, witnessed by staff. Frances Orme was in the gardens, seen by multiple people.

None could have tampered with the clock or struck the fatal blow in the true window." Ottoline Dunmore's hands trembled as she reached for the glove fragment. "You have the skill, the access, the motive," Ambrose Halloway pressed, his voice soft but relentless. "Your fingerprints are on the mechanism. The logbook entries are yours. The timed sequence proves only you could have advanced the chime, creating a false hour and masking the crime. You wanted order. You wanted authority. In life, You saw your chance slipping when Beatrice Whitlock moved to change the will." "I wanted the estate to run as it should," Ottoline Dunmore said, her voice breaking. "Beatrice Whitlock would have undone everything. I only meant to protect what was mine to keep." She folded her arms, the gesture defensive and weary. The rain outside had faded to a whisper, the canal water lapping gently against the gates.

Ambrose Halloway watched her, vindication mingling with sorrow. The clockmaker's report, the glove, the timed chiming comparison-all lay before them, the truth exposed. Ambrose Halloway drew a line beneath Ottoline Dunmore's name in his notebook. The estate's routines would press on, but the hour they kept would never again be trusted. The manor clock, its mechanism laid bare, stood as silent witness to the deception. The summer dawn grew brighter, but inside the Manor clock room and lock-keeper's cottage, the air remained heavy with the cost of order asserted and truth revealed.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

Percival Thorne set his teacup down with a deliberate clink, and the sound travelled further than it should have in the warm hush of the Manor sitting room. Morning light came through the half-drawn curtains as a pale haze, softening the edges of the canal charts pinned above the wainscot. The room smelled of burnt wood and old tobacco, and beneath that of the damp the rain had left overnight. Ambrose Halloway stood by the hearth with his notebook open. The pencil line beneath Ottoline Dunmore's name was an hour old and still black. The fire had burned down until its glow no longer reached the corners, and nobody in the room was frantic any more; they were simply waiting, pressed close by what had already come out. "You'll want clarity, Inspector," Percival Thorne said. His lightweight wool suit was immaculate; the tie had defeated him again.

The tension had gone out of his jaw and something warier had taken its place. Josephine Rutherford stayed near the kitchen door, her utility dress faded, her cardigan dark at the shoulders from the walk across the estate. Frances Orme perched on the edge of an armchair, tea dress crisp, hat set at a considered angle, one gloved finger travelling the rim of her cup. The summer daylight would stretch on for hours yet. Inside, the household sat quiet, waiting to be told where each of them now stood. Ambrose Halloway turned a page, and the sound of it came sharp over the ticking of the manor clock. "Percival Thorne, your alibi holds," he said, the London in his voice cutting clean through the quiet. "Three witnesses put you in the Manor House Library between four o'clock and half past. Beatrice Whitlock's pocket watch froze at three past midnight past three.

The manor clock chimed at twenty-five minutes past three, and its face showed a quarter to four." He let that sit a moment. "The candlestick was not in your hand." Thorne's shoulders came down. His smile returned, and the charm with it, but it hung on him tiredly now, and did not hide much. "Inspector," he said, more quietly, "I suppose I owe you an apology. I resented being doubted. I see now the hour was never mine to keep." He looked at Ambrose Halloway, and then, deliberately, at Ottoline Dunmore, whose composure had begun to give at the seams. "If you mean to doubt me again, do it where I can hear you." Ambrose Halloway inclined his head and let a dry smile pass. The air had eased. It had not cleared. "Josephine Rutherford," he went on, "the kitchen staff put you among them the whole of the murder window.

Their accounts agree with one another and with your own habits, which are regular to the minute. Nobody in that kitchen could have got at the clock, and nobody in that kitchen went upstairs." Her expression gave back nothing at all, but her fingers slackened on the clasp of her handbag. "That's how the canal runs, isn't it," she said, flatly. "Steady, except when it isn't." Frances Orme's smile lost its edge. There was no relief in Josephine Rutherford that anyone could have named, only a woman who had been suspected and was still standing where she had stood before. "Frances Orme," Ambrose Halloway said, "you were seen in the Formal Gardens, passing the fountain, when the chime sounded. The garden staff are clear on it, and there are several of them. Whoever was in the Manor House Library at that hour, it was not you." Her gloved fingers stopped on the cup.

"Inspector, society is a game, and I have learned the rules rather quickly. I hadn't understood how little it takes to undo a reputation." She glanced at Josephine Rutherford, then at Percival Thorne, and her smile turned honest for the first time that morning. "Thank you for seeing me as more than a player." Ambrose Halloway let the silence run. The manor clock ticked; somewhere beyond the windows a canal gate creaked on its hinge, the sound carrying up from the cut as it had carried up for two hundred years. Three people in that room had begun to breathe again. The fourth had not. Ottoline Dunmore stood by the hearth with her bias-cut skirt brushing the boards and her hands folded so tightly the knuckles showed, and her eyes had not left the clock mechanism on the side table. Her fingerprints were on it.

The tools were hers, kept in the case she had inherited with the estate, and she was the only person in the house who knew how to use them. The last entries in the logbook were in her hand. Before the death, "Miss Dunmore," Ambrose Halloway said, dry and quite unhurried, "Beatrice Whitlock was struck on the head with a heavy bronze candlestick. Her pocket watch stopped at ten minutes past three. The clock chimed at twenty-five minutes past three, and its face read a quarter to four. Someone had been into the striking train and pushed the chime forward by twenty minutes." He turned the notebook a fraction, though he did not look at it. "Your alibi begins at four and ends at half past, at the Lock-Keeper's Cottage, and I have no quarrel with it. The difficulty is that the house was meant to hear four o'clock and believe it, and the house did. She died at ten minutes past three.

Your afternoon had not yet started." Ottoline Dunmore's hands shook as she reached for the glove fragment lying beside the mechanism. She looked up, meeting Ambrose Halloway's steady gaze, and nodded once, a small, resigned gesture that spoke more than words could. The room held its breath, the weight of what had come to pass settling over them all. She closed her fingers on the scrap of leather, the last tangible piece of a moment that could never be undone, and the silence between them was heavy with everything left unsaid.

"And the estate, which you would not give up. It was a neat piece of work, Miss Dunmore. It only wanted the watch to stop somewhere else." He shut the notebook. "Mr Thorne, Miss Rutherford, Miss Orme - you may go. There is nobody left in this room but her." The relief, when it came, was a tired thing. Frances Orme set her cup aside without drinking from it. Josephine Rutherford's shoulders let go by an inch. Percival Thorne's charm came back to him, and wore rather better for having been humbled first. Ottoline Dunmore stood where she was with nothing held together any longer, and the glove fragment slipped out of her hand to the boards. Outside, the summer morning brightened over the gardens and the cut beyond them. In the sitting room the light only showed what the price of order had been.

"Take her," Ambrose Halloway said quietly, and the silence that followed was not peace, but it was the beginning of something like it.

## Chapter 10: The Price of Order

"She's gone," Percival Thorne said, and the words sat in the heavy afternoon air of the drawing room without anyone taking them up. The rain had eased. The damp had not; it lay in the carpet, and under the summer light coming through the half-drawn curtains the smell of burnt wood held on. Ambrose Halloway had expected the quiet afterwards to feel clean. It did not. Ottoline Dunmore's confession was signed, her place in the house stood empty, and the silence had the closeness of a room in which everybody knows the same thing. Halloway stood by the hearth with his notebook shut, the fire gone down to an ember or two. Above the mantel the manor clock ticked on, hands set true now, striking train restored, and he found he did not like the sound as much as he once had.

Josephine Rutherford came across the room in her faded utility dress, the shoulders of her cardigan still dark from the morning's rain. She stopped at the oak desk and ran her fingers along the edge of it, the way a person touches a thing to be sure it is still where it was. "It's a strange thing," she said, and her voice held level, "to watch the order of a place come apart, and then be asked to stitch it back." Frances Orme had not left the window. Her tea dress was crisp, her hat set at its usual exact angle. She looked at the canal charts pinned above the wainscot, then at Halloway, and the smile she gave him had less starch in it than before. "Society is a game, Inspector." The edge was there, but blunted. "The rules change when the players do. I dare say I shall have to learn them all over again." She turned the rim of her cup in her gloved fingers.

Halloway waited, thinking something else might follow it, but nothing did. The estate began to take itself back in small ways. Josephine's footsteps went off down the corridor towards the kitchen. Percival squared the stack of estate papers on the oak desk, his lightweight wool suit immaculate, his tie at last sitting straight. He glanced up with that sardonic flicker of his. "Well, Inspector. You've restored the hour, if not the harmony. In a house like this I suppose that's the most one can hope for." "Harmony is overrated," Halloway said. "It generally requires a good deal of lying." Percival folded a letter and laid it aside, slowly, as though the placing of it mattered. The routines would go on; they always did.

But nobody in the house would hear the clock strike again without a half-second of doubt, and Halloway, who had brought that doubt into the room himself, stood listening to the ember shift in the grate and thought the price had been steeper than he wished to say aloud. Beatrice Whitlock was in the room still - not as evidence, only as a presence. Halloway remembered how she had insisted on fresh lilies for the library, even when rationing made them next to impossible to come by. Josephine had stopped in the doorway, and her eyes had found the empty vase beside the hearth. "She had a way of making you feel your routine mattered," she said, low. "Even when she was refusing compensation, she'd ask after the canal. As if it were the heart of the place." Frances went to the mantel and brushed the smooth oak with two fingers.

"I never cared for her taste in music," she said, and the polite savagery came back a little. "Vera Lynn, every afternoon, as though hope could be wound up on a gramophone." She smiled; it was a tired thing, and Halloway read in it what a reputation costs when it comes undone. The estate would pick up its routines soon enough. The order Ottoline Dunmore had tried to hold it to would not be coming back with them. Halloway watched the room be reclaimed by ordinary use. Josephine gathered the canal charts and squared them into a stack. Percival opened a window and let the midsummer air in off the gardens. Above them all the clock ticked, hands true, striking train restored. Frances adjusted her hat and went to the door, her composure intact and altered at once. Halloway stayed by the hearth while the last of the glow went out of it. The silence in the room was not dread any longer.

It was only the quiet that comes after a thing has been paid for. Outside, the daylight lay long over the gardens and the canal beyond them. Inside, the household went on, each of them carrying something changed and something set down for good. Halloway closed his notebook. "Order is a fragile thing," he said, and the dry London in his voice had gone soft at the edges, "especially when it's built on borrowed hours." The drawing room kept its peace. The estate resumed its routines - not as before, but as it must.
