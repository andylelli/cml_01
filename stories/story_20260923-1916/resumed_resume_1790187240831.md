Gwendolyn's answer, from the doorway, was quick. "Nora did."

Bertram nodded, his gaze shifting to Nora. "You know the patterns of usage for both?"

"That's not what the torque readings indicate," Nora replied, the corners of her mouth lifting in a wry smile. She picked up a pencil, twirling it between her fingers.

Theodora set her program aside, smoothing her skirt. "I imagine only someone with technical expertise could manipulate both without being noticed," she said, her voice mild.

Bertram tapped his notebook. "Precisely. The pattern of ledger entries, the compass's wear, even the timing of the entries-all point to someone familiar with the routine and the tools."

Harriet poured tea for Theodora, her hands steady. "They don't realize," she murmured, the words carrying a private sting.

Nora watched the steam curl from her cup. "You think I'm the only one capable of sleight of hand?" Her voice was dry, her gaze unwavering.

Bertram answered, "Let's get back to the facts." He unfolded a slip of paper-a list of witness statements-placing it beside the map.

Theodora leaned in, her brows drawn. "I remember a scandal in the family over inheritance-someone forged a document, and it nearly ruined us," she said quietly, her fingers tracing the rim of her teacup.

Harriet set the tray aside, her eyes never leaving Nora. "You're good with engines, Nora," she said, the words polite but laced with meaning. "But people are trickier."

Bertram's focus did not waver. "The timeline narrows. The only person with access, opportunity, and knowledge is you, Nora. The others are cleared by both evidence and witness."

Nora set her cup down, the porcelain clinking against the saucer. "But the torque readings disagree," she repeated, but her voice was softer now.

Harriet's hands trembled as she gathered the napkins, the motion revealing a flash of vulnerability beneath her practiced calm. She remembered the pain of unjust dismissal, though she spoke nothing of it.

Theodora rose, crossing to the window. She watched the last light fade from the dunes, her silhouette reflected in the glass.

Bertram closed the map, folding it with military precision. "Facts before assumptions," he said again, the ritual closing of another step in the investigation.

The room fell silent, the ticking mantle clock marking the slow approach of night and the steady unspooling of secrets. Each person touched a different object-the cup, the pencil, the program, the map-as if grounding themselves in something solid amid the shifting sands of suspicion.

Nora's eyes met Bertram's across the table. "So what's next?" she asked, voice low.

Bertram replied, "We test the bearings," and the conversation moved on, the sense of finality settling over the parlor like the deepening dark.

## Chapter 8: Compass and Ledger

"Let's test it," Bertram Norbury said, setting the battered compass on the hotel office desk under the stark light of the desk lamp. The late evening pressed against the windows, shadows pooling in the corners where the day's warmth had already faded. Nora Quayle watched, arms folded, her expression unreadable.

Bertram adjusted the compass, first holding it level. The needle pointed true north, unwavering. Harriet Kestrel stood near the sideboard, her hands clasped tightly and her gaze fixed on the battered compass. "You really think a tilt makes that much difference?" she asked, her voice quiet.

"Yes," Nora replied, her answer clipped and certain. Bertram handed her the compass, and she took it, turning it slowly in her palm.

Bertram nodded at the desk. "Now tilt it, as you've seen others do."

Nora complied, angling the case as Gaunt had been known to do. The needle swung, hesitated, and settled a clear fifteen degrees off north. The difference was obvious, the offset plain for all to see.

Harriet leaned in, eyes narrowing. "So the judge's reading at nine o'clock and the actual orientation at a quarter to ten could be off by as much as fifteen degrees?" She glanced at Bertram, then at the open ledger on the desk.

"Exactly," Bertram said. He pointed to the entries between eight forty-five and a quarter to ten. "Now the timing-if we compare the witness statements from eight fifty to nine fifteen with the staff confirmations for Harriet, and the presence of Nora in the office-"

Harriet interrupted, her voice polished but edged. "If only they knew the difference between a muddy footprint and a muddy alibi-one washes out, the other stains."

Bertram smiled, faintly. "First, we look at what the compass shows," he said, turning the compass once more.

Nora watched the demonstration, her brow furrowed. She looked at the ledger, fingers tracing the uneven ink of entries made between eight forty-five and a quarter to ten.

"The ink here is fresher in some places than others," she observed, her voice dry. "Anyone with sense could see it's been tampered with."

Bertram nodded. "And only someone with knowledge of both the ledger's routines and the compass's quirks could manipulate both to fabricate an alibi."

Harriet moved to the window, drawing back the curtain to peer into the darkness. "I was cleaning rooms from ten minutes to nine to a quarter past nine," she said, her tone firm. "Mrs. Evans and Mr. Blackwell both saw me, and the cleaning cart never left the corridor."

Bertram checked his notes, comparing the times. "That clears you, Harriet. The staff confirm your presence, and the guest ledger supports it."

Harriet let the curtain fall, her hands smoothing her apron. "If only they knew how easy it is to miss the truth when you're looking for dirt."

"Montague Gaunt, of course, is cleared-dead men have the best alibis," Bertram remarked, his observational humour a brief balm against the tension in the room.

Nora's gaze lingered on the compass, her jaw set. "The compass disagrees," she said, but her confidence had begun to fray.

Bertram demonstrated the tilt again, the needle's deflection unerring. He let the compass settle, the needle holding fifteen degrees from true north. Bertram glanced at the open ledger, then back at the others, letting the implications register.

Harriet watched, silent now, her hands resting on the sideboard.

The room held its breath as the truth settled: the trick, once revealed, could not be unseen. The desk lamp cast sharp shadows over the compass and the ledger, both objects now bearing the marks of the evening's scrutiny.

## Chapter 9: The Bearings of Guilt

"Who else could have manipulated both the ledger and the compass readings, and known enough to use the judge's tilted bearing to fabricate an alibi?" Bertram Norbury's voice cut through the hush of the hotel lounge, the night pressing at the glass as the survivors gathered in uneasy silence. Gwendolyn Vance sat with her hands folded in her lap, her composure unbroken; Harriet Kestrel perched on the edge of a low table, a stack of napkins arranged with precise care. Theodora Lachlan stood near the viewing stand windows, one hand resting lightly on the frame.

Theodora's answer was immediate. "Only Nora."

Harriet glanced up, her tone polite but pointed. "If only they knew-how simply misdirection can ruin a life, or save it." She smoothed the napkins, her movements careful, and looked away as Bertram took a step forward, his shadow stretching across the patterned carpet.

Bertram's lips tightened; he spoke each word with measured care, his gaze unwavering as if to leave no doubt. The words carried the weight of finality. "Nora Quayle killed Montague Gaunt."

The silence that followed was absolute, broken only by the distant crash of waves against the dunes. Nora stood, her arms folded, gaze steady on Bertram. She let out a breath, her voice quiet but edged with bitterness. "He was going to ruin me-expose everything I'd worked for, take my career and my name and leave nothing but wreckage behind. I couldn't let him."

Gwendolyn closed her eyes for a moment, fingers tightening around a handkerchief. Theodora turned away, her reflection pale in the glass. Harriet gathered the napkins and rose, leaving them in a neat stack beside the decanter.

Bertram nodded, the case closed not by hope, but by the cold geometry of fact and motive. Outside, the wind rattled the panes, the hotel's lights flickering in the darkness as the world turned inexorably towards another day.

## Chapter 10: Bearings Restored

"Fresh towels for the guest rooms," Harriet Kestrel announced, pushing her trolley down the corridor with a brisk efficiency that had eluded the hotel for days. Late night clung to the windows, but inside, the rhythm of ordinary life returned, as if the proof of Nora Quayle's guilt, barely spoken, could be folded away with the linens and dust.

Bertram Norbury stood in the doorway of the office, the battered compass resting in his palm, its needle finally still. He looked over the ledgers, now restored to their proper order, and shut the last volume with a quiet finality. The next morning's train would carry him away, but for now, he took a moment to watch the flicker of the lamp on the brass paperweight, the object already beginning its slow transformation back to mere ornament.

Gwendolyn Vance, her posture as exact as ever, set about reviewing the hotel's accounts with a red pen and a new ledger-one that would bear her careful, unvarnished script. The sudden collapse of her family estate had forced her to accept the necessity of transparency, and her resolve showed in the firm line of her mouth as she checked each entry, her hand steady as she drew a line beneath the final sum.

Nora Quayle, silent, was led away by the constable, her boots leaving faint marks in the sand outside. She did not look back at the hotel, nor at the dunes where her career had come to an end. Theodora Lachlan watched from the window, her own troubles momentarily forgotten as she considered the way fortunes shifted with the tide.

Harriet paused at the threshold of the lounge, her hands full of folded towels, and took a moment to remember Montague Gaunt as he had once been-laughing in the garage, his voice bright with the promise of victory, untouched by suspicion or threat. There was a time, before secrets and bearings and ledgers, when he had been simply a racer, his ambitions unshadowed and his friendships unbroken.

Bertram, his own reputation restored, accepted a letter from Gwendolyn-an offer to consult further on the hotel's security. He tucked it into his pocket, a small token of trust regained, as the night deepened outside.

Gwendolyn, changed by the ordeal, took more time with the staff, her humour still dry but now carrying a warmth that surprised even herself. She left the door to her office open, the ledgers in plain sight, and made a point of inviting Harriet to tea.

Theodora, her fortune and reputation safe for now, returned to her hosting with a new caution, her laughter softer but still genuine. She arranged the racing trophies on the mantel, pausing to dust each one, her hands lingering on the names engraved from years past.

"Tea in the morning?" Theodora asked, pausing at the parlor door, her voice gentle.

"Of course," Gwendolyn replied, setting her pen aside.

Harriet, arms full of towels, smiled as she passed. "What peace we've found," she murmured, her gaze lingering on the quiet calm settling over the hotel.

The dunes outside were hushed, the sand smoothing over the last traces of violence, ready to resume their silent watch. In the hotel, the ordinary routines resumed, each survivor altered but not undone, as the bearings finally settled and the compass pointed, once again, to an uncertain but honest north.
