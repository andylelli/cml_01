"Yes," she replied, voice small.

He glanced at the fragment again, then set it aside, his mind turning over the implications.

A moth battered itself against the glass, its wings catching the light. Ottoline rose, gently cupping it in her palm before releasing it through the open window-an act as silent and delicate as any prayer.

Ambrose's gaze lingered on her, then dropped to the clock. "You're the only one with the skill to adjust this, aren't you?"

Ottoline hesitated. "I suppose so."

Ambrose's tone was almost gentle. "One mustn't jump to conclusions, but it's becoming increasingly difficult to ignore the evidence."

He closed the panel, rising to his feet. "You may go, Miss Dunmore. I'll finish here."

Ottoline paused at the door, her silhouette framed in the golden spill of the corridor lamp. "Goodnight, Inspector."

"Goodnight," Ambrose replied, watching her leave, the torn glove fragment still resting on the shelf-a silent witness to a night when time itself had been tampered with, and the truth remained just out of reach.

## Chapter 8: The Timed Clock Test

"Inspector Halloway, I believe the clockmaker's arrived," Ottoline Dunmore announced, standing in the threshold of the manor clock room. The early morning light caught the gleam of the brass pendulum as it swung with precise regularity-a fresh start, perhaps, for a day meant to expose what had been so carefully hidden.

Ambrose greeted the clockmaker, a stooped man in a worn waistcoat who set his battered case on the floor and began his examination without preamble. Ottoline lingered, her hands wrapped around her maintenance log, as Ambrose hovered nearby, notepad at the ready.

The clockmaker produced his report, the paper crackling as he unfolded it. "The striking sequence was altered, Inspector. The detent lever was advanced, causing the hour strike to sound twenty minutes early. When the clock face displayed a quarter to four, the chime actually sounded at twenty-five minutes past three." He glanced at Ambrose, his tone matter-of-fact.

Ambrose nodded, eyes narrowing. "That corresponds precisely with the victim's pocket watch-ten minutes past three to twenty-five minutes past three."

Ottoline's knuckles whitened on the logbook. "So, the chime and the time of death do not match."

The clockmaker continued, "Fingerprints were found on the mechanism-Miss Dunmore's, plainly visible. Her maintenance log shows alterations in the entries for yesterday afternoon."

Ambrose took the logbook, turning to the page marked in Ottoline's careful hand. "The sequence here doesn't match previous entries. This was changed."

Ottoline swallowed. "I... must have made a mistake."

Ambrose's gaze was steady. "A mistake, or a deliberate adjustment?"

Ottoline's answer was barely more than a whisper. "A mistake."

He set the logbook down, the weight of the truth settling in the room.

Frances Orme, summoned as witness, entered quietly. "I believe the clock was tampered with. I saw Miss Dunmore with tools near the clock, and she was the only one with knowledge to alter it."

Ambrose turned to Frances. "You're certain?"

Frances's answer was crisp. "Absolutely."

Ambrose gestured to the clockmaker. "Show us, please."

The clockmaker adjusted the detent lever, demonstrating how, by repositioning it, the chime would sound early. He set the minute hand to a quarter to four, then triggered the strike-twenty minutes ahead of the true hour. The room filled with the resonant clang, echoing the afternoon of the murder.

Ambrose and Frances watched as the experiment confirmed the mechanism's trickery.

Ambrose's tone was dry. "One mustn't jump to conclusions, but the evidence is damning. Miss Dunmore, your fingerprints, your log entries, your presence with tools-no one else had both access and skill."

Beatrice Whitlock, in memory, would have offered only a flat answer. "No."

Frances's retort was gentle but edged. "Inspector, if only all our clocks could be so easily reset. Unfortunately, time has a way of running out for those who think themselves cleverer than the hour."

Ambrose nodded, pocketing the logbook. "Thank you, Miss Orme. You may go."

Ottoline lingered, her hand resting on the clock as if seeking comfort in its steady rhythm.

Ambrose's voice softened. "You wanted control, Miss Dunmore. Instead, you've lost it."

The room was quiet save for the clock's tick-regular now, no longer deceiving. The echo of the chime lingered in the stillness, as if daring anyone to deny what had just been revealed.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

The sitting room was hushed, sunlight splayed across the carpet as Ambrose Halloway faced Percival Thorne, Josephine Rutherford, and Frances Orme. He held the clockmaker's report in one hand, Ottoline Dunmore's altered logbook in the other.

"Josephine, did you ever touch the manor clock mechanism?" Ambrose asked.

Josephine's reply was flat. "No."

Percival paced by the window, hands clasped behind his back. "Inspector, must we go over this again? I was in the library from four o'clock to half past four. Mrs. Tillingham and Frances both saw me. I had no reason to tamper with the clock, nor the skill-frankly, the only thing I ever adjusted in this house was the sherry decanter, and I did it badly at that. If you're looking for mechanical mischief, you'll have to look elsewhere, and if you're hoping for a confession, you'll wait as long as Josephine's canal for a new lock-"

Ottoline Dunmore's retort was quiet but clear. "You're not as clever as you think, Mr. Thorne."

Ambrose looked at each in turn. "Ottoline Dunmore killed Beatrice Whitlock."

The room was still, the echo of Ambrose's words hanging in the air like the fading chime of the clock.

Percival let out a long, shaky breath, sinking into an armchair. Frances's lips parted in surprise, one hand pressed to her throat, while Josephine's expression was unreadable, her hands folded tight in her lap.

Ambrose set the clockmaker's report on the table, the logbook beside it. "The evidence is clear. Ottoline had access, skill, motive, and opportunity. The clock was her accomplice, but the truth has caught up at last."

No one spoke. The silence was deep as the grave, broken only by the slow, deliberate tick of the hall clock, marking the last seconds of Ottoline Dunmore's freedom, and the first moments of the house's uneasy peace.

## Chapter 10: The Culprit Revealed

The drawing room was quieter than it had been for days; only the muted voices of constables in the hall drifted through the half-open door. Ottoline Dunmore sat on a straight-backed chair, wrists together, her gaze lowered. Ambrose Halloway stood at the window, the pale afternoon sun gilding the dust motes swirling in the air.

Josephine Rutherford entered with a basket of linens, pausing by the sideboard. "Inspector, do you want these delivered to the station?"

Ambrose's answer was flat. "No."

Percival Thorne stepped in, carrying a sheaf of estate papers. He hesitated, then set them on the writing desk, smoothing the top page as if it might erase the past week's turmoil. "I'll be resuming my duties, for now. The family's affairs can't be left untended."

Josephine's retort was dry. "Try not to misplace the keys this time."

Percival's attempt at humor was brief. "I'll have to keep better track-or hire someone who will."

Ambrose watched as the last of the constables escorted Ottoline from the room, her steps slow, dignity intact even in defeat. He turned to Frances Orme, who had resumed her usual seat by the fire, a fresh letter in hand. "Mrs. Orme, your social standing is secure. The estate will not pursue the matter further."

Frances's nod was gracious, her lips curving in a polite, unreadable smile.

Ambrose slipped his hands into his pockets, glancing at the clock. Its hands pointed unambiguously to four o'clock, the chime ringing true.

Percival, emboldened by the room's calm, addressed Ambrose. "Inspector, I'd like to make arrangements for the lock-keeper's cottages. Miss Rutherford deserves better."

Josephine looked up from her basket, surprise flickering in her gaze. "About time."

Ambrose inclined his head, a rare smile ghosting his lips. "Progress, Mr. Thorne. I'll make a note."

The memory of Beatrice Whitlock lingered not as a victim, but as a formidable presence-the woman whose brisk laughter once filled this room when the harvest was good, whose clipped advice had steered more than one gathering away from disaster.

Frances picked up a small silver frame from the mantel, brushing dust from its edge. "She would have approved of order restored, I think."

Ambrose nodded, his gaze distant. "Let us not complicate matters unnecessarily."

As the house returned to its routines, the clock in the hall struck the hour with measured precision-no tricks, no confusion. The staff moved through their tasks, linens folded, vases straightened, fires stoked. Outside, the first green shoots of spring pressed up through the gravel, indifferent to drama, intent on their own business.

The ordinary day resumed: the clock in the hall struck again, staff bustled through with fresh linens and polished silver, and the scent of burning wood drifted from the hearth.
