## Chapter 6: The Judge's Compass

"As it must be," Gwendolyn Vance said, her voice composed as dusk pressed against the hotel office windows. The golden hour made the brass compass on the desk gleam as though it might hold the answer to everything. Bertram Norbury stood by the map table, tracing the penciled routes with one finger, his other hand resting on the battered compass case. Nora Quayle leaned against a filing cabinet, arms crossed, eyes narrowed as she watched the process unfold.

Harriet Kestrel entered quietly, her hands folded before her. She glanced at the compass, her expression unreadable. "If only they knew how easy it is to get lost with a faulty guide," she said, straightening a stack of letters on the sideboard.

Gwendolyn adjusted the lamp again, her fingers steady. "It's a relief," she said, her voice so mild it almost vanished. "To be exonerated by witnesses rather than instruments." She smoothed her skirt, the gesture precise.

Harriet's gaze lingered on the compass. "Some people would rather trust machines than people," she said, arranging the letters into a neat pile. The words carried a sting that made Nora glance away.

Bertram ran a finger along the compass casing. "Notice the wear-always on the left," he said. "Our judge had a habit, and that habit led to a consistent error. It's not enough to clear everyone, but it's enough to clear Gwendolyn."

Theodora's voice was a whisper from the hall. "That would be unfortunate," she said, referring to the judge's mistake. Her hand rested on the doorframe, nails painted a glossy red.

Bertram turned to Nora. "If the compass reading is unreliable, we must rely on witness testimony. The judge's bearing can't place anyone with certainty." He set the compass in the center of the desk, the brass catching the last of the light.

Nora's reply was clipped: "Fact first." She pushed away from the filing cabinet, the movement abrupt.

Harriet smoothed a crease in the tablecloth. "If only they knew how quickly fortunes change," she said, her words soft but sharp.

A moth circled the lamp, its wings beating against the glass shade. Gwendolyn watched it for a moment before opening the window wider, letting it escape into the night.

Bertram's pencil hovered above the page. "We have to reconsider the timeline. The judge's compass can't be trusted, so the alibi it supported is suspect." He wrote a single line, his handwriting meticulous.

Theodora entered fully, closing the door behind her. She carried a stack of correspondence, the envelopes crisp and pale. "Family fortunes can collapse overnight," she said quietly, handing a letter to Gwendolyn. The exchange was brief: Gwendolyn's hand tightened on the envelope, Theodora's gaze lingering just a moment too long.

The office was quiet, the only sound the distant crash of waves against the shore. Each person touched the evidence-compass, letters, lamp, photograph-as if searching for certainty in the objects at hand.

The dusk deepened, the room slipping from gold to shadow. The judge's error had cleared one suspect, but the truth remained elusive, hovering just out of reach, waiting for the next turn of the clock and the next, inevitable, question.

## Chapter 7: Secrets Beneath the Surface

She stood near the fireplace, hands wrapped around a chipped mug, her stance as unyielding as the iron grate beside her. Bertram Norbury sat at the edge of a faded settee, his notebook balanced on one knee, pencil poised midair. Harriet Kestrel perched on a footstool, her posture deceptively demure, eyes bright and watchful. Theodora Lachlan lingered by the sideboard, her fingers tracing the rim of a silver tray.

"Miss Quayle, you knew the ledger's quirks better than anyone," Bertram said, his tone steady. "Exclusive access, patterns only someone with your experience would recognize." He tapped his pencil against the notebook, the sound a soft metronome in the quiet room.

Nora's lips curled in a wry half-smile. "Nice of the world to be so tidy for some," she said, swirling her mug. She stared into the tea as if it might reveal answers.

Theodora moved to the mantel, adjusting a porcelain figurine of a racing car. "Quarter past nine to a quarter to ten, I was at the beach viewing stand. The guestbook and three witnesses will say the same." Her voice was calm, but her grip on the figurine was tight.

Harriet's retort was sharp, polite savagery wrapped in velvet. "Some of us have no need to invent errands, Miss Quayle," she said, setting the biscuit on a plate. "The truth is simpler to remember." The words hung in the air, pointed but cloaked in courtesy.

Nora's answer was six words: "I prefer torque to memory games." She set her mug down, the sound decisive.

Bertram's gaze moved from Nora to the clock. "Nine o'clock to a quarter to ten. Your access to the ledger and knowledge of the compass's foibles would have let you exploit both, Miss Quayle." He closed his notebook, the snap audible.

Theodora's eyes lingered on the figurine. She adjusted its wheels, aligning them with the edge of the mantel. "That would be unfortunate," she murmured, her voice barely above a whisper.

Gwendolyn leafed through the invoices, her fingers steady. "The records show a pattern-entries made in your hand, Nora. And the ink changes with the times." She placed an invoice atop the stack, lips pressed thin.

## Chapter 8: Compass and Ledger

"Fact first," Bertram Norbury said, his voice firm as he set the battered compass on the hotel office desk. The late evening shadows pressed at the windows, the darkness broken only by the yellow glow of a single desk lamp. Nora Quayle stood opposite him, arms folded, her stance defiant, the spanner from earlier now tucked into her belt. The room was silent but for the steady tick of the wall clock.

"You said the judge's compass was always off by fifteen degrees," Bertram began, his gaze fixed on Nora. "I want you to show me." He gestured to the compass, the brass cool to the touch.

Nora hesitated, then stepped forward. She picked up the compass, holding it level, the needle settling on north. "This is how it's supposed to be used," she said, her tone even. She set it down, then picked it up again, tilting the casing slightly. The needle wavered, swinging off true by a clear margin.

Bertram watched closely. "Now tilt it-just as the judge did." Nora obliged, her hand steady. The needle shifted, the margin now precisely fifteen degrees west of true north. She set the compass down, the motion careful.

Harriet Kestrel entered quietly, a tray of tea balanced on one hand. She set it on the sideboard, her gaze lingering on the demonstration. "If only they knew how much depends on a steady hand," she said, pouring a cup.

"The difference is clear," Bertram said, his voice measured. "Level, the bearing is true. Tilted, it's always fifteen degrees off. That's enough to move a person from the office to the beach-on paper, at least." He looked at Nora, his eyes sharp.

Nora's reply was flat: "So the alibi's built on sand." She picked up a biscuit, breaking it in half, her motions precise.

Harriet's retort was gentle but pointed. "Most things here are, Mr. Norbury. We just tidy up after." She handed Bertram a cup, the china warm in his hands.

Bertram nodded, setting the compass beside the ledger. "You had exclusive access to both the ledger and the compass, Miss Quayle. You knew how to exploit the judge's habit-and how to adjust the timing entries to fit." He opened the ledger, turning to the page marked by ink discrepancies.

Nora's gaze was steady. "If you're looking for a confession, you'll be waiting a while," she said, her voice edged with humorless amusement.

Bertram didn't smile. "The ink at nine o'clock is fresher than the entries before and after. The hand is yours. And the timing matches the gap in your whereabouts." He tapped the page, the sound sharp in the quiet room.

Harriet poured herself a cup, her hands steady. "I was cleaning the east wing at the time. Mrs. Hargreaves and the bellboy saw me. I couldn't have been near the office." She sipped her tea, her posture unruffled.

Bertram nodded. "That clears you, Miss Kestrel. And as for Montague Gaunt-" He glanced at the empty chair near the window, the cushion still indented from the man's last hours alive. "His alibi is, unfortunately, permanent."

Harriet set her cup down, her gaze lingering on the compass. "If only they knew how often the truth comes down to a matter of degrees," she said, her voice soft.

Nora's hands tightened on the edge of the desk. "So we're back to the beginning," she said, her tone resigned. She glanced at the compass, then at the ledger, as if weighing their combined weight against her own.

Bertram closed the ledger, his expression unreadable. "Bertram folded his notebook, setting it atop the desk. The last demonstration lingered in the air: the compass, tilted, always gave the same error, a mistake someone could rely on. And the ledger-its entries adjusted, ink fresher at nine than before or after, just as Nora had shown. The pieces fit together, but the final name remained unspoken." ## Chapter 10: Confrontation and Aftermath

"That would be unfortunate," Theodora Lachlan said as she stepped into the hotel office, her voice gentle but edged with relief. A memory of Montague Gaunt flickered through the room-nothing to do with his death: in the summer of 1931, he had untangled a child's bright kite from the rose hedge, then raced a toy yacht against the hotel's gardener across the duck pond, shouting encouragement and curses in equal measure.
