## Chapter 6: The False Solution

The scent of damp wool and coal smoke clung to the air, and the sound of the wind rattling the windowpanes seemed to underscore every pause. Eleanor Voss stood at the centre of the room, her notes gathered in one gloved hand, the other resting on the battered logbook that had become her anchor in this shifting winter evening. The grandfather clock loomed in the corner, its brass face glinting dully, its hands unmoved. The contradiction between its silent decree and the bell tower's chime still gnawed at her, but the others in the room seemed eager for resolution.

Captain Ivor Hale leaned against the mantel, the glow from the fire casting sharp shadows across his features. 'The staff saw me in the lounge at the relevant time,' he said, his voice clipped, as if daring anyone to contradict him. 'Ask them yourself, if you must. I was here from two o'clock until nearly three. If the clock says otherwise, it's the clock that's wrong. ' His gaze was steady, but Eleanor caught the way his hand flexed against the marble, the faintest tremor betraying the effort it cost him to remain composed. Beatrice Quill perched on the edge of a settee, her posture immaculate but her eyes darting from Eleanor to the logbook and back again. The lamplight caught the sheen of her silk scarf, and her voice, when it came, was brittle. 'The kitchen schedule is there for anyone to see. I was in the Staff Quarters and Kitchen from two fifteen until three.

The logbook entry is in my own hand, and Mrs. Voss has already checked it. ' She smoothed her skirt, fingers trembling for a moment before she stilled them with practiced grace. 'If you want confirmation, the kitchen staff will tell you the same. ' The words rang with conviction, but Eleanor noted the flicker of relief that crossed Beatrice's face when no one pressed her further. Sylvia Trent lounged in an armchair beneath the blackout-curtained window, her arms folded and her expression sharp. 'If you're looking for someone to blame, you'll have to look elsewhere, Mrs. Voss. The gardener saw me in the garden lounge at the time in question-he even signed the entry log. I couldn't have been anywhere near the lobby or the clock. ' Her tone was dry, but the set of her jaw was defensive, and her foot tapped an impatient rhythm against the rug.

She looked away, as if daring Eleanor to challenge her account. Eleanor moved to the battered logbook, flipping to the relevant pages. The kitchen schedule was clear: Beatrice's name, the time, the neat checkmark beside it. The gardener's entry, too, was precise, placing Sylvia in the garden lounge, away from the scene. Captain Ivor Hale's presence in the lounge was corroborated by multiple staff signatures, each entry written in the careful hand of someone who knew the weight of a recorded fact. The evidence, on the surface, was impeccable. She let her finger rest on the page, feeling the roughness of the paper beneath her glove, and allowed a silence to stretch. The others watched her, expectation heavy in the dim firelight.

For a moment, Eleanor felt the isolation of her role-a single mind holding the weight of a murder, the truth suspended between the tick of the clock and the chime of the bell. She glanced at the grandfather clock, its hands frozen at a quarter to three, and then at the logbook entry: '2:00 PM - bell tower chimed. ' The contradiction was as sharp as ever, yet the room seemed to crave closure. Even Captain Ivor Hale's rigid posture had softened, as if the clearing of names had lifted some invisible burden. A tray of clock repair tools, their metal dulled by use, sat on a side table near the hearth. Eleanor let her gaze linger on them, recalling the faint scratch marks she had seen near the escapement wheel's pivot, the reversed orientation that still defied explanation.

She said nothing, only noted the way Hugo Vane's absence from the room left a hollow in the conversation-a silence that felt both deliberate and dangerous. Beatrice's voice broke the quiet. 'So, Mrs. Voss, does that settle it? We're all accounted for, aren't we? ' Her words were light, but her eyes searched Eleanor's face for reassurance. The others seemed to lean forward, hungry for a verdict. Eleanor offered a thin, measured smile. 'It settles some things, Miss Quill. Not all. ' She closed the logbook with a soft snap, the sound oddly final in the hush.

Sylvia's laughter was brittle, slicing through the tension. 'Perhaps the clocks in this place are the only ones with secrets left to keep. ' The remark drew a reluctant smile from Beatrice and a grunt from Captain Ivor Hale, but the relief in the room was unmistakable-a collective exhale, as if the clearing of names had restored a fragile peace. Yet Eleanor felt none of it. The contradiction between the brass hands frozen at a quarter to three-and the bell tower s echo at twenty minutes past two remained unresolved, a flaw in the fabric of the solution the others so desperately wanted to accept. She glanced once more at the grandfather clock, then at the tray of tools, and finally at the logbook. The case, it seemed, was solved for everyone but herself.

Eleanor's eyes narrowed as she tapped a finger against the logbook's margin. 'Look here,' she said softly, drawing the group's attention. 'Hugo Vane's name appears alongside notes about the mechanism-he had access, and more than that, knowledge to operate it in ways none of us could. That means he had the means and opportunity to manipulate the clock's workings without detection. ' Eleanor paused, then continued, 'Remember, the grandfather clock was running backward for twenty minutes before it stopped. Only someone with intimate knowledge could have engineered that subtle sabotage. Hugo's expertise and access make him the only one capable of such a feat. ' As the fire burned low and the wind pressed harder against the windows, Eleanor gathered her notes and rose. The lounge was quieter now, the tension eased, but the puzzle in her mind only deepened.

The evidence had cleared Captain Ivor Hale, Beatrice Quill, and Sylvia Trent, yet the timeline refused to align. The flaw was there, silent and unyielding, waiting for someone to look past the comfort of an easy answer. Eleanor allowed herself a final glance at the clock, its hands unmoved, and felt the weight of the unsolved hour settle once more upon her shoulders.

## Chapter 7: Secrets Beneath Secrets

Eleanor Voss paused outside the private study, her notes pressed to her chest, the corridor's hush broken only by the faint, persistent rattle of wind against the windowpanes. The air was cold, carrying a lingering scent of coal smoke and the sharp tang of metal, and the dim glow from a single shaded lamp cast long, uncertain shadows along the skirting boards. It was late evening, and the hotel's silence felt heavier now, as if the building itself were holding its breath. The firelight from the lounge had faded behind her, but the puzzle in her mind burned as fiercely as ever. She hesitated at the threshold, recalling the brittle relief that had threaded through the lounge only moments before. The others had drifted away, their alibis confirmed, their faces slackening with the promise of closure.

But Eleanor could not let go of the contradiction-the clock's hands fixed at 'a quarter to three', the bell tower's chime at 'twenty minutes past two'. The difference was no longer a mere curiosity; it was the fulcrum on which the whole affair balanced. She stepped into the study, the door clicking softly behind her, and let her gaze travel over the familiar landscape: the battered logbook, the tray of clock repair tools, the grandfather clock itself looming in the gloom. A faint creak signaled Hugo Vane's presence. He stood by the desk, his silhouette cut sharply against the lamplight, his hands clasped behind his back. The lines of his wool jacket were immaculate, but his posture was a fraction too rigid, as if he were bracing himself against an unseen blow. 'Mrs.

Voss,' he said, voice low and even, though his eyes darted to the clock and back again. 'You're not yet satisfied, I take it? ' Eleanor let the question hang, her own voice measured. 'Satisfaction is a rare commodity, Mr. Vane. I find it often vanishes just as one draws near. ' She crossed to the grandfather clock, gloved fingers tracing the edge of the glass door. The mechanism inside gleamed faintly, but her attention snagged on the tray of tools-tiny screwdrivers, tweezers, a slender brass lever, each laid out with a precision that spoke of habit rather than show. She picked up the lever, feeling its weight, and glanced at Hugo. 'You're fond of these, aren't you? Not every hotel owner keeps such a kit at hand. ' He managed a thin smile, but the tension in his jaw betrayed him. 'The clock is a family piece. I've always maintained it myself.

The war made proper repairmen scarce, and one learns to improvise. ' His tone was light, but Eleanor caught the flicker of something darker-defensiveness, perhaps, or the edge of fear. She set the lever down with care, her gaze moving to the clock's open panel. The delicate scoring along the edge of the escapement wheel's pivot were clearer in this light, irregular lines that had not been there in the months before. She remembered Hugo's earlier insistence that the clock was temperamental in winter, but now the explanation felt hollow. A silence stretched, filled only by the distant sound of the wind and the muted tick of a smaller desk clock.

Eleanor let her hand rest on the open logbook, its pages marked with Hugo's own entries: '2:20 to 2:50 PM-private study. ' No witness had signed beside the interval. 'You were alone here for nearly half an hour,' she said, her tone neutral. 'Plenty of time to attend to correspondence-and to the clock, if you wished. ' Hugo's reply was slow, almost careful. 'I assure you, Mrs. Voss, my only concern was the hotel's reputation. Dr. Finch had threatened to expose certain irregularities in the accounts. I asked her to keep her counsel until I could resolve the matter. That's all. ' His eyes did not quite meet hers, and his hand hovered over the tray of tools before withdrawing. The movement was slight, but Eleanor saw it for what it was-a tell, a sign of nerves that no amount of practiced calm could conceal.

She allowed herself a brief, sardonic thought: in a hotel where even the clocks kept secrets, perhaps the truth was the rarest guest of all. 'You're very skilled, Mr. Vane. These tools-' she gestured to the tray '-aren't for amateurs. And the mechanism itself-' she opened the clock's panel, exposing the escapement wheel and the faint, fresh scratches '-would confound most. But not you. ' Hugo stiffened, his composure fraying at the edges. 'If you're suggesting I tampered with the clock-' he began, but Eleanor cut him off with a gentle shake of her head. 'I'm suggesting only that you could have. No one else here has the knowledge, the access, or the tools. ' She let the words settle, watching the way his hands curled into fists at his sides, the way his gaze flicked to the door as if calculating escape. The silence that followed was thick with implication.

Eleanor's mind raced, the pieces of the puzzle shifting into a new configuration. The earlier assumption-that the clock's hands told the true hour-no longer held. The bell tower's chime at 'twenty minutes past two', the logbook's careful entries, the scratches on the escapement wheel, the exclusive presence of repair tools in Hugo's study: each detail, once innocuous, now pointed to a single, inescapable conclusion. The timeline, the motive, the means-all had to be reconsidered. Only Hugo Vane possessed the unique combination of skill and opportunity to manipulate the clock's concealed mechanism. She turned to face him fully, her voice soft but unyielding. 'You understand, Mr. Vane, that the truth will out-whether it's coaxed from a clock or a conscience. ' For a moment, something flickered in his expression-fear, perhaps, or the recognition of a game nearly lost.

But he said nothing, only straightened his jacket and looked past her, as if seeking refuge in the shadows beyond the lamplight. Eleanor lingered in the study after he left, the cold settling into her bones. She let her hand rest on the clock's open panel, tracing the scratches once more, the brass cool beneath her fingertips. The evidence was no longer ambiguous: only Hugo Vane could have operated the concealed lever, only he could have reversed the escapement and falsified the time. The others' alibis, once suspect, now seemed ironclad by comparison. The true timeline was emerging, and with it, the weight of motive and guilt. Yet even as the tension in the corridors deepened, Eleanor allowed herself a moment of dry wit.

She glanced at the battered logbook and murmured, 'It seems, after all, that time does tell tales-if one knows how to listen. ' The wind outside rose, rattling the windowpanes, and she closed the clock's panel with a soft click. The hour of reckoning was drawing near, and the secrets beneath Seacliff Hotel were at last beginning to surface.

## Chapter 8: The Final Trap

"If you please, gather round," Eleanor Voss called, her voice slicing through the hush that clung to the hotel's grand hall with its grandfather clock and bell tower. Shadows flickered against the marble as the wintry wind battered the entrance doors, and the faint echo of the bell tower's last chime lingered in the cold air. The scent of wax polish mingled with the sharper tang of brass and coal. Early night pressed against the blackout curtains, and the tension in the hall felt as taut as clock wire. The assembled faces caught the lamplight: Hugo Vane's composure brittle as frost, Captain Ivor Hale standing ramrod-straight in the gloom, Beatrice Quill clutching her scarf in white-knuckled silence, and Sylvia Trent's eyes narrowed, lips curled in a wry half-smile.

Eleanor pressed her palm to the battered logbook, recalling her own words-time does tell tales, if one knows how to listen-and let her gaze linger on the silent grandfather clock. Its brass hands, as ever, pointed stubbornly to a quarter to three. A hush fell as Eleanor opened the clock's glass door. The faint creak of the hinges was loud in the charged quiet. She drew out the winding key and, with careful ceremony, set it on the table beside a small tray of repair tools. Her gloved fingers hovered near the escapement, her breath visible in the chill. "Tonight, we will let the clock speak for itself. If I am wrong, then the murderer is still among us. If I am right, then time's lie will be revealed, and the true sequence exposed. " Sylvia's voice, sharp as frost, broke the silence. "You mean to set the hands moving? What will that prove, Mrs. Voss? " Eleanor met her gaze.

"Not just moving, Miss Trent. Watch not only the hands, but the heart of the machine. The bell tower chimed at twenty minutes past two-recorded in this logbook, witnessed by staff-and yet the clock's face insists on a quarter to three. That contradiction could only occur if someone intervened. Captain Ivor Hale, you claim the bell's chime matches your recollection? " Captain Ivor Hale nodded, his jaw tight. "I was in the smoking room with two staff. At twenty minutes past two, the bell rang. I checked my watch. The staff can confirm it. " "And you, Beatrice Quill? " Eleanor's voice was gentle but relentless. Beatrice's voice barely rose above a whisper. "I was at the front desk. The shift log and the guests will confirm it.

I did not leave. " "Sylvia Trent, the gardener's entry log places you in the garden lounge throughout the interval. " Eleanor's words were matter-of-fact, and Sylvia nodded once, arms folded. The air felt colder as each name was cleared, the circle tightening around the one who remained. Eleanor turned to Hugo Vane. "You were alone in your private study from twenty minutes past two until nearly three. No witness signed your logbook. Would you care to explain, Mr. Vane, before I proceed? " Hugo Vane's face was pale, but his voice, though strained, was steady. "I maintained the clock, yes. But murder? You're grasping, Mrs. Voss. I did nothing to that mechanism. " Eleanor did not answer him directly. She bent to the clock, her fingers deft and sure. From the tray, she selected a slender brass lever. The others leaned forward, tension mounting.

She set the escapement wheel in motion, then, deliberately, reversed the gear. The mechanism whirred, and the hands began to creep backward, the slow, inexorable movement visible to every eye. They crawled away from a quarter to three, retracing their path. Someone in the room was counting under their breath; Eleanor let them reach twenty before she spoke again. The room had gone silent except for the grind of gears and the whisper of her own breath. "You see," Eleanor said softly, "the clock was forced to run backward, by someone who understood its workings. That is the only way the face could show a quarter to three when the bell tower had already struck twenty minutes past two. The face was made to lie. " Captain Ivor Hale exhaled sharply. "So the murder - it did not happen at a quarter to three. The time on the clock was false. " He said it slowly, as though testing the shape of it.

"My alibi holds. " "And mine," Beatrice said, her voice steadier now, "if the bell tower is to be believed. " Sylvia Trent's laugh came out brittle. "I was in the garden lounge. The logs will show it. I couldn't have touched the clock, let alone the body. " Eleanor let the silence stretch before she went on. "Only one person had the tools, the seclusion, and reason enough to silence Dr. Finch. " She did not look away from Hugo Vane as she said it. "Dr. Finch was struck down with a heavy bronze statuette. The clock was set to run backward for twenty minutes, so the killing would seem to happen later - after Mr. Vane's alibi had already closed around him. " Hugo Vane's composure slipped. His hand went to his cuff, and there, caught in the dim light, was a torn piece of navy wool, snagged and hanging loose.

Eleanor stepped to the private study door, where a matching fragment of cloth clung to a splintered edge of the frame. She held it up without a word at first. "You left in a hurry, Mr. Vane. You left this behind you. " For a moment he said nothing at all. The silence held the whole room. Then, in a voice raw with something closer to grief than anger, he spoke. "Dr. Finch would have ruined everything. She threatened to expose my accounts. The hotel would have been finished - every job here lost. I tried to reason with her, but she would not listen. I-" His voice broke, and he stared at the clock as though its hands might yet turn back and undo what had been done. Eleanor's tone was gentle, but there was no yielding in it. "You chose to strike her, to protect your reputation and the hotel's future.

And then you chose to twist time itself, to buy your own escape. " The others stood without speaking, the weight of it settling over them. Captain Ivor Hale looked away, jaw clenched. Beatrice's eyes glistened, her composure finally giving way. Sylvia Trent's face was harder to read, caught somewhere between condemnation and an unwilling flicker of pity. At last Eleanor spoke again. "Justice is not always simple, Mr. Vane. You blurred the line between revenge and protection, and it cost a life. The clock's lie is ended now. Let that be the end of it. " The wind went on battering the stone walls, and the lamplight flickered as it had all evening. Eleanor rested her hand on the cold brass of the clock and felt, beneath her palm, the faint tick of the hour settling back into its place. Time, indifferent to all of it, moved on.

## Chapter 9: Suspect Clearances

It was late night, and the Hotel lounge and smoking room seemed to exhale at last, the wintry wind still rattling the windowpanes, the fire casting a flicker of warmth across worn leather and polished wood. Eleanor Voss stood at the hearth, her palm pressed to the cold brass of the clock, feeling the faint tick beneath her glove. The lamplight threw long shadows, and the scent of coal smoke hung in the air, sharp and persistent. The weight of the evening pressed in, mingling with relief and exhaustion, as if every surface in the room remembered what had passed. Captain Ivor Hale sat near the fire, one hand resting on his knee, the other gripping the arm of his chair with a force that belied his outward calm. He watched Eleanor expectantly, the set of his jaw betraying the strain he had carried since the first chime of the bell tower.

The silence was broken only by the wind and the faint creak of the floorboards as Beatrice Quill and Sylvia Trent took their seats opposite, each wrapped in the gravity of the moment. Outside, the blackout curtains held back the night, but the tension in the room seemed to pulse with every shifting shadow. Eleanor drew out the battered logbook, its pages edged with the faint scent of ink and paper gone soft with handling. She turned to the entries that had become her lodestar: the kitchen schedule, the front desk shift, the garden lounge log signed in the gardener's careful hand. 'We must be thorough,' she said, her voice low but unwavering. 'If we are to clear any name, it must be by the record and by testimony, not by wish. ' As she spoke, the others leaned in, each face caught by the lamplight, each breath held.

She began with Captain Ivor Hale. 'The bell tower chimed at twenty minutes past two,' Eleanor recited, tapping the entry with her pencil. 'The staff in the lounge confirm your presence at that time, and the logbook entry matches the hour precisely. ' Captain Ivor Hale exhaled, a long, slow breath that seemed to ease the rigid set of his shoulders. 'The staff will vouch for me,' he said quietly, nodding toward the closed door where, earlier, voices had murmured in support of his account. A tremor of gratitude flickered in his eyes, quickly masked by discipline. Turning to Beatrice Quill, Eleanor let the silence stretch a moment longer. 'Your name appears here,' she said, laying a gloved finger on the kitchen schedule. 'The log is in your hand, and the staff confirm you never left your post at the front desk.

No one saw you in the lobby or near the clock. ' Beatrice's lips parted as if to protest, but then she only nodded, her composure slipping into something softer, almost fragile. 'I did not move from my place,' she murmured, and the relief in her voice was as clear as the firelight glinting on her scarf. Sylvia Trent's turn came last.

Eleanor held up the gardener's log, the neat signature and time entry placing Sylvia in the garden lounge, far from the murder. 'This record clears you,' Eleanor said, her tone gentler now. 'The gardener's testimony is specific, and the entry log admits no doubt. ' Sylvia's chin lifted, her arms unfolding as she let out a breath that had been held too long. 'I was there,' she replied, voice edged with something halfway between vindication and exhaustion. 'Let no one say otherwise. ' The fire crackled, sending a burst of warmth into the chill, and for a moment the oppressive atmosphere loosened its grip. Eleanor allowed herself a brief pause, her gaze drifting to the window where the darkness pressed in, absolute and indifferent. She felt a weariness settle in her bones-the kind that comes not from the day's labor, but from carrying the burdens of others' secrets.

The trio across from her-Captain Ivor Hale, Beatrice Quill, Sylvia Trent-seemed to sag in their seats, the pressure of suspicion finally easing, if only a little. Yet relief was not unalloyed. As the logs and testimonies closed off paths of guilt, a different tension took their place. The knowledge that only one suspect remained-one whose alibi did not bear the weight of witness or written word-cast a longer, colder shadow than any accusation. Eleanor felt it keenly, the narrowing of possibility, the way the truth left scars even as it brought clarity. Her eyes lingered on the clock, the hands unmoved, as if time itself refused to advance until every consequence had been faced. Captain Ivor Hale spoke, his voice roughened by fatigue. 'It is a strange kind of freedom, Mrs. Voss, to be cleared by the record and not by trust.

I thank you, all the same. ' Beatrice Quill, twisting her scarf in her lap, nodded, her eyes shining with the tears she would not let fall. 'We do what we must to survive suspicion,' she whispered. Sylvia Trent, arms folded once more, added, 'And we carry the cost of what's been lost. ' The words hung between them, heavy as any verdict. Eleanor stood, gathering the logbook and her scattered notes. The truth had narrowed the field, but it had not brought peace. The relief of the cleared was matched by the loneliness of the one who remained unaccounted for, and by the knowledge that even justice, when it came, would leave the Hotel lounge and smoking room changed. She allowed herself one last look at the fire, at the faces of those whose innocence had been won not by faith but by the cold certainty of record and testimony.

Then she turned toward the door, the weight of unfinished consequence settling on her shoulders, and stepped into the wintry corridor beyond.

## Chapter 10: The Weight of Midnight

The hush of midnight pressed close in the Private study, dimly lit by the flicker of a single lamp. Shadows pooled along the walls, the scent of spent coal and old paper mingling with the faint tang of metal from the clock's open case. Beyond the window, the wind rattled against the panes with wintry persistence, and the cold seemed to seep into every corner, undeterred by blackout curtains or fireless grate. Eleanor Voss stood at the desk, the battered logbook open beneath her hand, her notes scattered like small islands on the sea-dark wood. She had gathered them here, not for another confrontation, but because the memory of the night's ordeal clung to the air, refusing to dissolve with the hour. The aftermath was a strange thing-neither relief nor triumph, but a hush laced with fatigue and disbelief.

Hugo Vane's confession, signed and witnessed, sat in an envelope on the desk's edge, as final as any document could be. The mechanical evidence lay exposed: the brass lever, the tray of tools, the clock's innards stilled in the position where she had last examined them. She found herself staring at the faint scratch marks on the escapement wheel, the trace of violence both mechanical and human, and wondered if anyone would ever look at the grandfather clock without remembering what it had concealed. The hands had once declared 'a quarter to three,' but the hotel's bell tower had told the truth: 'twenty minutes past two'-a contradiction now resolved, yet leaving its echo. Captain Ivor Hale entered quietly, the sound of his boots muffled by the rug. His expression was drawn, the fatigue of sleepless hours deepening the lines at his eyes.

He paused near the door, gaze lingering on Eleanor and the evidence arrayed before her. 'There's no going back to the way things were,' he said, voice low. 'The staff-everyone's talking in whispers. The hotel feels smaller. ' She nodded, understanding. His innocence was established beyond doubt, but the cost of suspicion had not vanished with it. The record had cleared him, but trust was harder to restore. Beatrice Quill appeared next, her composure newly fragile. She stood with her hands clasped, eyes flicking to the envelope and then away. 'They say the clocks will keep better time now,' she offered, her attempt at levity brittle as spun sugar. 'But I doubt the guests will forget what happened here.

Nor will the staff. ' Her voice caught, and Eleanor saw the exhaustion in the set of her shoulders-the price of weeks spent under silent scrutiny, the knowledge that her loyalty to the hotel had left her vulnerable to suspicion, however groundless. Beatrice's gaze lingered on the clock, then on Eleanor, before she retreated, leaving only the faint scent of her perfume behind. Sylvia Trent brought a different energy, her arrival heralded by the rustle of papers and the sharp clarity of her tone. 'I suppose this is the part where we all promise to learn something,' she said, arms folded beneath her coat. 'But what, exactly? That men will do anything to protect what's theirs?

That clocks can lie as easily as people? ' There was anger there, but also a reluctant sympathy-Eleanor caught it in the way Sylvia's eyes softened as she glanced at the desk, at the evidence of care and calculation that had nearly let the truth slip away. 'Dr. Finch deserved better,' Sylvia added, her words quiet. 'We all did. ' Eleanor did not argue. She thought of Dr. Mallory Finch as she had been in life-sharp, insistent, unwilling to be cowed by secrecy or threatened by power. The motive behind her death was no comfort: protection of reputation, the desperate wish to keep the hotel afloat, the fear that exposure would ruin not just Hugo Vane, but everyone who worked beneath his roof. In the end, there had been no clean victory, only a shifting of burdens. The confession had settled the facts, but it left the survivors with the more difficult work of living amid the wreckage.

A silence stretched between them, broken only by the wind and the faint tick of a smaller clock on the side table. Captain Ivor Hale lingered, as if unwilling to leave the room to its ghosts. Beatrice Quill pressed her gloves together, eyes shining with unshed tears. Sylvia Trent stared at the lamplight, her jaw set in grief and something like defiance. Eleanor closed the logbook with care, her hands steady, though the rest of her felt hollowed by the cost of knowing. In the end, justice had come, but it had not mended what was broken. Time in the Seacliff Hotel would move on, indifferent and unyielding, but those who remained would carry the memory-of Dr. Mallory Finch, of the hours stolen and restored, and of the truth that had come too late for comfort. Eleanor left the study last, her footsteps soundless on the thick carpet.

She paused at the door, her hand brushing the cold brass of the clock, and let herself imagine-for just a moment-that the hands might one day point to an hour untouched by violence or regret. Outside, the wintry wind keened along the eaves, and the darkness pressed close, but within the study, a single lamp burned on, holding back the night.
