## Chapter 7: Lies and Distractions

"The notes were in the top drawer." Montague Norbury laid a stack of correspondence on the polished table in the hotel lounge, the lamplight glinting off the crisp envelopes emblazoned with Katherine Quayle's initials. Desmond Kestrel, arms folded and face blank, watched the proceedings with a solicitor's practiced detachment, while Hector Gaunt inspected a brandy decanter on the sideboard, his reflection warped in the glass.

Neville Lachlan, cane balanced across his lap, traced his finger over the latest letter. "Plan and motive, by the look of it. This is Desmond's hand, and these are instructions-each one more desperate than the last." He shot Desmond an unreadable glance, then passed the letters to Bertram Vance.

Bertram, his manner exaggeratedly cheerful, fanned the notes with a flourish. "It seems our legal advisor is no stranger to intrigue. Letters to Hector, notes to himself, reminders about the will and the coastal cave... a veritable anthology of poor decisions. Wouldn't you agree, Gaunt?"

"No." Hector's answer was brief; he turned away, pouring a generous measure of brandy as Neville watched the liquid swirl in the glass.

Montague tapped a single sheet, annotated in red ink. "Desmond Kestrel's plan and motive are clear-he acted under Hector Gaunt's direction, hoping to delay the new will and preserve his own position." He flattened the sheet with a steady hand, then turned to the fencing foil resting against the chair.

Neville bent to examine it, his movements deliberate despite the old injury. "Fresh stains and handling marks, again matching Desmond's glove and grip. Neville traced the outline of each stain with the tip of his cane, as if scrubbing would erase the red marks that still clung stubbornly to the steel."

Desmond's expression remained neutral. "According to the records." He reached for the glove, brushing invisible lint from the cuff, his attention fixed not on the room but on the objects arrayed before him-evidence, artifacts, burdens.

Bertram tapped the blade, eyebrows raised in exaggerated surprise. "If you were so eager to help Hector regain the academy, why hide it? Or was that too great a risk-or not enough reward?"

Montague, eyes narrowed, pressed the point. "The notes speak for themselves. It's not the first time a man lied about money, but it's rarely so well documented."

Neville responded, tone mild but pointed. "That's a fair point."

Hector, swirling his brandy, fixed Montague with a cool stare. "If my only offence is creative accounting, I'll take my chances with the law." His words carried a practiced irony-the sort that might have charmed a jury, but here only drew a snort from Bertram.

Bertram, never one to let a rival off easily, leaned in. "You lied about the finances, Gaunt. Not the killing, perhaps, but the theft. Is there anything you'll admit to, besides bad luck?"

Hector's laugh was bitter and brief. "Fortune's never been a friend."

Neville, quietly, sorted the correspondence into two piles-one marked for official evidence, the other for private review. In the quiet, Bertram unfolded a racing slip from his waistcoat and tore it in half, the memory of a disastrous gambling loss flickering across his features before vanishing.

Montague turned to Desmond, voice softening. "Last month's courtroom defeat must have stung, Desmond. Yet you pressed on-why?" Still you soldiered on-was it loyalty to Hector, or something more?"

Desmond didn't answer, simply rolling the glove into a neat bundle and placing it beside the blade.

In the corner, a grandfather clock tolled the quarter hour. Bertram, idly spinning a coin on the tabletop, watched the shadows stretch across the room, his impatience barely contained.

Montague closed the folder of evidence, setting his cane against the table's edge. "The case is narrowing; the facts are insistent. We must revisit the window-tides, keys, and all." He gathered the documents, gesturing for the others to follow.

Neville stood, wincing as he shifted weight onto his damaged knee, gripping the cane for support. His eyes remained fixed on the foil. "If it's answers you want, we're due some before dawn."

Bertram patted his shoulder, a gesture more mocking than sympathetic. "The sea's patience is matched only by Norbury's. Let's not keep either waiting."

Outside, the wind rattled the shutters, the murmur of the hotel's guests a faint, sustaining background to the deeper silence of suspicion. The group moved as one, evidence in hand, toward the truth that waited in the echoing corridors beyond.

## Chapter 8: The Corridor of Truth

"It was possible, then." Neville Lachlan's voice, thin but resolute, echoed off the damp stone as the group huddled inside the tidal cave's entrance. Afternoon light, filtered green by kelp and water, cast wavering shadows across the slick rocks. Montague Norbury led the way, cane tapping, as Desmond Kestrel waited, hands in pockets, eyes fixed on the exposed window seven feet above.

Bertram Vance, notebook in hand, addressed Montague. "You believe the killer entered here, at low tide, at twenty minutes past three?"

"Yes." Desmond's answer was flat; Bertram made a note and looked up, eyebrow raised.

Montague gestured to the group and began the demonstration. "You'll see: at twenty minutes past three, the tide withdraws far enough to reveal this cave. The lower window, when unlatched with a duplicate key concealed in a fencing foil, grants access to the victim's room. The feat requires both timing and nerve."

Hector Gaunt, standing apart, watched Montague's progress with a curl of his lip. "Let's see if the facts support your theory, Norbury. I imagine we'll find the critical ten minutes between twenty minutes past three and half past three are all the murderer needed."

Neville steadied himself against the cave wall, shifting his weight as Montague, with some assistance, climbed the rocky ledge and demonstrated the key's use. The duplicate, now in police custody, fitted the lock perfectly-just as the hollow foil, recovered from Desmond's effects, had proved in earlier examination.

Desmond Kestrel offered no protest, his face impassive as Montague completed the reenactment. The group watched in silence, the only sound the slow drip of water and the distant crash of surf beyond the cave mouth.

Bertram, observing the process with a mix of awe and disdain, launched into a speech. "So, we see it all: the solicitor's blueprints, the duplicate key, the foil itself as both weapon and entry tool. The route is marked by time-twenty minutes past three, the low tide; half past three, the murder complete. All the cleverness in the world cannot erase a stain so carefully tracked by the clock. And now, Norbury, will you name the hand that wielded the blade?"

Neville broke in, voice barely above the waves. Neville gave a slow, reluctant nod, acknowledging the force of the deduction without another word.

Montague stood upright, cane in one hand, duplicate key in the other. "Desmond Kestrel killed Katherine Quayle-timing his entry by the tide, exploiting the window's mechanism, and leaving behind the only trail he could not erase: the truth of means and motive."

Desmond Kestrel, standing straight and composed, answered in his own words. "She found the trust papers-proof of what I'd done. She threatened to show Hector at the garden party tomorrow. I couldn't let her destroy him, or me, in front of everyone."

The cave seemed to contract around them, the old stones holding their secrets as the afternoon light faded to a dull gold. Outside, the ordinary sounds of the sea resumed-waves grinding over pebbles, gulls calling, the world indifferent to the sorrows and betrayals just proved. No one spoke for a long moment; even Bertram's pen stilled.

Montague set the key on the sill and stepped out into the sunlight. The shadow of Katherine Quayle lingered a moment longer in the open window above, then vanished into the returning tide.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"Let us have it plain," Montague Norbury declared, setting his cane beside the low table in the hotel lounge and letting it roll a measured inch before catching it with trembling fingers. Afternoon light spilled through the tall windows, casting patterns across the carpet and over the silent figure of Katherine Quayle, who lay at rest in the adjoining room-a presence more potent in memory now than in flesh. The group gathered around Norbury, each one holding some tangible piece: a hat, a letter, the cool stem of a glass, as if needing proof of their own continued life.

Desmond Kestrel, standing by the marble hearth, glanced in Norbury's direction. "Proceed." Montague reached for a decanter, pouring a careful measure into his glass, his mild tremor making the act deliberate rather than clumsy. The scent of brandy hovered in the air, anchoring the moment in ordinary ritual even as the extraordinary pressed upon them.

Neville Lachlan eased himself into an armchair, setting his cane against one knee and laying his palm flat across its polished wood. "Not every day one's reputation is restored by process of elimination," he said, his tone bandaged with wry humor. He watched the motes swirling in the angled light, his thoughts unreadable.

Hector Gaunt sat apart, hands folded together on the edge of Katherine's well-thumbed fencing manual, the cover softened by years of use. The memory of his public disgrace, recent and raw, clung to him-a thing shown each time he let his gaze drift to the lounge's grand mirror, yet never mentioned by any.

Bertram Vance drifted between the sideboard and the armchair, fingering a pack of playing cards with the restlessness of a man who has survived both fortune and suspicion. "Well, that's a fine mess," he remarked, flicking a card onto the table in a gesture that managed to be both resignation and polite derision. Desmond's lips twitched, the barest ghost of a deadpan smile.

Montague's hand lingered on the cut-glass decanter, steadying himself as he addressed the group. "We are gathered to clear the innocent, and name those whose only crime is misfortune." His voice carried the firmness of a final order delivered on a deck swept clear by storm. Hector glanced up, the line of his jaw set in habitual defiance.

"Your alibi stands, Gaunt," Montague announced. "From two fifty to three twenty five, not a minute unaccounted for. And the billiard room-witnessed by four, at least." Hector nodded, his hand tightening on the fencing manual before he released it, the pages splaying outward like the wings of a trapped bird. The book was swiftly gathered up by Neville and set back on the side table, the gesture both practical and respectful.

Bertram performed a neat shuffle with the cards. He looked to Montague, who inclined his head as he reached for his cane-always within grasp, always a reminder of what time and fortune erode.

Neville's gaze passed over the others, his fingers absently tracing a gouge in the armrest where Katherine once rested her hand during long council meetings. "The fencing hall was full-three o'clock to a quarter to four, too many eyes to slip away." He closed his own eyes for a moment, as if summoning the clamorous roll of students, the chorus of voices that stood, at last, as his defense.

Montague moved the decanter an inch, aligning it with the coaster. "Confirmed by all. You, Bertram-staff saw you in your rooms, your maintenance logs checked and checked again. Salt on the window latch, yes, but only what work required. No more." Bertram gave the faintest of bows, the cards still restless in his fingers.

Desmond, shoulders square, met Montague's gaze. "And the glove marks?" he asked, the cadence of a man who already knows the answer to the question. Montague nodded, offering the fencing foil's hilt for inspection.

"Lachlan's glove left its mark," Montague confirmed, "but evidence and alibi both exonerate him. The stain's timing falls before the murder, not after." Neville received the foil, running his thumb along the grip before setting it aside with a finality that brooked no argument.

No one spoke for a long moment. The hush was broken only by the faint click of Bertram's cards and the sound of the wind stirring the drapes. "Steady as she goes," Montague murmured, restoring the cane to its upright position and drawing himself to his full-if slight-height.

Hector finally looked up, voice low. "I owe the lot of you an apology. I made myself a target, and the suspicion was not unwarranted-if only for my past." His words fell heavy, but his posture eased minutely, as if the admission let in a draft of cleaner air.

Neville inclined his head, accepting both the apology and the moment's peace. "We all have our shadows, Gaunt. Some longer than others." He gripped his cane, shifting with the effort of old pain.

Desmond, arms folded, regarded the others. "My mistake was in thinking the law a shield. It never is." He turned to Hector, a glance that held the complicated gratitude of a man newly spared, if not entirely redeemed.

Bertram shuffled the cards one last time, setting the deck square and true. "If we're keeping score, I'd say I'm still down by several hands, but at least the stakes are honest." His smile was all polite savagery, the kind that takes a measure of comfort in having survived yet another round.

Montague, moving with the care of a man who has learned to ration his energy, reached for the fencing foil and set it upon the side table beside Katherine's manual. "She would have approved of precision, if not the outcome," he said quietly, the memory of Katherine Quayle's understated humor surfacing in the corners of his eyes.

The silence that followed was unbroken by argument, only by the ordinary sounds of the hotel: glass set upon wood, the ticking of the lobby clock, a chambermaid smoothing a cushion Katherine once favored. Outside, the sun's last rays gilded the water, and within, those who had been suspects resumed the patterns of their lives-changed, perhaps, but cleared.

## Chapter 10: The Revelation

A clatter of teacups broke the hush-the evening service beginning again, china and silver handled by staff who had wept their share and now, by necessity, resumed their rounds. Montague Norbury, seated at his accustomed place by the lounge window, accepted his cup from a trembling hand and set it carefully on the saucer before him, the steam rising in ribbons that blurred his reflection on the glass. The proof of Desmond Kestrel's guilt had been settled in the tidal cave, evidence gathered, keys catalogued, and now the world leaned back towards its customary shape.

Hector Gaunt stood at the mantel, running his fingertip along the edge of a tarnished medal, then picking it up and turning it over in his palm before setting it back in the row. "Not today," he said, in answer to Montague's unspoken question. Bertram Vance, who had just entered with a folded newspaper, shrugged and moved to the bar, his fingers drumming an idle pattern against the polished wood. Montague observed both men, noting the changes wrought in a day: Hector, straight-backed once more, had already arranged for the return of certain academy ledgers, a gesture intended for the public eye if not for his own private exoneration; Montague himself had sent for his medical bag, promising to resume evening lectures for the junior staff-an old habit, quietly resumed.

Neville Lachlan adjusted a padded chair near the window, his knee braced but uncomplaining, and surveyed the room with a look that was nearly satisfaction. "It seems we've returned to order," he remarked, hands steadying a battered notebook as he settled into the routine of marking attendance for tomorrow's lessons.

Bertram leaned against the bar, addressed Hector. "Well, you've dodged more than one bullet, but I imagine the echo will linger. Are you planning any grand apologies for the academy, or just a brief note to the papers?" His tone was flinty, but the smile that accompanied it was less barbed than before.

Hector's reply was short. "I'll draft something suitable." Montague flicked a crumb from his cuff, his attention moving to the gilt clock on the sideboard as its hands drifted toward the hour.

Neville, watching the tea tray's slow progress across the carpet, spoke up. "For what it's worth, I'm grateful for the clarity-there are few things more corrosive than suspicion among colleagues. I'd like to think we can return to something resembling peace, even if the shape of it won't be quite the same." His words stretched out, careful and well-measured, as he folded his notebook shut and placed it atop his lap with finality.

Bertram, voice even, retorted, "Peace is as peace does," then turned to pour himself a drink.

One by one, the suspects cleared by the arrest of Desmond Kestrel found their words. Montague accepted a faint thank you from Neville, who offered it with a firm handshake, the weight of years and shared hardship behind it. Hector, facing Bertram, spoke quietly, his hands folded before him. 'I brought trouble here,' he said. 'The unrest-some of it is my fault.' Bertram, for his part, owned up to his resentment, naming it as the product of long exclusion and briskly forgiving the rest with the careless grace of a gambler who has learned to cut his losses. Inspector Sefton's signature still glistened on the stack of release papers folded on the table-each suspect's name listed, charges struck through, the official stamp pressed deep into the parchment.

Montague himself felt the mild tremor return as he lifted the teacup, the porcelain rattling briefly against the saucer. He steadied it with a second hand, remembering-as the steam rose, ordinary and comforting-the time Katherine Quayle had stood in the same spot, quietly correcting a student's grip on a foil while offering, in her understated way, a lesson in both fencing and restraint. The memory bore no connection to her death, only to the constancy of her influence.

The evening stretched out, soft-voiced, as the lounge filled with the ambient sounds of conversation, clinking glass, and the occasional burst of laughter from a far table. Yet the air held a charge-a note of unease that wouldn't quite dissolve, as if the echo of violence lingered just below the surface. Still, the lounge began to fill again: chairs scraped, voices rose and lowered, and the ordinary habits of the day crept back to life, fragile and incomplete. Outside, the tide crept back, covering the cave and smoothing the rocks, indifferent to the secrets once carried in on its back.
