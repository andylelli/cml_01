This is much weaker than the tidal-cave locked-room draft. It has a promising core — a strangling, a forged post-mortem letter, a duplicate key, ladder dust, and an impersonation at the dig site — but it reads less like a polished story and more like assembled mystery components. The central solution is there, but the plot is over-repetitive, several scenes contradict or duplicate themselves, and the final proof is too cluttered.

I’d score it around 69/100.

Investigator: Inspector Gerald Harcourt
Victim: Dr. Beatrice Langley

What works

The best idea is the forged letter. The ink was not available before 11:30, so if Langley supposedly signed the letter after that, the letter must be false because she was already dead. That is a classic fair-play clue and one of the strongest pieces in the draft.

The lock clue is also promising. The duplicate key shifts the bolt but does not fully engage it, meaning the apparent locked-room condition can be manipulated. That gives the case a physical mechanism instead of relying only on testimony.

The ladder dust comparison has a clear “aha”: the dust shows a single climber, not the confusing overlapping pattern first suggested, and Pike’s shoe matches the print exactly. That is the best physical proof against Pike.

Main issue: there are too many mechanisms

The solution seems to require all of this:

Pike impersonated Charles Wentworth.
Pike used a forged excavation pass.
Pike climbed at the dig site.
Pike used the duplicate study key.
Pike strangled Langley with the scarf.
Pike forged a letter after death.
Pike manipulated the apparent timeline.
Pike left a fresh smudge on the scarf.

That is too much. The reader can follow it, but it feels busy rather than elegant.

Choose one main deception and make the rest support it:

Main deception:
Pike made it look as if Charles Wentworth had been at the dig site/study at the critical time.

Supporting proof:
The ladder dust does not match Charles.
The forged pass belongs to Pike.
The duplicate key belongs to Pike.
The forged letter was written after Langley died.

The scarf should probably just be the murder weapon, not a separate mini-mystery in the stables.

The opening is overloaded

The first chapter gives us ligature marks, silk scarf, locked study, clock at 11:30, missing watch, ladder dust, overlapping shoes, Charles’s muddy boots, gambling debts, Margot’s notebook, Evelyn’s tray, Leonard’s ledger, and Sir Edmund’s estate anxiety.

That is too many clues at once. The best opening version would focus on three things:

1. Langley was strangled with the silk scarf.
2. The study appears locked.
3. The clock says 11:30, but the missing watch/footprints suggest the time is false.

Then introduce the ladder/dig-site material in Chapter 2.

The big contradiction: time of death is unclear

The draft keeps mentioning 10:10–10:45, 10:15–11:30, 11:30, and after half past eleven. The reveal says Pike’s murder window was 10:10 to 10:45, but the forged letter clue says the letter was introduced after 11:30. Both can work, but the timeline must be made explicit.

Use this:

10:10–10:45 — Pike impersonates Wentworth, enters the study with the duplicate key, and kills Langley.
11:30 — new ink arrives; Pike later uses it to forge a letter implying Langley was still alive.
After 11:30 — forged letter is introduced among Langley’s papers.

Then the deduction becomes:

The letter does not prove Langley lived until 11:30. It proves someone was tampering with her papers after she was dead.
Chapter 8 reveal is too list-like

The reveal gives the right answer: Leonard Pike killed Dr. Beatrice Langley. It identifies the forged pass, duplicate key, ladder dust, scarf, and forged letter.

But it reads like an evidence inventory. Make it more dramatic:

Harcourt placed Charles’s boot beside the print.
Too small.

He placed Pike’s shoe over the dust impression.
The sole disappeared into it.

“There was no second climber,” Harcourt said. “There was no Wentworth at the ladder. There was only Leonard Pike, wearing another man’s name.”

Then:

“The scarf killed her. The letter lied for you. The key let you leave the study seeming locked. But the dust kept your shape.”

That would give the reveal a proper “click.”

Character Life / Relationship Richness: 6/10

There are good fragments: Margot’s earlier dismissal, Evelyn’s insecurity in the household, Sir Edmund’s winters and estate stewardship, Charles’s poverty background, Pike’s reputation and account books, Langley’s insistence on order and truth.

But much of it is told rather than dramatized. For example, “memory of a humiliating dismissal” is mentioned repeatedly, but we do not really see how it shapes Evelyn beyond nervous efficiency. Sir Edmund’s “harsh winters” memory appears too often without changing the action.

The best human detail is Langley debating the provenance of a Roman brooch — that makes her feel like a scholar, not only a victim. Keep that.

Humour / Wit: 4/10

This is a big drop from the tidal-cave story. Most of the repeated humour is formulaic:

“Let’s cut to the chase.”
“That’s the way of things.”
“Numbers don’t lie, people do.”
“Order, Mr. Wentworth insists.”

They read like assigned catchphrases rather than organic wit. The best line is Pike’s:

“Numbers don’t lie, people do.”

That fits his accountant/auditor role, but it should appear once, not as a defining label.

Better character-specific wit:

Pike: “An honest ledger is a terrible enemy. It remembers what gentlemen forget.”
Margot: “If everyone here is innocent, Inspector, the dust has developed criminal tendencies.”
Evelyn: “In this house, even the keys have superiors.”

Those would raise the wit score quickly.

Chapter 10 has the right idea but too much scaffold

The aftermath does the right things: Pike is bound, the scarf is sealed, the accounts are closed, Margot’s career improves, Evelyn’s position steadies, Langley is remembered for scholarship and truth, and Ashford Manor returns to routine.

But it contains broken/overwritten lines:

“Sir Edmund spoke at length…”
quotation marks break inside his speech
“a gesture that carried nothing the plot needed”
“the survivors changed in concrete ways”
“His reputation restored…”

Those are scaffold notes, not fiction. The line “a gesture that carried nothing the plot needed” must be deleted — it is explicitly meta-text.

Biggest prose/scaffold problems

These need immediate cleanup:

duplicated lines: “His finger traced the pale powder…” appears twice.
duplicated speech: “The study is the heart of Ashford Manor’s order…” repeats.
repeated “Inspector, [clue]…” lines that sound like notes rather than dialogue.
“Dr. Langley’s alibi is confirmed” — she is the victim, so this is wrong.
“a gesture that carried nothing the plot needed” — remove immediately.
“the survivors changed in concrete ways” — outline language, not prose.

Fastest fixes
Simplify the solution: Pike impersonated Wentworth; dust/pass/key prove Pike; forged letter extends false timeline.
Make the timeline explicit: 10:10–10:45 murder, 11:30 ink arrival, post-11:30 forged letter.
Cut the silk scarf stables subplot down by 70%; keep it as weapon evidence only.
Remove all duplicated lines and meta-scaffold text.
Rewrite Chapter 8 reveal around the shoe/dust test.
Stop giving every suspect long alibi speeches.
Give Langley one or two more scholar-life details earlier.
Replace repeated catchphrases with sharper character-specific wit.
Overall

This has a workable mystery underneath: post-mortem forged letter + duplicate key + ladder dust exposing Pike’s impersonation. But it is currently buried under repetition, extra mechanisms, duplicated passages, and scaffold language.

As written: 69/100.

With a simplified clue chain, clean timeline, sharper reveal scene, and heavy prose cleanup, it could reach 80–83/100.
