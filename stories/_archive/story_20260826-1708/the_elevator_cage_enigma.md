## Chapter 7: Secrets Beneath Secrets

Sylvia Trent's heels clicked sharply against the cracked linoleum as she swept into the Service corridor and back stairwell, the dusk outside pressing its blue-grey chill through the half-open window. The corridor was thick with the smell of old soap and machine oil, and the only sound besides Sylvia's footsteps was the distant hum of the elevator machinery-a mechanical heartbeat muffled by layers of dust and time. Eleanor Voss followed, her own steps measured, the echo of the day's last sunlight flickering along the painted walls. The hush that had settled over the lounge earlier clung to them both, broken only by the faint rattle of wind against the glass. Eleanor paused just beyond the stairwell, letting her eyes adjust to the dimness. The corridor's shadows seemed to stretch and deepen as the evening settled in, swallowing the last of the summery light.

She caught the faint scent of something acrid-grease, perhaps, or the residue of a long-neglected cleaning. The silence was not complete; the soft creak of pipes and the distant clatter of crockery from the kitchens below gave the place a living, restless quality. "I wonder," Eleanor said, her voice low, "whether you might help me with something. The staff recall you asking after the elevator's workings a few days ago-quite detailed questions, I'm told. Her laughter, usually so quick and bright, faltered. "Curiosity is a terrible habit," Sylvia replied, her tone rapid-fire but uneven. "I was bored, that's all. The night porter was so dreadfully slow, and I thought I might amuse myself with a little engineering. Heaven knows there's nothing else to do in this mausoleum. The puzzle's shape had changed, and with it, Eleanor's understanding of the night Dr.

Mallory Finch died. A faint echo of laughter drifted from the lounge, a reminder that the rest of the hotel still clung to the comfort of easy answers. Here, in the deepening shadows of the service corridor, Eleanor felt the old certainty slip away. ## Chapter 8: The Pattern Emerges

"The wind's dropped at last," Eleanor murmured, setting her notebook on the narrow writing desk by the window. The air in her room still carried the salt and damp of the storm that had worked at The Selwyn Hotel all day. Night pressed in, the last clouds scattered, and nothing broke the hush but the crash of waves far below. Moonlight came through the open transom and moved slowly across the wallpaper. She drew the curtains partway and let the pale glow settle on her papers. Her hand went to the locket at her throat, thumb finding the worn edge of the clasp out of long habit. Inside was a faded photograph: Dr. Mallory Finch, smiling in a garden long since dug under, her arm around a much younger Eleanor. Eleanor closed it again without looking longer than she had to and turned to her work. The desk was a chaos of diagrams, timelines, and notes.

Her notebook, its cover smudged from hours of handling, bore sketches of the elevator's brass gate, and beside one of them she had written the measurement twice, as though she doubted her own hand: the scratch sat exactly half an inch above the bottom latch. She had drawn the main corridors, the service passages, the suite where Dr. Finch had died. Every margin was crowded with questions. The bell had sounded at half past nine. The indicator had not budged from ground floor. No one had seen the cage move. She turned to a fresh page and set out a column of names. Captain Hale had been in the sunken cocktail lounge; the bartender remembered pouring for him, and two guests at the same table put him there for the whole forty minutes without prompting.

Beatrice Quill had been in the kitchens, and two of the staff had described her presence throughout the interval before Eleanor had finished asking. Hugo Vane had spent the window in the lobby giving a statement to three reporters, none of whom had let him out of sight, and all three had said so with the weary precision of men who take notes for a living. Eleanor had pressed each account for a gap and found none. She looked at what was left on the page. Sylvia Trent. Eleanor wrote the name slowly. She remembered the laughter-too bright, pitched a shade above the room-and the way Sylvia's hands worked at each other when she was not using them. The night porter placed her in the lobby from a quarter past nine and could say nothing at all about the hour before it. There was the print in the service corridor dust, narrow and high-heeled.

There were the staff whispers about her questions concerning the elevator: how the bell was wired, what the indicator did, whether anyone watched it. And there were the papers she had clutched to her chest, Dr. Finch's signature showing for a heartbeat before she turned them away. Eleanor set down her pen. The pieces sat closer together than she liked. The wardrobe creaked as the wind shifted. Eleanor pressed her palms flat on the desk and felt the cool of the wood. She had always prided herself on seeing what others missed and on refusing to leap; the habit had served her thirty years. It was no help to her now. The bell, the indicator, the scratch, the print-each on its own was a curiosity, and she could imagine an advocate explaining any one of them away. Together they would still not compel a confession. She needed something that could not be talked past.

Her gaze returned to the diagrams. A person who understood the mechanism could ring the bell from below, leave the indicator sitting at ground floor, and never move the cage an inch. Knowledge of that kind was not common, but it was not secret either. Still she hesitated. She thought of Mallory's trust, and of the locket, and of what it would cost to be wrong in front of a room. Discretion was the only reputation she had, and it was also the last thread that tied her to her friend. A knock startled her upright. Beatrice Quill's voice came muffled through the door. "If you're plotting to stay up all night, Miss Voss, do let me know so I can warn the staff. The kitchen's out of cocoa, and the night porter's nerves are quite undone. " A pause. Then, lower: "We're all counting on you, you know. " The footsteps went away down the corridor, and the words stayed behind.

Eleanor allowed herself a wry smile. Even now Beatrice's dryness was a kind of balm. It did not last. She bent back over the notebook under the single lamp and considered what she actually had: three accounts she could not shake, one woman who could not account for herself, and a mark in the dust. The mark was the only thing in the case that had a shape, and shapes could be laid against one another. A shoe, or the wrong shoe, in front of witnesses. She gathered her notes, squared the pages, and laid her pen beside the locket. The moonlight had shifted; half the desk was in shadow now. Tomorrow she would call them all together and put the question where no one could answer it with words. Tonight she permitted herself the doubt-that she had overlooked something, that her loyalty to Mallory had made her quick where she ought to have been slow. The hush deepened.

Eleanor sat on with her diagrams, and beneath the window the tide came in as it always had.

## Chapter 9: The Final Trap: The Discriminating Test
