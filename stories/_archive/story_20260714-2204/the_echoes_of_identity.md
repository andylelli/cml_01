## Chapter 9: Confronting Hugo

Midnight pressed its cold fingers against the windows, and only a sullen glow from the hearth flickered over velvet armchairs and the battered old music player standing sentinel nearby. The room's air, thick with the musk of wet wool and ashes, mingled with something sharper-brandy, perhaps, or the metallic tang of spent nerves. Beyond the glass, rain whispered against the panes, while inside, silence blanketed the guests, interrupted only by the occasional creak from the settling embers. Eleanor Voss rested near the fireplace, notebook balanced in her palm, the weight of revelation settling on her like a sodden coat. No longer a haven, the gathering space brimmed with a tension that seemed to make every shadow twitch. Circling slowly, Eleanor's eyes found Sylvia Trent by the mantel, sketchbook clasped against her chest so tightly her knuckles blanched.

The flare from the hearth picked out the minute tremor running through Sylvia's fingers. Eleanor revisited the timeline in her mind-the staff's testimonies confirmed Sylvia's whereabouts. The maid and several guests had seen Sylvia in her room, absorbed in her sketches, at the time of Dr. Finch's death; no chance she could have slipped away unnoticed. The clarity of the evidence left no room for doubt: Sylvia Trent was cleared. Nearby, the captain stood at the drinks cabinet, his neatly folded uniform jacket slung over a chair's back. Beatrice Quill glided over, voice subdued. "You were with us in the billiards room at the time, weren't you? " Hale gave a brief nod, glancing at the clock above the mantelpiece. "I was. Several of you saw me there. I didn't leave until after the music started. " Beatrice said, "Yes, and Hugo was with us as well.

We all heard the music begin at ten minutes past eight. " The cluster of recollections fit together with precise, mournful certainty. Eleanor noted to herself: the captain's movements were accounted for elsewhere; he was beyond suspicion. With Sylvia Trent no longer under scrutiny, the circle of suspicion tightened to a single figure. That knowledge landed like a stone in Eleanor's chest-no more evasions, no comforting ambiguities. The truth had taken on a chilling clarity. Hugo Vane sat apart, hunched and rigid, his silhouette thrown askew by the dull orange pulse from the hearth. The sharp creases of his suit had softened, his once-steady grip now winding a handkerchief between restless fingers. A draft seeped along the floorboards, carrying the faint scent of damp earth and extinguished smoke. Within these four walls, all action and consequence narrowed to this reckoning.

Eleanor steadied herself, voice low and unflinching, each word delivered with the precision of a surgeon. "Mr. Vane. " The brittle sound startled even her, slicing the charged hush. "You know why we're here. The evidence, the contradictions-they all point to you. Tell us the truth. " Her words lingered, heavy and irrevocable. The others-Hale, Beatrice, Sylvia-sat in taut silence, faces limned by the hearth's dying glow, each waiting for the inevitable. Hugo flicked his gaze towards Eleanor, then away, settling on the music player as if hoping for rescue from its silent shell. He drew a shallow breath, knuckles whitening around the abused handkerchief. "You think you know everything," he rasped, "but you don't know what it's like. The war, the debts, the pressure-Dr. Finch threatened to ruin me. She would have seen the hotel lost, the staff out on the street.

I tried to reason with her, but she wouldn't listen. " Eleanor advanced, shadow stretching over the worn rug. "You received a note-her hand, her words-summoning you at ten minutes past eight. The music player was set to begin at that precise moment. Witnesses heard the music, loud enough to conceal an argument and a raised voice. The door was locked from inside, but you alone were expected. Your alibi for later is ironclad, but for that window-only your own testimony exists. " Muscles corded along Hugo's jaw, composure slipping as his posture stiffened. "I went to her room as she asked. She was already angry-accusing me of stealing, of lying. I begged her to reconsider, to give me time. She wouldn't. She threatened to expose me to the board, to the papers.

I-I lost control. " His voice broke, and for a heartbeat, the entire space seemed to contract, the hearth's light shrinking to a trembling filament. "You killed her," Eleanor stated, tone stripped of judgment and pity alike-just the cold impact of fact. "You strangled Dr. Finch while the music played, knowing it would mask her cries. You locked the door behind you, left her there, and waited for the record to end before alerting anyone. The note, the timing, the volume-they formed your alibi, but they revealed your guilt. " In the hush that followed, the wind's lonely wail seeped into every alcove. Hugo's frame slumped, surrender draining the last rigidity from his shoulders. "I killed her," he whispered, the confession barely more than a sigh. "I did it for the hotel-for the staff, for myself. The debts were crushing me.

I thought if I could just buy time-just a little more time-no one would have to know. But she-she wouldn't bend. " A small, strangled sound escaped Beatrice Quill as she pressed her hands over her mouth. Sylvia Trent's eyes shimmered with unshed tears, composure unraveling as the magnitude of Hugo's words settled over them all. The captain's jaw set hard, but he stared fixedly into the coals. What once had been a haven of laughter and melody now carried the chill of a crypt. As Hugo sat motionless, Eleanor watched for a flicker of relief, remorse, or even anger. Only exhaustion remained. "There were other paths," she said quietly. "You could have asked for help. " His head moved in a slow, bitter arc. "Pride, Miss Voss. Pride and fear. They're as deadly as any weapon. I never meant for it to end like this. " Footsteps sounded in the hall-the officer summoned earlier by Eleanor drawing near. A heavy quiet descended. Beatrice wept in muffled fits, her composure crumbling into grief. Sylvia stood by the misted window, forehead pressed to the cold pane, arms wrapped around herself for warmth. Hale poured a measure of whisky, hands steady but movements leaden, as if every gesture cost him dearly. The ache of exhaustion crept into Eleanor's very bones. The truth, at last laid bare, brought no comfort-only the empty ache of loss and the knowledge that justice, such as it was, rarely mends what's broken.

Eleanor often remained in the common room, staring into the fragile embers. Dr. Finch-unyielding, formidable-lingered in her memory, as did Hugo Vane, brought down by despair and pride. Though the case had ended, the echoes persisted, as relentless as the winter wind sighing at the windows. Relief glimmered briefly as the flames caught a new log, warmth pulsing into the chilly air.

For an instant, Eleanor permitted herself to believe that healing might come with time. She closed her notebook, pages weighted with sorrow and truth alike, rose, and drifted across the worn carpet to join the others, even as the first wan light of morning crept into the corners of the hotel's gathering room.
