Agatha gathered the keys, her fingers trembling just enough to betray her age-or something else-before she composed herself. She turned away, her shadow stretching long across the library's patterned rug.

A silence settled, the weight of authority, of opportunity, of secrets too long kept. Edith watched Agatha's retreat, her own thoughts a tangle of suspicion and inevitability. The night was lengthening, and with it, the sense that the truth was drawing near, inexorable as the tolling of the manor's bells.

---

## Chapter 8: The Final Trap

"Isn't that just delightful?" Violet Radcliffe's words echoed through the main hall, her tone dancing with sardonic amusement as she surveyed the tableau before her. The late evening air was taut, expectation humming in the spaces between the assembled company: Edith Penhale, Agatha Innes, Millicent Ashcombe-and, in spirit, Leonard Nettleship, whose absence pressed upon the room with a weight all its own.

Edith produced the battered pocket watch and the manor hall clock's logbook, setting both on the crimson velvet runner atop the oak table. Edith's voice was even, but her hands betrayed a hint of tension as she set the clocks side by side. "Let us compare them directly," she said, inviting the others to look for themselves.

Millicent, her baton gripped tightly, glanced at Agatha. "And if they do not, Miss Penhale?"

Agatha's reply was flat. "Then we must accept the official record."

Violet moved closer, her reflection doubled in the glass of the clock face. "Official records are so rarely wrong-except, I suppose, when they are composed by the living and contradicted by the dead."

Edith arranged the watch and clock side by side, gesturing for all to observe. The pocket watch, hands unmoving, marked quarter past seven in the evening. The manor hall clock, untouched since the morning, insisted stubbornly on half past seven in the evening. Millicent's eyes narrowed as she leaned in, tracing the hands of each clock. She stiffened, lips drawing into a thin line. "Fifteen minutes apart," she murmured, her finger hovering between the dials. "What are we to make of that?"

Millicent's short retort broke the hush. "I would wager my position in the school that someone here knows exactly how to advance a clock fifteen minutes and leave no trace."

Violet's voice was low, edged with irony. "Perhaps the clock simply wished to be in two places at once. A talent some of us might envy."

Edith lifted the logbook, displaying the entry in Agatha's hand-correction to the winding time, ink still fresh. "The choir school ledger, Rector. Your handwriting corrects the original time. You alone have the keys, the authority, and access."

Agatha's lips pressed together, a fine tremor at the corner of her mouth betraying her control. "I acted in the interests of stability, Miss Penhale."

Leonard's ghostly earnestness hovered in the air-a remembered speech on the sanctity of the conductor's baton, the necessity of trust, and the folly of placing faith in anything but the music itself.

Violet's gaze lingered on Agatha, her expression unreadable. "Stability, it seems, is a matter of perspective."

Edith's eyes moved between the clocks, the ledger, and the assembled faces. The test was complete-the evidence irrefutable. The time was no longer in question, but the hand that had set it so.

Millicent let out a slow breath, baton lowering to her side. "There is only one person in this room who could have orchestrated such a performance."

Agatha's gaze flicked to Edith, her authority faltering for the first time. The hall was silent, the test concluded, and the truth-discreet, undeniable-began to settle, as implacable as the cold tick of a wound clock.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"Not beyond reason," Edith Penhale said, the words falling quietly in the drawing room, where the hush was thick as velvet. The drawing room seemed to constrict, every eye turning as Edith's words fell. Millicent's hand stilled. Violet's breathing caught. Millicent Ashcombe and Violet Radcliffe sat apart, their posture speaking of exhaustion and something like relief.

Millicent looked up, baton resting across her knees. "So, Miss Penhale-who is to be believed?"

Edith's answer was flat. "The records, and what they cannot explain."

Violet's retort slipped out, twenty-five words, tone sardonic and brittle. "If only the world operated on the principle of elimination, Miss Penhale, I imagine I would feel much lighter. But I have not found it so, in practice or in inheritance."

Millicent's reply was six words: "Some inheritances are more cursed, Violet."

Edith opened the practice room ledger, turning to the page where Leonard Nettleship's neat script placed Millicent in the practice room from seven o'clock to a quarter to eight. "You were seen, Miss Ashcombe. Multiple students and the choirmaster-Leonard himself-confirmed your presence. The timeline is unbroken."

She produced the ledger samples, setting them in front of Violet. "And your hand, Miss Radcliffe, is unmistakable. The secretary's office roster, the correspondence-every entry corroborates your presence there. The inconsistencies are errors of fatigue, not deception."

Violet's eyes softened, her posture relaxing as she folded the papers. "For once, I am grateful for my own untidy scrawl."

Millicent smoothed a wrinkle from her skirt, baton steady in her hand. "Then who is left, Miss Penhale? Who had the opportunity, the means, and the motive?"

Edith's gaze settled on the choir school ledger, the correction in Agatha Innes's hand, and the clock key. "The only person who could advance the manor hall clock, control the records, and assert the official time was Agatha Innes. She orchestrated the deception-deliberately fixing the time of death at half past seven in the evening, relying on the tampered clock and her unique authority."

A silence fell, the room holding its breath. Leonard's spirit, if it lingered, was not in accusation but in memory-a gentle hand on Millicent's shoulder, a smile for Violet, a fondness for order none but Edith had truly understood.

Violet, quietly, picked up a letter, her thumb brushing the embossed crest. She did not look at the others, her gaze dropping to the words that had once spelled financial ruin for her family-nothing to do with murder, everything to do with survival.

Edith closed her journal, her pen tapping once against the wood. "The innocent are cleared. The rest is a matter for confession."

Millicent stood, baton in hand, no longer trembling. "I should have trusted the music, not the time."

In the quiet that followed, the truth settled into the room-not with drama, but with the certainty of clocks that would, finally, keep honest time.

## Chapter 10: The Rector's Unimpeachable Word

"How utterly... enlightening," Millicent Ashcombe murmured, as the last of the police constables filed from the rector's study. The night pressed close against the windows, the lamplight drawing long shadows across the desk where Edith Penhale now sat, her back never quite to the door.

A visible head wound, dark and unmistakable, marked the place on the carpet where Leonard Nettleship had fallen. The heavy candlestick, its base bloodied, lay beside the now-empty shroud-a relic of violence, stripped of all solemnity. Edith watched as an officer lifted it with gloved hands, the weight of the metal a small echo of the authority that had wielded it.

Agatha Innes sat in her chair, her composure unraveling, hands folded tightly in her lap. She did not look at Edith, nor at the candlestick, but at the clock on the mantel-its hands now set, at last, to the true time.

Edith's voice was steady. "The truth, Rector, cannot be set ahead or behind by decree."

Agatha's reply was flat. "It is finished."

Violet Radcliffe entered quietly, her steps careful on the parquet. She set a sheaf of unopened letters on the desk-her debts, now to be settled in public, not secrecy. "It is over, Miss Penhale, or as over as these things ever are."

Millicent Ashcombe, standing by the window, slipped her baton into her music case. Her eyes, for once, were free of calculation. "I will not return to the practice room tonight," she said. "There will be no need."

Leonard Nettleship's memory lingered not in the wound, nor in the weapon, but in a faded photograph on the bookcase shelf-him as a boy, violin in hand, a father's hand resting on his shoulder. The music that had shaped him was older than scandal, untouched by the hour of his death.

Edith gathered her journal, tucking the pen into its spine. She had already begun the letter to Leonard's niece, the words precise: "The truth is established. The innocent are cleared. The house will recover."

Agatha, hands trembling, reached for her keys, setting them on the desk with a finality that needed no explanation. She did not speak again.

The rector's study, stripped of its authority, began to resume its ordinary use-papers stacked, ledgers locked away, the clock quietly marking the honest hour. Edith Penhale rose, glancing once at the door, never turning her back to it, and stepped into the corridor where the hush of night was broken only by the faint, regular ticking of time, restored.
