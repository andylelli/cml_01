## Chapter 10: What Remains in the Painted Shadow

"She's gone, then," Margaret Halloway said, her voice carrying through the hush that had settled over the Hotel lounge and foyer. The fog still pressed against the windowpanes, diffusing the lamplight into a pale, uncertain glow. Somewhere in the corridor, a door closed with a soft thud-a constable's departure, perhaps, or simply the wind shifting down the passage. The ledger, spectacles, and battered candlestick had been removed, leaving the table bare but for a single cup of cold tea. Eleanor Fairchild remained by the fire, her hands folded in her lap, the silence punctuated only by the faint, persistent tick of the mantel clock and the distant echo of a radio-Bing Crosby, faint as memory-drifting up from the lounge. The air in the foyer was thick with the scent of damp wool and polish, the aftermath of a spring night spent in quiet upheaval.

Isabel Thornton's absence was as palpable as her presence had been: the beaded handbag, the chain of her pince-nez glasses, now gone. The constable had taken her away not long before midnight, the words of her confession already a settled fact. No one spoke of it directly; the truth hung between them, too raw for immediate handling. "If you want the last of the logs, they're in the office," Agnes Fletcher offered, her voice steady but edged with fatigue. She gathered the stray napkins from the sideboard, her hands moving with the practiced care of someone who had weathered more than one crisis in this hotel. "I'll see the lounge is put to rights before morning, Miss Fairchild. Or as close as I ever manage." She paused, glancing at the empty hearth, then added, "I suppose I'll be polishing out candlestick marks for the rest of my days. At least it's honest work."

Eleanor Fairchild looked up, the firelight catching in her eyes. "Thank you, Agnes." She did not elaborate. Across the room, Dr. Henry Cavendish stood by the window, his left hand tucked into his pocket, the right lingering awkwardly at his side-an old mishap, perhaps, or a remnant of the night's strain. He watched the fog shift outside, the lines of his face drawn and thoughtful, but did not join the conversation. Thomas Merrick leaned against the doorframe, his cap in hand, boots leaving faint marks on the polished boards. He glanced at Margaret Halloway, then at Eleanor Fairchild, the tension in his posture replaced by something quieter-relief, perhaps, but also uncertainty. "So what happens now, Miss Fairchild?" he asked, his voice rough but without accusation. "The guests'll want to know if it's safe to stay. Folk talk, you know." "They always do," Eleanor Fairchild replied.

She rose, smoothing her skirt, and crossed to the window. The glass was cool beneath her hand, the condensation already beginning to reform where the police had wiped it away. Outside, the street was empty but for the faint glow of a lantern bobbing in the mist-Blakeney's routines resuming, even as the hotel's had been broken and reknit. Margaret Halloway set a tray of cups on the sideboard with more force than necessary, the clatter sharp in the quiet. "I'll be up before dawn," she said. "The guests will want breakfast, and the sheets won't change themselves. I suppose we'll have to see about the bookings-word gets out fast, and not just in Blakeney. I'll need to write to the agency, let them know the hotel's open as usual. Or as usual as it ever gets after a murder."

Her gaze lingered on the empty chair by the hearth, a flicker of something softer passing across her face, though she said nothing of the man whose plans had once threatened the hotel's future. Eleanor Fairchild watched her, noting the way Margaret's hands stilled on the tray-a rare moment of hesitation in a woman usually so brisk. The loss was not only of life, but of certainty; the old order, with all its tensions and alliances, had been unsettled. In the hush, Eleanor Fairchild remembered the way Charles Darnell used to recite poetry at breakfast-never quite matching the mood, always with the earnestness of a man expecting applause. She almost smiled, the memory as sharp and out of place as the bloodstain had been on the manuscript. Agnes Fletcher, gathering the last of the linen, paused beside Eleanor Fairchild.

"Strange, isn't it, how the place feels emptier now, even with everyone still here. I suppose that's what truth does-takes up more space than a lie ever could." She offered a self-deprecating chuckle, the sound gentle. "Well, I'll leave the philosophy to the guests. I've got enough trouble with stains as it is." The radio in the lounge shifted to a new tune-Gracie Fields, her voice bright and determined, cutting through the quiet with a note of springtime optimism. Thomas Merrick straightened, the sound drawing him back to the present. "Suppose we ought to get on, then. Tides wait for no one, not even the law." He touched his cap to Eleanor Fairchild and slipped out into the mist, his silhouette swallowed by the fog before the door had closed. Margaret Halloway lingered, her gaze sweeping the room as if memorizing its new arrangement.

"If you're staying, Miss Fairchild, I'll see you have a fresh room. The one you've been using-well, best not to sleep with the ghosts tonight." She did not wait for a reply, but her tone held a note of invitation, or perhaps forgiveness. The old rivalry, softened by shared ordeal, left space for something like camaraderie. Eleanor Fairchild remained by the window, watching as the fog shifted and the first pale hint of spring dawn pressed against the glass. The Painted Shadow Hotel would endure, she thought, but not unchanged. The truth had cost them all-Isabel Thornton her freedom, Margaret Halloway her certainty, Agnes Fletcher her peace, Thomas Merrick his innocence, and herself the comfort of distance. The ledger was closed, the candlestick gone, but the scars would linger, as persistent as the fog that crept in each night from the marshes.

She let her hand rest on the cool glass, the condensation reforming beneath her touch. Outside, a gull cried-a thin, plaintive sound, quickly lost in the hush. Within, the hotel's routines resumed: cups gathered, linen folded, the fire stoked for morning. The Painted Shadow Hotel, for all its secrets, had survived another night. Eleanor Fairchild turned from the window, the weight of what remained settling quietly into her bones. "Goodnight, Margaret," she said. "Goodnight, Agnes." The words, simple and final, carried the only certainty the spring night could offer.
