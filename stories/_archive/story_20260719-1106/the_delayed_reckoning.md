Anticipation thickened the room, underscored by the distant brine of the sea. Beatrice Quill went first, rolling up her sleeve with trembling hands to dab the oil on her forearm.

Her freckled skin remained untroubled as the minutes ticked by. Hugo Vane followed, movements brusque, wiping his hand on a handkerchief after. No reaction. Sylvia Trent, unfazed, applied the oil with measured grace, her wrist angled in the pale light. Still, her skin remained unmarked, cool beneath the steady morning glow. The alibi for Sylvia Trent was unshakeable-multiple witnesses had seen her in the lobby delivering the painting to Dr. Finch when the oil was last used. Hotel records confirmed it. Eleanor took in the evidence, her mind parsing the timeline, eliminating possibilities as methodically as a cryptographer testing ciphers. Reactions and records together made the truth plain. Sylvia was cleared: her presence in the lobby at the crucial time left no doubt. Beatrice's whereabouts in the conference room were corroborated by staff.

Hugo had been seen outside by both the gardener and the night porter. The test, the timeline, the witnesses-all converged on the same conclusion. Only Captain Ivor Hale, already in custody, had shown sensitivity when the oil was tested earlier. Eleanor recalled her own hesitation as she watched her: the faint mottled rash blooming on her arm, echoing Dr. Finch's reaction. The evidence was conclusive-opportunity, knowledge, and the unmistakable rash. The others, now exonerated, stared at the door through which Hale had vanished, their relief shadowed by regret and something darker. Hugo Vane broke the silence from his station at the fireplace, jacket grazing marble. 'So that's it, then. All those hours, all that suspicion-and in the end, it was Captain Ivor Hale all along. ' His gaze flickered toward Eleanor, seeking confirmation.

His expression, however, was resolute, touched with fatigue. 'You saw what the rest of us wouldn't. ' 'I saw what was there, Hugo. The rest was listening-to what no one said. ' Eleanor stepped to the center of the room, the thick rug muffling her movements. Accusation had left the air heavy, but beneath it ran sorrow and a thin current of relief. She glanced at the bottle, its label stark in the growing light. 'He confessed because the evidence allowed nothing else. The rash, the oil, the note about the rare plant-each piece pointed to him. But it's the motive that haunts me. Not greed, not hatred-only a desperate wish to protect what he thought was his. ' A quiet sound drew her attention: Beatrice stood near the doorway, hands tightly clasped, exhaustion carved deep into her features but her eyes clear. 'I never thought he...' She hesitated. 'He always seemed so steady.

I suppose we all have our breaking points. ' She looked to Eleanor. 'What happens now? To the hotel, and the rest of us? ' The question lingered, uncomfortably sincere. Sylvia Trent, her composure now a careful shield, stepped forward, scarf a pale streak against her dark suit. 'The Oceanic Hotel will survive,' she said, though her tone was brittle. 'It has weathered war and rationing, and it will weather this. But there will be questions-about Captain Ivor Hale, about Dr. Finch, about all of us. ' Meeting Eleanor's eyes, challenge softened by gratitude, she added, 'You did what was necessary. Still, I wonder if any of us will ever truly feel safe here again. ' The weight of the day settled on Eleanor. She thought of Dr. Finch-her intellect, her ambition, and her secrets. She thought, too, of Ivor and his confession: 'I killed Dr. Finch. I poisoned her with the oil.

She was destroying what little reputation I had left, undermining me at every turn. ' The words had been stripped of bravado. In that moment, Eleanor had seen not a villain, but a man undone by fear and pride. A sardonic laugh from Hugo Vane broke the spell. 'Funny, isn't it? All that talk of loyalty and tradition, and in the end, it was desperation that did him in. ' He turned to Beatrice, his expression unusually gentle. 'You'll be all right, Quill. You're stronger than you think. ' Beatrice managed a wan smile, tension easing from her stance. From the radio in the corner, the clipped tones of a newsreader spoke of rationing and reports from the capital. The outside world kept its pace, untroubled by secrets and loss. The familiar drone was oddly comforting; life would continue-guests would come and go, the sea battering the shore, the hotel enduring, if changed.

Sylvia crossed to the window and drew back the curtain, letting in a shaft of sunlight. 'We'll have to answer for what happened here,' she said. 'But perhaps now, at least, we can begin again-without secrets, or at least without this one. ' A question lingered in her eyes as she looked to Eleanor. 'Will you stay on, Miss Voss? The hotel could use someone with your... talents. ' Considering the offer, Eleanor allowed herself a faint, rueful smile. 'Perhaps for a little while. I find I have a fondness for the sea-though not, I hope, for any more mysteries. ' Her gaze lingered on each of them in turn. 'We've all been marked by this, in ways we may not yet understand. But we endure. That is the lesson, I think. ' A quieter, steadier silence returned. Beatrice straightened, voice regaining strength. 'I'll see to the guests. They'll need reassurance. ' She moved toward the door, step purposeful.

Hugo Vane followed, cynicism softened by respect. Sylvia lingered by the window, lost in her own thoughts. Eleanor lingered at the edge of the lounge, eyes drifting to the table where the bottle rested-a final, silent witness. She remembered Ivor's last words: 'I killed Dr. Finch. I poisoned her with the oil. ' The motive-jealousy over Dr. Finch's reputation-was old as ambition and as tragic. What she felt was not triumph, but a deep, aching regret for all that had been lost. Sunlight shifted across the room, warming the lounge by degrees. Outside, the sea glimmered under the pale sky, restless yet bright. Eleanor drew a steadying breath, the scent of salt and distant flowers threading through the air. She turned from the window and joined the others. For all its cruelty, the world offered a hope of renewal, and she chose, in that quiet moment, to trust in it.

The bottle on the table caught the sunlight for an instant before shadow returned. The lounge was still, save for the soft radio and the distant rush of the tide. At the threshold, Eleanor paused, her hand on the doorframe, allowing the silence to settle-a promise of gentler days ahead.
