Eleanor's gaze flicked to Sylvia, who had risen from her seat earlier in the evening, ostensibly to fetch a letter from her room.

On her way back, she had paused near the clock, her gloved hand brushing the case in a gesture that lingered a beat too long to be nothing. Eleanor let her gaze drift around the room, taking in the small details: the way Captain Ivor Hale's jaw worked as he stared into his glass, the way Dr. Finch's composure frayed at the edges, the way Hugo Vane's sardonic smile faded when he caught Sylvia's eye. The spring evening pressed in, the rain still whispering against the glass.

The clock, the smudge on the wood, the pendulum sitting crooked behind its glass-Eleanor turned each one over in her mind, and each one seemed to point the same way. As the meal drew to a close, Eleanor excused herself from the table and drifted toward the sideboard. She paused before the clock, her fingers hovering just above the glass. The smudge was faint but there, and the brass pendulum, when she leaned close, showed the marks of a hand that had recently touched it. Behind her, the conversation resumed in uneasy fits and starts, but Eleanor heard the tremor under Sylvia's laughter, the wit gone brittle at the edges. Eleanor returned to her seat, her mind racing. The room's silence after Sylvia's remark about Beatrice had been absolute, the kind that follows old wounds rather than new ones.

The rivalry between the two women had not died with Beatrice; if anything it had sharpened, grown more dangerous now that its object could no longer answer back. Eleanor felt that silence settle on her shoulders, heavier than any accusation spoken aloud. As the guests began to disperse, Eleanor caught Sylvia's eye across the table. For a moment, something unguarded flickered in Sylvia's expression-fear, perhaps, or regret. Then it was gone, replaced by her usual poise. 'Miss Voss,' Sylvia said, her voice low and even, 'I do hope you're not letting old stories cloud your judgment.

The past has a way of making everything seem more complicated than it is. ' Eleanor offered a knowing smile, raising her eyebrows in silent reply. 'You'd be surprised, Miss Trent, how often the past is the only thing that makes sense. ' She watched Sylvia turn away, composure unbroken, though her hands trembled just slightly as she gathered her things. The spring night deepened outside, the rain easing to a steady patter. Eleanor lingered in the dining area, her gaze moving between the battered clock and the empty seat at the table. The theory she had carried into dinner no longer sat as easily as it had. In its place, something else was taking shape, a suspicion that pointed not just to opportunity but to motive and means both. Beneath the evening's polite talk lay resentment enough, she thought, for more than one old story to still be bleeding.

## Chapter 7: The Pattern Emerges

Dr. Mallory Finch's footsteps echoed across the marble as she entered the lobby of the seaside hotel, her white coat trailing behind her in the dim glow of the overhead sconces. The night outside pressed cold and damp against the tall windows, the last patter of rain tracing silent rivulets down the glass. Eleanor Voss stood at the heart of the lobby, her hands folded around her notebook, gaze fixed on the battered clock above the mantel. A faint scent of extinguished fire lingered, mingling with the sharper tang of brine that crept in each time the wind rattled the doors. The hour was late, the hush inside broken only by the distant clink of glassware from the bar and the low, urgent voices of guests who dared not linger in the open. Shadows pooled in the corners, stretching long across the faded rug, while lamplight flickered over the brass face of the clock.

Eleanor's eyes did not leave it. The hands remained, as ever, at twenty minutes past eight. She felt the tension of the day's inquiries settle into her bones, the sense that something-some crucial seam-was about to give. Captain Ivor Hale entered from the corridor, his overcoat still damp, the scent of wet wool and tobacco clinging to him. He paused by the reception desk, his gaze traveling from Eleanor to Dr. Finch, then inevitably to the battered clock. The air between the three of them was thick with unspoken questions. Eleanor broke the silence first, her voice low but clear. 'We need to walk through it again,' she said, her words carrying the weight of command. 'Not for the constable, not for the record-just for ourselves. Too much depends on what we think we know. ' She gestured to the clock, her gloved hand steady. 'The timeline is wrong. I can feel it. ' Dr.

Finch's composure was brittle, her usual clinical calm fraying at the edges. She glanced at the clock, then away, her fingers fidgeting with the edge of her cuff. 'I was in the dining room from seven forty-five until a twenty minutes past eight,' she repeated, but the assertion sounded rehearsed, as if she needed to hear it aloud to believe it. Captain Hale shifted, his hand tightening on his hat brim. 'I was in the garden,' he said, his voice rough with fatigue. 'The air was foul with the storm, but I needed it. ' He did not meet Eleanor's eyes. The battered clock loomed over them, its brass pendulum visible through the glass panel. Eleanor stepped closer, her heels clicking on the marble. She leaned in, scrutinizing the clock's innards. The pendulum hung slightly off true, its arc marred by a faint, greasy fingerprint.

The glass bore a subtle smear, as if someone had steadied themselves against it, and the wood below showed a fresh scratch, the varnish still raw. She drew her finger along the edge, feeling the roughness, and saw the faintest tremor in Dr. Finch's posture. 'Has anyone touched this since the body was discovered? ' Eleanor asked, her tone deceptively mild. Captain Hale shook his head, but Dr. Finch hesitated, her lips parting before she caught herself. 'Not that I saw,' Dr. Finch said, but her voice was thin, uncertain. Eleanor watched her, noting the way her gaze darted to the clock and then away, refusing to settle. The silence that followed was taut, the only sound the faint creak of the old building settling into the night. Eleanor's attention returned to the clock. The pendulum, she realized, had not always hung so askew.

The dust along the lower edge of the glass had been disturbed, the brass newly polished in places where no one had reason to clean. She pressed gently on the case; the pendulum swung, then stuttered, as if it had been forced out of rhythm. 'It's been moved,' she murmured, more to herself than to the others. 'Recently. ' Captain Hale's face darkened. 'Are you suggesting one of us tampered with it? ' he asked, his voice rising above a whisper for the first time. The accusation hung between them, unspoken but felt. Dr. Finch's hands twisted together, her knuckles white. 'Why would anyone bother with the clock? ' she said, but the protest was hollow. Eleanor's gaze lingered on her, then on the captain, weighing their reactions. The lobby's atmosphere shifted, the tension mounting as the implications settled in. Eleanor did not answer Captain Hale directly.

Instead, she turned to the battered clock, her expression unreadable. 'Someone wanted us to believe the wrong time,' she said quietly. 'Someone wanted to shape the story that would be told. ' Her words fell into the hush, and for a moment, no one moved. A burst of laughter from the bar drifted into the lobby-a jarring note of normalcy that broke the spell. Eleanor allowed herself a small, ironic smile, the tension in her shoulders easing for a heartbeat. The world outside their triangle of suspicion went on, indifferent to the secrets pressing in on all sides. She turned back to Dr. Finch, her voice gentle but insistent. 'If there's something you're not telling me, now is the time. ' Dr. Finch looked away, her jaw working. 'I've told you everything I know,' she said, but her tone was less certain than before.

Captain Hale's eyes narrowed, suspicion and fatigue warring in his expression. 'We're all being led in circles,' he muttered. 'By a clock, no less. ' Eleanor let the silence stretch, watching both of them. The battered clock, the smudged glass, the pendulum's unnatural tilt-each detail was a thread, and she could feel the pattern emerging, though the shape of it remained just out of reach. She made a note in her book, her hand steady. The facts were accumulating, and the false timeline was beginning to unravel. A draft crept through the lobby as the doors to the terrace shifted in the wind, carrying with it the scent of salt and the faint, metallic tang of the sea. Eleanor drew her cardigan tighter, feeling the spring chill settle over her. She looked once more at the clock, its hands unmoving, its silence more eloquent than any confession.

The next step was clear: the truth lay in the mechanism, in the tampering that had shaped every alibi, every recollection. But for tonight, the pattern was enough. Suspicion had narrowed, and the stakes had risen. The final test was coming, and with it, the reckoning they all feared. As Dr. Finch and Captain Hale drifted toward the staircase, their movements slow with exhaustion, Eleanor lingered in the lobby, her gaze fixed on the battered clock. The spring night pressed close, the rain outside now only a memory, but the sense of urgency remained. She had seen what she needed to see: the clock's pendulum, the smudges, the fresh scratch. Someone had tampered with the clock, and the timeline everyone trusted was a lie. The truth, Eleanor knew, was finally within reach.

## Chapter 8: The Final Trap

"Let us begin," Eleanor said, her voice low but unwavering as it cut through the hush of the lobby. The spring morning light crept in pale and uncertain, glancing off the marble floor and catching on the battered brass of the clock above the mantel. The air still held the smell of an extinguished fire and the damp of last night's rain. Shadows moved across the faded rug, and somewhere on the upper landing a door creaked, loud in all that quiet. Eleanor did not move from her place before the clock. Its hands stood at twenty minutes past eight, and she studied them as though watching long enough might make them tick forward and confess what they had seen. A greasy smear marked the glass panel; the brass pendulum hung slightly askew, its swing broken by some recent, careless hand.

The room was cold, the kind of cold that seemed to come up out of the walls, and yet a bead of sweat traced her temple. She let her fingers hover above the case, feeling the scratched wood, the smooth chill of the glass, then turned to face the room. "We are here because the truth has been twisted," she said, each word falling into the hush like a stone dropped into still water. "The clock tells one story. The witnesses tell another. I believe the clock was tampered with, deliberately, to mislead us about the time of death. " She let that sit, watching for the first flicker of reaction. Sylvia Trent stood near the window, rigid, her gloved hands clasped before her. Dr. Mallory Finch lingered by the staircase, her white coat immaculate, her eyes shadowed with fatigue. Captain Ivor Hale leaned on his cane by the reception desk, jaw set.

Hugo Vane hovered near the bar, his gaze flicking from the clock to Eleanor and back again. "The clock stopped at twenty past eight," Eleanor went on. "Yet several of you saw Beatrice Quill alive well after that. If the clock were telling the truth, that could not be. So someone set it back-and in doing so, tried to buy themselves an alibi they hadn't earned. " She let the silence stretch, and the lobby seemed to draw in around it. She crossed to the sideboard, where the heavy, ornate book still lay, a dark stain blooming at one corner, its spine dented. She lifted it and turned the bloodied edge toward the room. "This struck Beatrice Quill. The wound at her temple matches its shape, its weight. Whoever held this book left traces behind-on its cover, on the clock, on the story of that morning. " Hugo Vane shifted his feet. "You can't mean-any of us could have touched it.

That clock's forever needing winding, and the book, well, anyone might have moved it. " The defiance in his voice didn't reach his eyes. "No, Mr. Vane," Eleanor said. "You were in the bar from eight o'clock on. The barman saw you. So did three other guests. You could not have slipped away unnoticed. "The gardener saw me. So did Dr. Finch, from the window. " Eleanor nodded. "The mud on your shoes bears that out, and so do two witnesses. You could not have come inside, touched that clock, and gone back out again without being seen. " The captain's jaw worked, but he offered nothing further. Dr. Finch's composure had worn thin. "I was in the dining room," she said, her voice unsteady. "The staff saw me.

Guests remember me pouring tea at a quarter past eight. " Eleanor met her eyes, gentle but immovable. "Your presence there is confirmed by more than one witness. Eleanor turned at last to Sylvia Trent, who stood without moving, her face pale, her eyes wide and fixed. Somewhere far off a second clock ticked on, the only sound in the room. "Miss Trent," Eleanor said, her tone soft but immovable, "you told us you were in your room from eight until half past. The smudge on the glass matches the powder from your gloves.

The pendulum was knocked askew, and the scratch on it is fresh. No one else had the chance to stop that clock and shift the hour. " Sylvia's composure cracked; her breath came fast and shallow. She tried to speak and the words would not come. Eleanor pressed on, her voice steady. "You struck Beatrice Quill with that book. Eleanor Voss stood at the room's heart, the hush broken only by the subdued movements of those left behind.
