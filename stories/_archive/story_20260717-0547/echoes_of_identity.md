'To surviving,' he said, raising his glass in a toast that was half bitter, half resigned. 'And to the ones who didn't. ' He drank, then set the glass down with more force than necessary. The gesture was not quite forgiveness, but it was acceptance of sorts.

Eleanor watched them, the survivors of a tragedy that had reshaped them all. She felt the exhaustion settle in her limbs, but also a strange, fragile hope. The truth had come at a cost-one that could not be measured in justice alone. The bonds between those who remained had been altered, perhaps strengthened, perhaps only frayed in new ways. She would carry the memory of this night, the weight of the choices made, long after the guests had gone and the hotel's walls had been scrubbed clean of blood and secrets. The gramophone sat silent now, its needle at rest, the music spent. Outside, the rain had ceased, and the only sound was the faint creak of the old building settling into the wintry night. Eleanor closed her notebook at last, the finality of the gesture echoing in the hush. She did not know what tomorrow would bring-scandal, perhaps, or the slow, uncertain work of rebuilding.

But for tonight, the truth was enough. She lingered at the window, watching the darkness, and listened as the drawing room filled with the quiet, uneasy breathing of those left behind.
