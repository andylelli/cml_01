## Chapter 8: The Timed Clock Test

"Inspector Halloway, do you believe the clock can truly lie?" Ottoline Dunmore's voice was quiet as she stood in the manor clock room beside Ambrose, the morning light slanting across the floorboards. On the worktable lay the clockmaker's report, pages crisp and marked with diagrams, alongside Ottoline's own logbook and a set of fine tools.

Ambrose turned to her, the clock's open face reflecting in his glasses. "It's not a matter of belief, Miss Dunmore. It's a matter of mechanism."

Ottoline's hands rested on the edge of the table, trembling only slightly. "The clockmaker says the striking sequence could be altered, if the detent lever is adjusted. The chime would sound earlier, though the hands would still display the later time."

Ambrose nodded, picking up the report. "The clock's striking train can be advanced by repositioning the lever-just as your logbook suggests was done yesterday. The chime at twenty-five minutes past three, though the face read quarter to four. And the victim's watch stopped at ten minutes past three, matching the true time of death."

Ottoline's lips parted, as if to protest, but she simply gazed at her logbook. Ambrose slid it toward her, open to the most recent entry. "Your maintenance records-altered. The clockmaker's fingerprints report matches. Ottoline Dunmore, your prints are on the mechanism."

She shut her eyes, swallowing hard. "I-I only meant to-"

Ambrose's tone was gentle, but unyielding. "Flat answer, if you please."

Beatrice Whitlock's voice came back in memory, clipped and sure. "Always. Next question?"

Frances Orme, entering with a rustle of silk, regarded the scene with a polite, sharpened smile. "Inspector, are we to have a demonstration? I do adore a good show-especially when time is the main attraction."

Ambrose nodded, gesturing to the clockmaker who now entered, toolbox in hand. "We'll test the sequence. Mrs. Orme, would you care to watch?"

Frances settled on a chair, smoothing her skirt. "I wouldn't miss it for the world, Inspector. I expect the outcome will be... illuminating."

The clockmaker set the detent lever to the altered position, wound the striking train, and reset the hands to quarter to four. "Observe," he said, his accent thick but his manner exact. The chime sounded at twenty-five minutes past three, the clock face still reading quarter to four.

Frances clapped, the sound as soft as her words were sharp. "How very clever. The chime lies, the hands lie, and all our alibis vanish like smoke."

Ambrose looked to Ottoline. "You had the skill, the access, and the motive. The altered logbook, the glove fragment, and now the fingerprints-everything points to you."

Ottoline's shoulders slumped. "It's curious-how time unravels everything we try to hold together."

Ambrose closed the logbook, his hand lingering on the worn cover. "One mustn't jump to conclusions, but... the clock tells the truth, now that we know where to look."

For a moment, the room was quiet, the only sound the faint ticking of the clock restored to honest time. Frances stood, adjusting her hat with a small, satisfied smile. The clockmaker packed his tools, nodding to Ambrose as he left.

Ottoline remained by the table, her fingers tracing the edge of the logbook. Ambrose placed a gentle hand on her shoulder, then withdrew, leaving her alone with the relentless honesty of the ticking clock.

Outside, gardeners paused to glance toward the manor's windows, shears held mid-air over tangled rose stems. A maid hesitated at the path, linens bundled in her arms, eyes flicking up at the clock tower before hurrying on. The day's business resumed, but with each movement marked by a hush, as if the murder had pressed an invisible weight on the estate's routines.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"Inspector Halloway, are we to have the truth at last?" Percival Thorne's voice rang out across the manor sitting room in late morning, the survivors assembled in a semicircle about the hearth. Ambrose Halloway stood with his back to the dying fire, the logbook and evidence bag resting on the table beside him.

He regarded the group: Percival, arms folded, gaze sharp; Josephine Rutherford, hands clasped over her apron; Frances Orme, posture immaculate, lips pursed; and Ottoline Dunmore, pale but composed.

Ambrose began, his words measured. "Last evening, the clock's mechanism was tested. The logbook was produced, and fingerprints identified. Ottoline Dunmore's prints were present-on the clock, on the lever, on the altered maintenance records."

Josephine's voice was flat, her gaze fixed on the floor. "Flat answer: It fits."

Percival, seizing his moment, delivered his speech with sardonic flourish. "So, Dunmore, it comes to this. The estate's quiet mouse, winding clocks and scribbling in ledgers, turns out to have the sharpest teeth. How fitting. You always did long for more authority-now you'll have the full attention of Scotland Yard. Did you really think you could rewrite time, Ottoline, and no one would notice the plot twist?"

Ottoline's reply was soft but steady. "Six words: Time tells, even when people don't." She looked down, her fingers tracing the table edge.

Frances Orme tapped her gloved fingertips against the arm of her chair, a wry smile curving her lips. "Well, Inspector, it seems we're all innocent except the one who kept us punctual."

Ambrose met Ottoline's gaze. "You repositioned the detent lever, advanced the striking train, and created a false chime at twenty-five minutes past three. The hands read quarter to four, but the deed was done shortly after ten minutes past three. You alone had the skill, access, and motive. You alone could bend time to your will."

Ottoline closed her eyes, exhaling. "I wanted the estate to change. I was tired of being a caretaker for other people's mistakes."

Percival watched her, a flicker of something like regret in his expression. Josephine stood silent, a hand resting lightly on the mantel. Frances adjusted her cuff, her gaze distant.

Ambrose closed his notebook with a finality that seemed to echo through the room. "One mustn't jump to conclusions, but... the evidence is complete. Ottoline Dunmore, you are the only one who could have committed this murder."

The sitting room was silent save for the faint ticking of the hall clock-now, for once, keeping honest time-as the truth finally settled over Lockwood.

## Chapter 10: The Culprit Revealed

"Lockwood will keep better time now, I think." Josephine Rutherford's words opened the afternoon in the manor drawing room, sunlight glinting off the polished wood of the table where the evidence rested. Ottoline Dunmore had been led away, her calm replaced by a silence that echoed in every tick of the restored clock.

Ambrose Halloway lingered near the hearth, his notebook closed, his hands at ease for the first time in days. He unfolded a letter bearing the county seal and reread its simple lines; his name was cleared, his reputation unblemished, the inquiry concluded without so much as a magistrate's footfall on Lockwood's gravel drive. He would leave behind a record of meticulous, incorruptible work-one small change in a career built on such victories.

Percival Thorne stood by the window, a telegram in hand, the lines of worry eased from his features. He gazed at the telegram's closing words, offering him a modest position in the city-an escape from Lockwood, the chance to begin again-his name now free of suspicion. He folded the telegram, slipped it into his pocket, and ran a hand through his hair, savoring the unfamiliar lightness of relief.

The air in the room was different now, as if the house itself had exhaled. Josephine, freed from accusation, returned to her duties with a new autonomy-her petition for lock-keeper's cottage improvements granted at last. She dusted the mantel with brisk efficiency, pausing just once to adjust the photograph of Beatrice Whitlock beside the clock.

Frances Orme, no longer under Beatrice's shadow, arranged a vase of roses on the sideboard, her movements precise, her gaze clear. She spoke of charity garden parties, her voice light and untroubled, as if the estate's darkness had never touched her.

In a quiet moment, Ambrose found himself recalling Beatrice Whitlock-not as the stern mistress of the estate, but as she once was in a faded photograph: laughing at a summer picnic, a wide-brimmed hat askew, a slice of cake in hand. He remembered the way she had always insisted on order, yet allowed her grandchildren to run wild on the lawns-a memory tucked away, untouched by the manner of her death.

Percival, standing by the open window, breathed in the scent of cut grass, the distant call of a rook drifting on the breeze. He looked back at the room-not as a stage for suspicion, but as the heart of a house that would, once again, resume its ordinary life.

Ambrose gathered his things, pausing to adjust the hands of the manor clock. The mechanism hummed, its tick steady and honest. He nodded to Josephine, who met his gaze with a small, wry smile.

"That's how the canal runs, isn't it?"

Ambrose's reply was dry as ever: "You can't rush the tide."

Percival, glancing at the clock, quipped, "At least the clocks are honest now-pity the same can't always be said for the company."

Josephine deadpanned, "Six words: Company changes, clocks just tick on."

As the afternoon faded, the drawing room filled with the sounds of everyday life-a tray rattling in the hall, the murmur of voices from the garden, the steady, truthful ticking of the manor clock. And so, with its secrets laid bare, Lockwood returned to its routines, its tragedies subsumed by the relentless, ordinary march of time.
