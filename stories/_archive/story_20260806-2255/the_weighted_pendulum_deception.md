She pressed the point, her voice softer now. "But the chimes-did they not sound before the hands reached the hour? Several guests recall the discrepancy. " Sylvia's reply was immediate, almost defensive. "It always does that. The chimes are a bit eager, but the hands catch up soon enough.

The flaw was not in his story, but in her own assumptions. A silence settled, broken only by the distant sound of a radio in the lobby-Bing Crosby again, crooning through the static.

But beneath the surface, Eleanor felt the ground shifting. She had eliminated Captain Hale, not by catching him in a lie, but by forcing herself to see the limits of her own reasoning. The clock, once a silent witness, had become a trickster-its hands and chimes telling different tales, its pendulum heavy with secrets. She glanced at the velvet pouch of weights on the side table, the set of brass scales, the open logbook, and the staff records-each a piece of a puzzle that refused to settle.

As the fire dwindled to a faint glow and the night pressed in, Eleanor allowed herself a moment of doubt. She had followed the evidence, but the evidence had led her in a circle. The true culprit remained hidden, shielded by the very contradictions she had tried to unravel. Yet in that moment of uncertainty, she found a strange clarity: the path ahead would not be lit by easy answers, but by the willingness to question every certainty-even, and especially, her own.

## Chapter 7: Secrets Beneath the Surface

Beatrice Quill pressed the logbook flat against the narrow table, the rough paper catching beneath her palm as the lamp flickered over the Service corridor. The hush held as Eleanor drew a slow breath, her eyes on the logbook's neat columns. The previous hour's doubts still clung to her-the sense of chasing her own tail in circles.

' She slid the book across, her hand steady, her jaw set, as though daring Eleanor to find a flaw in it. The truth is, I have little faith in appearances tonight. ' Beatrice's mouth quirked into a brittle smile, her eyes flashing with something like wounded pride. 'Appearances are all some of us have left,' she said, and then, softer, glancing away: 'I've worked too hard to risk it all on a moment's carelessness. But she saw through me. She always did. ' Beatrice's expression softened, her earlier defensiveness giving way to something like sympathy. 'We all have ambitions, Miss Trent. Some of us are just better at hiding them. ' Her voice carried an edge, but the sting was aimed at herself.

Eleanor let the silence stretch, the only sound the distant tick of the grandfather clock in the lobby, muffled by the corridor walls. ' She closed the book. 'Whatever else you've concealed, it isn't murder. ' Sylvia exhaled, her shoulders sagging as if a weight had come off them. 'Thank you, Miss Voss.

I suppose I needed someone to tell me I wasn't the villain, even if I felt like one. ' Her laugh was brittle, but genuine. 'I'll try to keep my stories to the facts from now on. The puzzle at the heart of the case-the manipulation of time, the silent testimony of the clock-had not. Eleanor gathered her notes, her gaze lingering on the logbook and the stack of statements. She felt the ache of fatigue in her bones, and something else beneath it-not quite relief. Two names were off her list now, but the contradictions that remained were sharper for it. Somewhere in the gap between chime and hand, the truth was still waiting. As she turned to leave, Eleanor paused, her hand on the doorframe. 'We are none of us entirely innocent,' she said, more to herself than to the others. 'But tonight, at least, we know who is not guilty. ## Chapter 9: The Final Trap: Discriminating Test

At first light, with the hotel lobby still steeped in the hush of early morning, Eleanor Voss set her notebook on the marble-topped table beneath the grandfather clock. The air was cool, tinged with the faint scent of beeswax and the lingering trace of last night's rain. Shadows stretched long across the terrazzo, and the amber glow of the lamps cast a steady, watchful light over the small gathering. The mood was taut-anticipation wound as tightly as the mainspring in the clock itself. The previous evening's laughter, sparked by Beatrice Quill's dry remark about kitchen timers, had faded; now, only the low voices of the assembled guests and the soft ticking of the clock broke the silence. Eleanor's voice, when it came, was measured and clear. 'Thank you all for joining me so early.

There is one last matter to settle-one that concerns the very heart of this crime. ' Her gaze swept from Beatrice, who stood with arms folded and chin high, to Captain Ivor Hale, his posture rigid by the window, to Sylvia Trent, notebook clutched to her chest, and finally to Hugo Vane, who lingered near the clock, his expression carefully composed. 'We have all relied on the grandfather clock's account of the evening,' Eleanor continued, 'but I believe it has led us astray. ' She opened the glass door of the clock with a gloved hand, the faint metallic creak echoing in the quiet. The pendulum's brass bob gleamed in the lamplight. Eleanor lifted it free and set it on the brass scales she had prepared-her gesture deliberate, almost ceremonial.

The needle wavered, then settled two ounces heavier than the standard weight recorded in the maintenance log. 'You'll recall,' she said, 'that the clock's chimes sounded five minutes before the hour, yet the hands lagged behind. When I weighed the pendulum bob, I found it heavier than it ought to be. ' She let the scales rest, the proof plain for all to see. 'A heavier pendulum slows the swing,' Eleanor explained, her tone precise. 'This clock has been running slow by exactly ten minutes. Beatrice Quill's composure wavered as Eleanor addressed her next. 'Miss Quill, the service corridor logs and staff signatures place you in the narrow passageways behind the kitchens throughout the critical window. Eleanor turned to Sylvia Trent. 'Miss Trent, guest statements place you in the lobby lounge, away from the clock room, at the time in question. All eyes returned to Hugo Vane. Eleanor's tone sharpened, the last vestige of gentleness gone. 'Mr. Vane, only you had the opportunity and the skill to alter the clock's mechanism. I believe you confronted her here, and when words failed, you seized the letter opener and stabbed her.
