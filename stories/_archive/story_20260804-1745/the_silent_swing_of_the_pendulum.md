Beatrice Quill's voice broke the silence, softer than before. 'What happens now, Dr. Finch? To the hotel, to us? ' Her question was not rhetorical, but neither was it answerable. Dr. Finch hesitated, then replied with the dry wit that had seen her through darker days. 'We go on, I suppose. The clocks will be wound, the guests will arrive, and the rain will find its way in, no matter how we bar the doors. ' The line drew a faint, ironic smile from Hugo Vane, but the relief was fleeting.

A moment later, Sylvia Trent crossed the lobby, pausing by the grandfather clock. She reached out, almost absently, to straighten the guest ledger on the reception desk-a gesture so ordinary it might have gone unnoticed, except for the way her hand lingered, as if anchoring herself to the familiar. 'I'll see to the tea,' she said, her voice steadier. 'There's always work to be done. ' The simple act restored a measure of order, a signal that life, however altered, would resume. Dr. Finch lingered by the clock, her reflection ghosted in the glass. The mechanism would be repaired, the wound in the carpet patched, but the memory of what had been concealed here would not fade so easily. She pressed her palm to the brass, feeling the chill of metal and the faint vibration of the pendulum's swing.

The cost of truth was visible in every face, in every silence that followed her through the lobby. Yet the truth, once spoken, was a kind of mercy-however cold, however incomplete. Outside, the rain eased, leaving the air washed and raw. The lobby's hush was broken by the arrival of new guests, their voices tentative as they crossed the threshold into a house forever changed. Dr. Finch watched them, her expression unreadable, then turned away from the clock. The facts were what they were; the rest belonged to the living. She moved toward the service corridor, her footsteps quiet, the echo of the pendulum's last swing following her into the uncertain spring light. Captain Ivor Hale was responsible; the evidence allowed no other reading.
