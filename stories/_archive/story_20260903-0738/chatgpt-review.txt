This is a nice change of setting — the theatre gives you fresher atmosphere than another seaside hotel clock mystery — but as a mystery draft, it is not as strong as the best clock/veranda versions yet. The concept is good: a spotlight/shadow illusion instead of a timepiece trick. But the execution gets muddled because the story sometimes explains the solution before the final trap, and Chapter 10 falls back into the old “repeat the case” habit.

I’d score it around 78/100.

What works

The theatre setting is a major plus. The opening gives a clear backstage world: stale powder, damp wood, blackout curtains, applause fading from the auditorium, costumes, vanity, logbook, and a bloodied brass paperweight. That immediately feels more specific than another generic hotel lounge.

The clue concept is also promising. The story establishes an apparent contradiction:

lighting rig log: one spotlight at 45 degrees
painted shadow: 135 degrees
difference: 90 degrees
witnesses claimed two figures were seen

That is a good fair-play idea because it uses the physical stage environment, not just statements.

Kestrel is a plausible culprit because he has access to lighting, diagrams, stagecraft knowledge, and resentment over creative control. The final trap links him to the spotlight angles and his own diagrams.

Character Life / Relationship Richness: 7/10

This category is doing decently. The theatre world naturally creates relationships: actors, stage manager, patron, outsider, company politics, creative-control disputes, professional jealousy, loyalty, reputation, performance. That gives the story more built-in social texture than the hotel clock versions.

Gwendolyn’s vanity and vulnerability come through well; she performs even while afraid. Marguerite feels like someone trying to keep the theatre functioning while everything fractures. Ferdinand has patrician evasiveness. Kestrel has bitterness and wounded pride.

The best character-life material is Kestrel’s resentment: his rehearsal notes are pressed so hard they nearly tear the paper, and his anger over creative control becomes a physical detail, not just a stated motive.

But the relationships are still more theatrical archetype than lived history. We need clearer emotional bonds:

What did Montague do to Kestrel specifically?
Was Gwendolyn protected by Montague or ruined by him?
Why is Marguerite loyal to the company despite hating its secrets?
What does Ferdinand’s patronage actually cost him?
Did Dorothy know Montague, or is she purely investigating?

Right now, the characters have style, but their relationships still lack enough personal past.

Main issue: the optical trick needs clearer logic

The story says the spotlight was at 45 degrees, the painted shadow was at 135 degrees, and therefore the shadow could not have been cast by the recorded spotlight. That is intriguing, but the reader needs a clearer mental picture.

At the moment, it is not fully clear whether:

the shadow was physically painted on the backdrop,
the backdrop was rotated,
a second false figure was painted,
the spotlight made one real actor look like two people,
witnesses saw a silhouette, reflection, or pre-painted shadow.

The best version would be:

The second “figure” was not a person. It was a painted shadow on a movable backdrop, positioned so that from the audience seats it looked like a second man standing beside Montague. But when Dorothy reset the spotlight to the recorded forty-five-degree angle, the real shadow fell in the opposite direction. The painted shadow was ninety degrees wrong. Only the person who designed the lighting diagram could have known how to place it.

That gives the reader a clean image.

Chapter 8 and Chapter 9 overlap

Chapter 8 prepares the final test and already proves a lot: it sets the spotlight to 45 degrees, shows the beam cannot create the painted 135-degree shadow, connects the diagrams to Kestrel, and identifies his access and motive.

Then Chapter 9 repeats much of the same test and also clears the false suspects. It does too much at once.

Better structure:

Chapter 8: Preparation only. Dorothy sets the stage, arranges the original backdrop, examines diagrams. End with “the test was ready.”
Chapter 9: Final trap only. Run the spotlight test, show the impossible shadow, accuse Kestrel, get confession.
Chapter 10: Aftermath only. No evidence recap.

Chapter 8 is almost doing this already, but it goes too far into proving the solution.

Chapter 10 still repeats the case

This is the recurring issue. Chapter 10 begins with aftermath, but then repeats:

passphrase variation
Gwendolyn’s alibi
orchestra logs
Marguerite’s alibi
green room logs
Ferdinand’s alibi
Kestrel in custody
lighting records and painted shadow

That belongs to Chapters 7–9, not the final chapter.

Chapter 10 should focus on:

the company after Kestrel is gone
the empty chair / cancelled rehearsal
Gwendolyn facing an audience without Montague
Marguerite deciding whether to keep the show open
Ferdinand withdrawing funding or committing to save the theatre
Dorothy leaving as the stage lights cool

The last line also reverts to reveal mode:

“You did it.”
Ivor Kestrel nods.

That should not be in Chapter 10. The culprit should already be gone or already confessed.

Scaffold/prose leakage to remove

These lines still feel generated rather than written:

“The clocks put it at half past nine at night.”
“Each detail was planted, waiting for its later significance.”
“Dorothy Gaunt, now detective-in-charge…”
“The night’s curtain had only just risen.”
repeated “Grand Marquee Theatre District, cloaked in drizzle and expectation”
“stage manager’s exclusive access” even though Kestrel, not Marguerite, is the culprit
final “You did it.”

The phrase “each detail was planted” is especially bad because it describes the story as a constructed mystery instead of letting the clue exist inside the fiction.

Fastest fixes
Clarify the illusion: painted shadow + real spotlight = impossible geometry.
Give Kestrel one stronger motive scene with Montague: blackmail, creative humiliation, contract control, or theft of work.
Keep Chapter 8 as setup, Chapter 9 as reveal, Chapter 10 as aftermath.
Remove all evidence recap from Chapter 10.
Delete “each detail was planted” and other scaffold phrases.
Add one human relationship beat between Dorothy and Montague, or Kestrel and Montague.
Overall

This is promising because it moves away from clock puzzles into a setting-specific theatrical illusion. That is a good direction. The atmosphere is strong, and the characters have a natural social world.

As written: 78/100.

With a clearer optical explanation, stronger Kestrel/Montague motive, and a non-repetitive Chapter 10, this could reach 85–88/100.
