But who-why? " Eleanor's reply was sharp. "Because the timeline is the only shield for the murderer. If the time of death appears later, alibis solidify. But the scratches on the clock, the misaligned minute hand, and the testimony of the operator who confirmed Hugo Vane's call at nine all point to one conclusion: the clock was manipulated to misrepresent the moment Captain Hale died. " She turned to Hugo Vane. "You, Mr. Vane, are cleared. The operator's log, which I requested from the local exchange, shows your call began at five minutes to nine and ended at ten past. You could not have left the line to tamper with the clock or commit murder; the connection was never broken.

Therefore, your alibi holds. " Hugo Vane's relief was visible-a shuddered breath, the tension in his shoulders easing as he nodded. Next, Eleanor faced Beatrice Quill. "Miss Quill, your whereabouts are confirmed by Sylvia Trent and the hotel's staff. The broken vase incident you feared would damn you was an accident, witnessed by the maid and corroborated by the accounts ledger you were balancing at the time. You are ruled out-your alibi is solid because you could not have left the library unnoticed. The evidence clears you. " Beatrice Quill's eyes filled with tears, her voice catching as she managed a quiet, "Thank you. " Eleanor's gaze shifted to Sylvia Trent. "Miss Trent, your innocence is proven by your family's telegram, delivered at half past eight and signed for at the desk. The messenger's record and the signature in the logbook confirm your presence away from the scene.

You could not have committed the murder. The family connection proves your alibi holds. " Sylvia Trent's posture softened, her arms dropping to her sides as the suspicion lifted. A hush fell, the only sound the faint ticking of the clock mechanism Eleanor had re-engaged. The glow of the desk lamp flickered, casting Dr. Mallory Finch's face in sharp relief. Eleanor's tone hardened. "Dr. Finch, you alone remain. Your claim: you were in the reading room from just after eight until the commotion. But the clock's tampering required knowledge of its mechanism and access at a precise moment. Witnesses recall hearing you argue with Captain Hale just before nine. And the physical traces-oil on your gloves, the faint scent lingering on your sleeve-match the clock's mechanism. These are not coincidences. " Dr. Mallory Finch's lips parted, but no words came. Eleanor pressed on, her voice unwavering.

"The true time of death is not ten minutes past nine, as the clock claims, but a quarter past nine-as shown by Captain Hale's own watch, found in his pocket, stopped at the moment of his struggle. You strangled him, Doctor. The marks on his neck, the absence of defensive wounds, the lack of noise-all point to a sudden, silent attack. Afterwards, you tampered with the clock, setting it back to ten minutes past nine, to create a false window and shield yourself with the confusion. " The tension in the lobby thickened, the candlelight quivering as Dr. Mallory Finch's composure began to crack. Her hands, so steady in the operating theatre, now trembled. "You think you know everything, Miss Voss? " she whispered, her voice brittle. "You think you understand what it is to be destroyed by a man's ambition? Captain Hale threatened everything I had built-my reputation, my practice.

He would have seen me ruined, all for a professional slight. I did what I had to, to protect what was mine. " Eleanor's eyes did not waver. "You killed him to protect yourself-from exposure, from disgrace. But you also tried to protect others from the consequences of his actions. The line between right and wrong blurred in your desperation. But the evidence is clear. You had motive, opportunity, and the means. The clock's tampering, the argument, the oil on your gloves, and the timeline all point to you-and only you. " Dr. Mallory Finch's shoulders sagged, the fight draining from her. "You're clever, Miss Voss. Too clever. I never meant for it to end this way. " Her confession was quiet, almost lost in the lobby's hush. The others watched, some with pity, some with horror. The moral ambiguity hung in the air-was this justice, or merely another tragedy born of pride and fear?

The lobby door swung open, and a constable entered, summoned by Eleanor's earlier telegram. As Dr. Mallory Finch was led away, Eleanor felt a mingled sense of relief and regret. The truth had been named, but at what cost? The springtime dawn crept in at the edges of the sky, pale and uncertain. The guests drifted away, their faces etched with exhaustion and something like hope. Eleanor lingered by the reception desk, her hand resting on the now-honest clock. The mechanism ticked on, steady and unyielding. She let herself exhale, the tension in her chest easing for the first time since the investigation began. The case was closed, but the shadow of its moral cost would linger-reminding her that even in the bright vernal light, some truths are never simple, and justice is rarely clean.

## Chapter 10: The Revelation

The steady drumming of rain on the tall lobby windows mingled with the cold, grey light filtering through the glass, lending the late night a chill that even the hotel's marble could not match. Eleanor Voss lingered by the reception desk, her hand still resting on the now-honest clock, the mechanism ticking with a smooth, unyielding rhythm. The springtime air was heavy with the scent of damp stone and the faint spice of freshly brewed coffee, a comfort that could not quite dispel the oppressive hush. Around her, the soft whispers of guests drifted from the far corners of the lobby, but none dared break the tension that had settled since Eleanor summoned them. Dr. Mallory Finch stood near the edge of the lamplight, composed yet impossibly pale, her gloved hands folded tightly at her waist. The confrontation hung between them, as inevitable as the dawn.

Eleanor drew a slow breath, steadying herself. The time for ambiguity was over. "Dr. Finch," Eleanor said, her voice carrying through the hush, "we've reached the end. The evidence is no longer a tapestry of contradiction but a chain-each link forged by your own actions. " She glanced to the clock above, its hands set right at last. "You alone had both the motive and the means to kill Captain Hale. You strangled him. The marks on his neck, the lack of struggle, the timing. Afterward, you tampered with the lobby clock to misrepresent the time of death. " A flicker of emotion passed over Dr. Mallory Finch's face-pain, anger, then a weary resignation. She did not speak. Eleanor pressed on, her cadence unyielding. "The mechanism showed fresh scratches, the minute hand misaligned. The scent of oil on your gloves matches the clock's mechanism.

Your argument with Captain Hale was heard by several guests. The commotion was reported at twenty minutes past nine, but the clock insisted on ten minutes past nine. Captain Hale's own watch stopped at a quarter past nine. You tried to build yourself an alibi by rewriting time itself. " The lobby's hush deepened as the consequence of Eleanor's words settled. Beatrice Quill, eyes wide and glistening, pressed her notebook to her chest. "I-I never believed it could be her," she whispered, voice trembling. "But I saw Dr. Finch near the lobby, just after the storm. She was alone. " The admission was not an accusation, but an echo of relief and sorrow. Eleanor's gaze passed over Beatrice Quill, her tone gentler. "Beatrice Quill, you are cleared because the broken vase incident was an accident, witnessed and corroborated by the housemaid and the accounts ledger you were reconciling.

Your alibi holds; you could not have left the library without being seen. " Beatrice Quill let out a breath she seemed to have held for hours, her relief mingled with regret. She turned to Sylvia Trent. "Sylvia Trent, you are cleared. Family connections prove your innocence-the telegram you signed for at half past eight is recorded in the logbook, and the messenger's testimony confirms your presence away from the scene. Your alibi is confirmed, and you could not have committed the crime. " Sylvia Trent's elegant posture softened, her eyes shining as the suspicion lifted. Eleanor addressed Hugo Vane next. "Hugo Vane, you are cleared because business records prove your calls were legitimate. The operator's log from the exchange shows your call began at five minutes to nine and ended at ten past.

You could not have left the line to tamper with the clock or commit murder; your alibi holds. " Hugo Vane closed his eyes, the tension draining from his shoulders. Eleanor paused, letting the weight of the moment deepen. "I, too, am ruled out," she said softly, "because my presence in the lobby throughout the evening was witnessed by the staff and guests alike. There is no avenue for me to have committed the act I have now described. " The declaration brought a hush that bordered on reverence. Dr. Mallory Finch's composure finally cracked. She stepped into the lamplight, her voice thin and brittle. "You want a confession, Miss Voss? Very well. I killed Captain Hale. I strangled him while the rain battered the glass, while the hotel slept. He threatened to destroy everything I had built-my reputation, my practice, my future.

I tampered with the clock to protect myself, to create confusion and buy time. Revenge for professional rivalry, nothing less. " She drew herself upright, but the weight of her admission bowed her shoulders. "He was going to expose me-over a difference of opinion, over pride. I did what I thought was necessary. I see now how wrong I was. " Her words hung in the air, the moral ambiguity raw and unresolved. The doors opened, and a constable-face impassive-stepped forward, taking Dr. Mallory Finch gently by the arm. She did not resist. The guests watched, some with horror, others with a sympathy they could not quite name. As Dr. Mallory Finch was led away, she glanced back at Eleanor, a flicker of gratitude-or perhaps condemnation-passing between them. The lobby remained heavy with silence, the aftermath settling like dust.

Beatrice Quill wept quietly, her tears for lost innocence as much as for the man who had died. Sylvia Trent lingered by the window, staring out into the rain-soaked night, while Hugo Vane poured himself a glass of whisky with hands that shook. In the quiet, relief and regret mingled-the cost of truth exacted from each in their own measure. Eleanor lingered by the reception desk, her hand once more resting on the clock. The mechanism ticked on, steady and honest at last. She exhaled, the tension in her chest easing but not vanishing. Justice had come, but it was no clean victory. In the vernal gloom of the lobby, with the scent of rain and coffee in the air, Eleanor understood that the boundaries between right and wrong were as elusive as ever. The truth was named, but the cost would linger-etched into the marble, the memories, and the ticking of the clock.
