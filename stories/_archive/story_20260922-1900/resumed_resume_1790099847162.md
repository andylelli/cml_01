Frances smiled, arranging a stack of correspondence with deliberate care. "I do like to be seen, Inspector. It's so much easier than being believed."

Ottoline Dunmore hovered by the door, her notebook clutched to her chest. "Everyone's accounted for, then. Except me." She pressed her back against the cool wood, her breath shallow.

Ambrose gave a wry smile. "Everyone but the clock itself, which still refuses to keep honest time." He moved to the writing desk, straightening a blotter. "We know the chime sounded twenty minutes before the hour, and that the hands of the clock do not align. The mechanism was tampered with-deliberately."

Percival, unable to resist, delivered a speech: "Inspector, your obsession with the clock borders on the romantic. Perhaps it's not about time at all-perhaps it's about power, or legacy, or the peculiar thrill of seeing the whole household dance to one person's tune. If you're searching for a villain, you'll need to look beyond the clockwork."

"Or behind it," Ottoline murmured, her eyes fixed on the carpet.

Ambrose set down his pen, the sound sharp in the hush. "The evidence points away from the obvious suspects. Percival, Josephine, and Frances all have corroborated alibis for the window between ten minutes past three and twenty-five minutes past three. Only someone with documented access and mechanical skill could have arranged the clock's deception."

Josephine's flat answer to Ambrose's direct question about her movements: "Kitchen, the whole time." Ambrose moved on.

Ottoline, pressed for her view, listened to Frances's long speculation about the nature of guilt and the allure of secrets before replying with six words: "Order hides chaos, not the reverse."

Frances, not to be outdone, busied herself with the decanter, pouring a finger of sherry into the nearest glass. "To the end of mysteries," she toasted, the crystal catching the firelight.

Ambrose watched the liquid swirl, the gold catching at the rim. "We're left with the clock as both witness and accomplice. Someone used it to create a false alibi-and only one person could have managed the trick."

Josephine slid a coaster beneath the glass, her expression unreadable. "You'll have your answer soon enough, Inspector," she said, her voice flat.

Ambrose gathered the statements, aligning the papers with almost obsessive neatness. "The evidence eliminates all but one. The clock's lie was not a mistake-it was a message. Now we must decide to whom it was addressed."

The fire crackled, sending a shower of sparks up the chimney. Percival stared into the flames, his face illuminated by the shifting light. "All that's left is for you to name your culprit, Inspector."

Ambrose's eyes flicked to Ottoline, then to the clock in the hall, whose ticking seemed, for a moment, to falter and resume.

Outside, twilight pressed against the windows, the world beyond the glass receding into shadow. In the sitting room, the truth hovered, waiting to be claimed.

The company sat in uneasy silence, the pieces of the puzzle scattered before them, each waiting to see who would be called upon to account for the lost minutes, and the life stolen in their passage.

## Chapter 7: A Glove and a Growing Puzzle

"See here, Inspector," Ottoline Dunmore said, her voice wavering as she knelt beside the grand manor clock in the half-lit clock room. Late evening pressed against the leaded windows, and the air was close with the scent of machine oil and old wood. She reached beneath the clock's casing, fingers brushing against a torn fragment of glove wedged near the mechanism.

Ambrose Halloway crouched beside her, retrieving the fragment with a gloved hand and holding it up to the lamplight. "A match for yours, Miss Dunmore?" he asked, his gaze steady. He set the torn piece on the clock's mahogany shelf, its edges frayed but its color unmistakable.

Ottoline swallowed, then nodded. "I... must have snagged it while adjusting the clock. I didn't notice at the time." She smoothed her skirt, her hands trembling as she brushed dust from the hem.

Ambrose examined the clock's interior, careful not to disturb the delicate gears. "You had cause to open it, given your duties. But the timing is rather convenient." He set a small wrench next to the glove fragment, his pen poised over his notebook.

"Convenience doesn't make guilt," Ottoline replied, her eyes fixed on the scarred floorboards.

The clock's pendulum swung in its uneven arc, the motion reflected in the brass fob watch Ambrose placed on the shelf. "We're left with a mechanism that's been altered, a glove implicating you, and a chime that sounded at the wrong time. Yet the motive remains shrouded."

Percival Thorne's voice carried from the doorway, sharp and theatrical: "Inspector, you seem to be constructing a whole edifice of suspicion out of a scrap of leather and a few ill-tempered springs. If you ask me, the only thing this clock is guilty of is old age-and perhaps a little vanity." He leaned on his cane, the silver handle glinting in the lamplight.

Ambrose's flat answer, when asked if he truly suspected Ottoline: "Not yet certain." He closed his notebook with a snap.

Ottoline rose, brushing her palms together. "It's curious-the more we seek order, the more disorder we find. The glove proves nothing by itself, but the clock can't lie about when it was last opened." She lifted the clock's faceplate, revealing fresh fingerprints on the brass surround.

Ambrose compared the fragment to Ottoline's other glove, the match undeniable. He set both pieces on the narrow shelf, arranging them side by side. "Glove fragments, fingerprints, altered mechanisms-each a thread. The pattern will emerge."

Percival stepped further into the room, tapping the grandfather clock's pendulum. "You're determined to find a villain among us, Inspector. But perhaps the real culprit is the estate itself-a place built on secrets and silence."

Ottoline replied with a short retort: "Estate's innocent. People aren't." Percival's mouth twisted, but he let the matter drop.

Ambrose peered at the logbook lying open on the worktable, its most recent entry dated the day of the murder. He ran his finger down the column of maintenance notes, pausing at a smudged signature. "All recent adjustments by Ottoline Dunmore, including one marked shortly before the chime."

Ottoline picked up a brass winding key, turning it over in her hand. "Responsibility often falls to those who least desire it," she said, setting the key down with a soft click.

The room was silent but for the ticking clock and the faint rustle of papers as Ambrose turned the logbook's pages. "We're close," he said quietly. "The answer is somewhere between the last chime and the first lie."

Ottoline leaned against the clock, her reflection wavering in the glass. "It's a strange feeling, being watched by time itself. Every tick a reminder of what's lost-and what cannot be reclaimed."

Ambrose gathered the glove fragment, the winding key, and the logbook, arranging them in a careful row on the shelf. "Objects don't lie, but people do. It's the task of the detective to know which is which."

Outside, a breeze rattled the windowpane, and for a moment the clock's ticking seemed to accelerate, as if hurrying toward a conclusion none could yet see.

Ottoline straightened, smoothing her hair with trembling fingers. "There's nothing left but to wait, is there, Inspector?"

Ambrose pocketed the glove fragment, his gaze lingering on the clock's defiant face. "Wait, and test what we think we know-against what the clock will tell us."

The room settled into stillness, the puzzle gathering weight with each passing tick.

## Chapter 8: The Timed Clock Test

"Let's see what the clock reveals, Inspector," Ottoline Dunmore said, her voice barely above a whisper as she and Ambrose Halloway stood in the manor clock room early the next morning. The air was brisk, the morning sun throwing long bars of light across the carpet as the clockmaker's report lay open on the table between them.

Ambrose adjusted his spectacles, running a finger down the neat columns of the report. "The clock's striking sequence," he read, "was advanced by repositioning the detent lever. The chime sounded at twenty-five minutes past three, while the face displayed a quarter to four. The victim's watch, stopped at ten minutes past three, doesn't match the apparent chime, but aligns with the true time of death." He set the report down, its edges curling slightly.

Ottoline took up her logbook, flipping to the most recent entry. "Here-maintenance recorded the morning of the murder. But there's a second entry, unlogged, the handwriting... mine." She pressed her hand to the page, her pulse visible in the tremor of her wrist.

Ambrose lifted the magnifying glass and examined the clock mechanism. "Your fingerprints here, Ottoline-on the detent lever, the escapement, the winding key. No other prints recent enough to matter." He set the glass aside, the lens casting a distorted reflection on the table's varnish.

Ottoline's shoulders slumped. "I wanted to keep the clocks running. I didn't mean... I didn't mean for any of this."

Ambrose's tone softened. "Intentions matter. But the facts remain: the clock was tampered with, the time of death falsified, your logbook altered." He touched the clock's brass face, feeling the cool metal.

Frances Orme entered, her posture immaculate, her eyes sharp. "You believe the mechanism can settle what people cannot," she said, eyeing the report. "Objects have no loyalty to the living."

Ambrose nodded. "The test is simple. We'll replicate the sequence, using the same adjustments noted in the logbook. If the chime sounds early, we'll have proof the clock's displayed time was false." He handed Ottoline the winding key.

Ottoline hesitated, then set the detent lever as she had before. She wound the clock, her breath catching as the mechanism clicked into place.

Ambrose checked his pocket watch, announcing, "It's exactly ten minutes past three." He nodded for Ottoline to proceed.

The clock's gears engaged, the striking train whirred, and-at twenty-five minutes past three-the chime rang out, though the clock face insisted it was still a quarter to four.

Frances, watching, gave a speech: "Well, Inspector, it seems you've caught the ghost in the machine. The clock lies, the watch is honest, and all that's left is to decide which of us is clever enough to use such a trick to their own advantage. I suppose it's almost admirable, in its way-if only the result weren't so tragic."

"Truth is less clever," Ottoline replied, her voice barely audible.

Ambrose turned to Frances. "You saw the test performed. The clock was set to deceive-the chime creates an alibi where none should exist."

Frances arched an eyebrow, her lips pursed. "And the fingerprints? The altered logbook?"

Ambrose gestured to the evidence, his voice measured. "Both point in one direction. Whoever altered the clock had to know its secrets-and leave silent traces behind."

Ottoline folded her hands, the morning light glinting off her wedding band. "I thought if I could control the time, I could control the outcome. But the truth always finds its hour."

Ambrose set the winding key on the clock's base, his gaze steady. "Objects don't lie, Ottoline. People do. The test tells us everything we need to know."

The clock ticked on, untroubled, as outside the first birdsong of morning rose above the hush.

For a moment, the room was filled only with the quiet certainty of proof-the chime still echoing in the minds of all who had heard it, now exposed for the false comfort it had been.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"Let us be clear, then," Ambrose Halloway announced, standing before the assembled company in the manor sitting room late that morning. Sunlight spilled through the tall windows, catching the dust motes swirling in the still air. He set the evidence-the glove fragment, the logbook, the clockmaker's report-on the low table, each object an accusation and a record.

Percival Thorne perched on the arm of a leather chair, his foot tapping a restless rhythm. "Well, Inspector, do we finally have the pleasure of knowing who ruined Lockwood's peace?" His voice was edged with relief and something darker.

Josephine Rutherford folded her arms, her gaze fixed on the glove fragment. "You're not blaming the staff again, are you? Because I've had enough of that particular tune." She smoothed the nap of the settee, her jaw set. It was the third night in a week she'd slept in the nursery, too restless to stay in her own room since the murder.

Ambrose inclined his head. "No, Mrs. Rutherford. You, Miss Orme, and Mr. Thorne are all cleared. The evidence is specific-your fingerprints on the inner dial, the log entries marking your entry just before the murder, and the clockmaker's test showing the adjustment happened at that hour. It can only be Ottoline Dunmore."

Frances Orme crossed the room, her heels clicking on the parquet. "So the clocks are honest after all, Inspector? Or just less cunning than the rest of us?"

Ambrose picked up the logbook, weighing it in his palm. "Honest in their way, Miss Orme. The clock told us who had opportunity, and the test proved intent."

Percival, unable to resist, delivered a speech: "So all these hours, all this suspicion, and it comes down to Ottoline-our very own timekeeper. I suppose there's a lesson in that: never trust a person who insists on punctuality. Or perhaps, never trust anyone at Lockwood at all."

Ottoline stood by the window, her face pale. "I only wanted-"

"You wanted control," Frances interrupted, voice smooth as silk. "And you got it, for a time."

Josephine's flat answer to Frances's question about her own feelings: "Glad it's over." Frances shrugged, moving to pour herself a cup of tea.

Ottoline's shoulders shook. "I thought I could fix everything if I could only fix the time. I didn't mean for it to end like this."

Ambrose's voice was calm, final. "Ottoline Dunmore killed Beatrice Whitlock and tried to use the clock to disguise the hour and her own absence. The deception failed because the evidence of time-the victim's watch and the clock's mechanism-could not be made to agree."

The room stilled, the weight of the revelation settling over them like dust. Josephine brushed a crumb from the table, her movements slow and deliberate.

Percival looked away, his hand tightening on the chair's finial. Frances regarded Ottoline with a mixture of pity and contempt, her lips pressed into a thin line.

Ambrose gathered the evidence, sliding it back into his case. "Justice will see to the rest," he said quietly.

Outside, the manor clock chimed the hour-this time, for once, precisely as it should.

## Chapter 10: The Culprit Revealed

"Order has been restored, after a fashion," Ambrose Halloway announced, standing once more in the manor drawing room as the afternoon sun flickered across the polished floorboards. The case was closed: Ottoline Dunmore had confessed-her trembling voice echoing in the high-ceilinged hall, as constables led her past the assembled household, her face pale with resignation.

Percival Thorne stood at the window, hands clasped behind his back, the lines of his face softened by the unexpected reprieve. "So, Inspector, what becomes of us now?" he asked, fingers tracing the carved sill as he watched a pair of doves settle on the garden wall.

Ambrose set his battered notebook on the mantel and removed his hat, the gesture final. "For me, it's back to London. And perhaps a little less faith in punctuality, even in the country." He glanced at the clock, now ticking with measured certainty, its chime echoing down the hall.

Josephine Rutherford, polishing a silver tray, gave a deadpan reply to Ambrose's question about her plans: "Same work. Fewer secrets." She stacked the tray atop a neat pile, her face unreadable.

Percival turned from the window, his voice unusually soft. "I'll take up the estate's management again-under the solicitor's watchful eye, I expect. No more shortcuts, no more codicils in the dark." He set a ledger on the writing desk, the leather cover cool beneath his hand.

Frances Orme arranged a bouquet of lilies in the tall vase, her movements precise. "Society will have its gossip, but I find I prefer the quiet after a storm. Lockwood will recover. It always does."

Ambrose watched as Josephine straightened the curtains, smoothing the heavy fabric with a practiced hand. "You'll keep the lock running, Mrs. Rutherford?"

"That's how the canal runs, isn't it?" she replied, her tone as steady as the water itself. She glanced at the clock, satisfied to see its hands aligned at last.

Percival, picking up the estate keys from the tray, couldn't resist one last retort, responding to Josephine's long musings on estate life: "No more missing candlesticks, then." Josephine grunted, unimpressed, and returned to her work.

Ambrose tucked his notebook away, pausing to gaze at a photograph of Beatrice Whitlock as a young woman-poised beside a row of roses, her head thrown back in laughter. In that image, there was no hint of the iron will or the tragedies to come, only the memory of a summer afternoon lost to time.

The drawing room, once a stage for suspicion and grief, was returned to its proper order: the clock restored, the candlesticks gleaming, the estate's routines quietly reasserting themselves. Outside, the distant sound of the canal gate opening mingled with the low hum of bees in the lavender.

For a brief, golden moment, Lockwood was simply a home again-the scent of beeswax and lilies drifting on the air, the mantel clock marking each second, and sunlight warming the faded carpet in a patchwork of peace.
