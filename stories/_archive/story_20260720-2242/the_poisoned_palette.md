## Chapter 10: The Culprit Revealed

The sound of rain tapping the windows blended with the hush that settled over the hotel Dining Room, where the soft glow of lamplight struggled to warm the dim, wintry night. The scent of extinguished candles hung in the air, mingling with the last traces of coffee and the faint aroma of cold, savory meats. Eleanor Voss kept her hand wrapped around Beatrice Quill's, the pressure a fragile reassurance in the aftermath of Dr. Mallory Finch's confession. Darkness pressed at the tall glass, and the cloudy reflections on the table turned every face ghostly, every gesture uncertain. Captain Ivor Hale shifted in his seat, the fabric of his navy blue suit creaking as he leaned forward, elbows on the linen. His voice, low and measured, cut through the silence. 'It's done, then,' he said, though the words were more question than statement.

The storm outside had slackened, but the tension within the room clung as stubbornly as the scent of burnt wicks. Hugo Vane, seated across from Hale, drummed his fingers once on the tabletop, then fell still. 'It doesn't feel finished,' Hugo murmured, the bravado stripped from his tone. 'Not with so much left broken. ' Eleanor released Beatrice's hand and reached for the decanter, its cut crystal surface now only a reminder of the ordeal. She turned to Captain Hale. Because your presence is firmly recorded, and the timeline is unbreakable, you could not have handled the decanter. That evidence holds. ' The relief on Captain Hale's face was subtle-a loosening of his jaw, a slow exhale-but it was there.

Hugo Vane's gaze flickered toward Eleanor, wary but grateful. 'And me? ' he asked, voice raw. Eleanor met her eyes. 'You are innocent, Hugo. You were in the lounge with other guests, discussing art and the new radar equipment, when the drinks were poured. Multiple witnesses confirm your alibi. Because your presence was witnessed and the timeline is precise, you could not have tampered with the decanter. ' Hugo's posture eased, his shoulders settling as if a physical weight had been lifted. Beatrice Quill, her fingers still trembling, seemed unable to speak. Eleanor's voice was gentle but certain. 'Beatrice, your alibi is confirmed. The housekeeper saw you in the lounge, fetching your sketchbook, as the waiter set the steaming roast between them. You were not present at the sideboard when the decanter was last handled.

Because this evidence is clear, you are ruled out. ' At last, Beatrice exhaled-a shaky, half-sob of relief-and pressed a hand to her face. The Dining Room held its silence, the only sound the soft patter of rain and the faint rustle of newspapers left behind by guests who had long since retreated. Dr. Mallory Finch sat apart, hands folded in her lap, eyes downcast. Her confession lingered in the air-the words spoken moments before, brittle and irrevocable. 'I killed Sylvia Trent,' Dr. Finch had said, her voice stripped of pretense. 'I poisoned her because I was afraid-afraid of what she would expose, of how it would destroy someone I cared for. The bonds between the survivors were frayed, yet not wholly severed-each person marked by what they had lost and what they had learned.
