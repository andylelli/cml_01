He thrived on our fear. " Eleanor felt the bitterness of the truth settle in her chest. The motive was not greed, not in the simple sense, but the suffocating pressure of financial ruin. Dr. Finch had acted to save herself from disgrace, perhaps even to shield others from Hugo Vane's predations. Yet the cost was a life-and the shattering of her own soul. The door opened, and the sound of footsteps in the corridor signaled the arrival of an officer from the village, summoned by Sylvia Trent's earlier telegram. Eleanor stepped aside as Dr. Finch straightened, her hands clasped before her. She did not resist as she was led away, her head bowed, the weight of her actions pressing her down. The echo of her confession lingered in the cold air, a final, irrevocable note.

Eleanor remained in the room, the silence broken only by the faint sound of the wind and the distant crash of waves against the cliffs. The clock, its hands forever fixed at ten minutes past eleven, seemed less an accuser now than a monument to the lengths desperation could drive a person. She thought of Beatrice, who would be spared ruin; of Captain Hale, whose dignity was restored; of Sylvia, whose pen would capture the story for those who needed to believe in justice. A moment later, Beatrice appeared at the threshold, her eyes red but her voice steady. "Thank you, Eleanor. For believing me. For seeing the truth. " Captain Hale lingered in the corridor, his posture less rigid, a quiet respect in his nod. Sylvia offered a brief, approving glance, her rivalry with Eleanor softened by the ordeal they had shared.

Eleanor's gaze lingered on the clock, the cold brass now warm beneath her palm. The investigation had ended-not with triumph, but with the weary knowledge that justice, such as it was, had come at a cost. The Cliffside Coastal Hotel would carry the scars of this autumn, but for the first time, the air felt lighter, the gloom less oppressive. Outside, the wind shifted, carrying with it the scent of salt and the promise-however fragile-of peace. She closed her eyes, letting the silence settle. The case was over, but the memory of what had been lost-and what had been spared-would remain. When she opened them again, the light had changed, and the world beyond the window seemed, if not brighter, at least less shadowed. Eleanor turned from the mantel, her footsteps soft on the carpet, and left the room behind, the clock's silent testimony echoing in her wake.
