Beatrice's shoulders stiffened, and for a moment, she seemed to shrink into herself. 'Simple? ' Beatrice echoed, her voice unsteady. 'Nothing about Sylvia was ever simple. She-she had a way of making you feel as if you were always on the edge of something. Approval, disappointment, I never quite knew which. ' She hesitated, glancing at Eleanor as if weighing how much to reveal. 'We were friends, yes. But there was always an... understanding. I envied her, sometimes. Her confidence. The way she could walk into a room and make everyone notice. ' The admission hung between them, raw and unvarnished. A faint breeze rustled the leaves overhead, and Eleanor let the moment linger, her own breath slow and steady. She thought of the photograph she'd seen earlier in the dining room-Beatrice, Dr.

Finch, and Sylvia, all smiling, a relic of lighter days. 'Envy can make us do strange things,' Eleanor said quietly, her words more observation than accusation. 'But it doesn't make us murderers. ' Beatrice's laugh was sharp, almost desperate. 'If envy were a crime, half the county would be behind bars. ' She pressed her gloved hand to her forehead, as if to steady herself. 'I was at the market that morning. You can ask anyone. I bought tea, sugar, what little there was to be had. I saw Sylvia later, yes, but only in passing. I-' She broke off, her voice catching. 'I didn't want her to see me. Not after what was said the night before. ' Eleanor's gaze sharpened. 'What was said? ' The question was soft, but it cut deep. Beatrice hesitated, her eyes shining in the dim light. 'We argued. About the hotel, about Hugo Vane's schemes. She thought I was too eager to please him.

She said I'd sell my soul for a place at the table. ' The bitterness in Beatrice's voice was unmistakable, and for a moment, Eleanor glimpsed the ambition and insecurity that lay beneath the surface. The garden's silence pressed in, broken only by the distant clatter of crockery from the Dining Area. Eleanor turned the conversation, her tone shifting. 'Dr. Finch's records show she was with you until eight twenty-five. Captain Hale's duty roster puts him in the bar. The witnesses at the market confirm your errands. The facts, as they stand, seem to clear you. ' She watched Beatrice's reaction, searching for relief, for guilt, for something more. Beatrice's mouth twisted in a wry smile. 'So the facts are in my favour for once.

How fortunate. ' She glanced away, her eyes following the flicker of lamplight from the hotel windows. 'But facts are only as good as the people who keep them, aren't they? Clocks can be set, stories rehearsed. I suppose you'll have to decide which you trust more. ' Eleanor let the words settle, her mind racing. The contradiction between the lounge clock-fixed at ten minutes past eleven-and the web of alibis and witness statements was now undeniable. The medical records, the duty roster, the market's busy chatter-all pointed away from Beatrice. Yet the tension in Beatrice's posture, the evasive glances, the bitterness in her tone, all suggested a relationship with Sylvia that ran deeper than friendship and more complicated than rivalry.

A sudden gust of wind rattled the branches overhead, and Beatrice flinched, her composure slipping. 'You think I'm lying,' she said, not quite a question. 'You think I had something to do with it. ' Eleanor shook her head, her voice gentle. 'I think you're afraid, Miss Quill. But not of me. ' The words seemed to reach Beatrice, who let out a shaky breath, her bravado faltering. For a moment, the tension eased, replaced by a fragile understanding. Beatrice's eyes glistened, and she looked away, blinking rapidly. 'What's life without a little drama? ' she murmured, the old quip surfacing, but this time it sounded hollow. Eleanor offered a small, wry smile, a silent acknowledgment of the burdens they both carried. The garden, once a place of order and beauty, now felt heavy with secrets. Eleanor closed her notebook, the pages filled with contradictions and half-truths.

The investigation had reached a turning point: the facts no longer fit the story she'd been told. Beatrice's nervous demeanor, her evasions, and the complicated bond with Sylvia had upended Eleanor's assumptions. The truth, it seemed, was more elusive than ever. Eleanor glanced down at her notes, then looked up with a steady gaze. 'Dr. Finch was attending another patient at the time of the murder,' she said aloud, tapping the page. 'Captain Hale was on duty in the bar, and Sylvia-well, she was the victim, so that clears her from suspicion. ' She paused, then added, 'But Hugo Vane's fingerprints were found near the mechanism that stopped the clock. ' Eleanor closed her notebook and looked thoughtfully at Beatrice. 'Dr.

Finch was with another patient at the exact moment the clock stopped-ten minutes past eleven,' she said. 'Captain Hale's duty roster confirms he was in the bar then, and Sylvia, being the victim, obviously couldn't have been involved. ' She tapped the page again. 'But Hugo Vane's fingerprints on the clock's mechanism suggest he had the opportunity to tamper with it. ' As the first drops of rain began to fall again, Eleanor lingered beneath the linden, her mind racing through the evidence-the clock's stubborn hands, the scratches on its case, the web of alibis, the witnesses who saw what they wished. She realized, with a chill, that the answer lay not in the facts alone, but in the spaces between them. The garden lights flickered on, casting long shadows across the wet gravel, and Eleanor Voss understood that the next question would be more dangerous than any she had asked before.

## Chapter 7: The Discriminating Test

Eleanor Voss set her notebook on the low table, the soft thud swallowed by the hush that had settled over the hotel lounge. Rain streaked the tall windows, a persistent whisper against the glass, while lamplight threw long shadows across the fixtures. The air was thick with damp wool and a faint tang of salt, a reminder that the storm had only just passed. Night pressed close against the building, and each guest sat on the edge of a chair, wary and watchful, as though the dark outside might slip in and claim someone else before morning. Hugo Vane stood apart, his double-breasted suit immaculate, but his posture rigid as a sentry's. He adjusted his cufflinks with deliberate care, eyes fixed on the mantelpiece clock. Beatrice Quill sat nearest the window, her hands folded tightly in her lap, the silk scarf at her throat knotted too high. Dr.

Mallory Finch hovered near the drinks cabinet, her blazer buttoned to her throat despite the warmth, spectacles catching the lamplight. Captain Ivor Hale, arms crossed, leaned against the wall, his gaze averted from the others. Eleanor's own heart beat an anxious rhythm as she looked them over, the coroner's report a weight in her pocket and Sylvia Trent's lifeless form never far from her thoughts. She drew a slow breath, steadying herself. 'Thank you all for coming,' Eleanor began, her voice even but edged with resolve. 'There is a matter that cannot wait. ' Her gaze lingered on the clock above the mantel, its brass face gleaming dully, hands unmoved since the morning's discovery. 'We have relied too much on what we wish to believe. Tonight, we must rely on what can be proven. ' Beatrice's voice, brittle, broke the silence. 'Are we to sit here all night, Miss Voss?

The war will be over before you're satisfied. ' The attempt at levity fell flat, her laughter dying in the thick air. Captain Hale's jaw tightened, but he said nothing, his eyes flicking to the clock and away again. Dr. Finch shifted her weight, the creak of leather betraying her unease. Eleanor let the jibe pass and reached for the clock, lifting it from the mantel with care. The scratches along its case caught the lamplight, a scatter of marks that had not been there before the night of the murder. 'This clock,' she said, 'has been sitting here as though it saw everything that happened in this room. ' She turned it slightly, so the light fell differently across the case. 'But a witness can be made to lie. ' For a moment no one breathed.

Hugo Vane's composure faltered, his fingers drumming a nervous tattoo against his thigh. 'If you're suggesting someone tampered with it, you'll need more than innuendo, Miss Voss. ' His tone was sharp, but his eyes did not quite meet hers. Beatrice shifted in her seat, the silk of her dress rustling. Dr. Finch's hand went to her wrist, where a slim watch peeked from beneath her cuff. Eleanor seized on the gesture. 'Dr. Finch, may I see your watch? ' The request was polite, but left no room for refusal. Dr. Finch hesitated, then unfastened the strap and handed it over. The watch was small, silver, its face clear and unblemished. Eleanor held it up beside the clock, so both faces caught the lamp at once.

The lounge fell silent but for the rain and the faint tick of the second hand. 'Ten minutes past eleven,' she read aloud, turning the wristwatch so the room could see the numbers for themselves. Then she turned the clock. Its hands stood exactly where they had stood since the body was found - at the same ten minutes past eleven. She waited. Dr. Finch checked her own watch again, frowning, and Captain Hale muttered the time under his breath as if repeating it might make it agree. It did not. The clock had not moved since morning; the watch had gone on ticking all the while. Captain Hale's brow furrowed, his voice rough with disbelief. 'That's not possible. I saw the clock myself-' He broke off. Beatrice's eyes widened, her lips parting without a sound. Hugo Vane's jaw clenched, and he looked away, the color draining from his face. Dr.

Finch's hands trembled as she took her watch back, fumbling the clasp. Eleanor set the clock back on the mantel, her movements unhurried. 'Someone wanted us to believe Sylvia Trent died at a time that suited their story. ' She said nothing more, only watched the faces in front of her - a flicker here, a stillness there - while whatever fragile agreement had held the group together came quietly apart in the lamplight. A long silence followed, broken only by the distant wireless - Bing Crosby's voice, faint and melancholy, drifting in from the Staff Quarters. The ordinary world pressed at the edges of the extraordinary, and for a moment Eleanor felt the weight of what she had set in motion. She caught Beatrice's gaze and saw tears standing unshed. Captain Hale's fists were clenched at his sides, the wariness in his eyes now tinged with something closer to dread. Dr.

Finch stared at the floor, jaw set, as if bracing for an accusation that had not yet come. Hugo Vane was the last to speak, his tone brittle with forced calm. 'You've made your point, Miss Voss. But what does it prove? Anyone could have touched that clock. ' The challenge came out hollow, his bravado fraying at the edges, and Eleanor heard the fear underneath it more clearly than the words themselves. The tension in the lounge had become a thing you could almost touch. And yet, underneath it, Eleanor felt something closer to relief - the sense that once a fact was seen plainly, it could not be argued back into shadow. The path ahead was still dangerous. But it was no longer formless. She gathered her notebook, her hands steady at last. 'We are not finished,' she said quietly, 'but tonight, the clock has spoken.

The next question is: who gave it its voice? ' She named no one, but the words landed all the same. The group sat frozen, caught between suspicion and fear, the truth close now - but not yet claimed by anyone in the room. As Eleanor turned away from the mantel, the rain came harder, drumming against the glass. For a moment the war outside, the blackout, the whole grinding shortage of ordinary life, seemed impossibly far away beside what had just happened in this room. In the lamplight, the faces around her were etched with uncertainty, each one holding something back. The night was far from over, and the reckoning had only just begun.

## Chapter 8: Clearing the Suspects

"Let's not pretend we can sleep now," Eleanor said, her voice steady as rain battered the tall windows streaked with rain. Night wrapped the room in a heavy hush, lamplight casting flickering shadows across the faces gathered in a tense semicircle. The scent of spilled gin and lingering smoke drifted through the air, and the sound of water trickling down the glass was relentless, almost hypnotic. Eleanor's eyes darted from the clock above the mantel-its hands fixed at ten minutes past eleven-to the guests, each of whom seemed to hold their breath in the dim, summery gloom. The clock's brass face gleamed in the lamplight, its unmoving hands marking a moment that would not release them. Eleanor's gaze lingered on it, the contradiction clawing at her composure. She remembered the early assertion: the clock, lounge, shows, minutes, nine.

Now, the time before her was an accusation, not an alibi. A hush settled as she walked to the mantel. The air was thick with anticipation, the rain outside a steady drum that underscored the silence within. Hugo Vane broke the quiet with a brittle, impatient cough. "You've gone over these details again and again, Miss Voss. If you have something to say, now's the time. " His words were clipped, but beneath them trembled a note of uncertainty. Eleanor's attention shifted, registering the tension in Hugo Vane's posture-the rigidity of his shoulders, the way his hand hovered near his jacket pocket as if reaching for a shield that wasn't there. Captain Ivor Hale stood by the window, arms crossed, his gaze fixed on the rain-laced darkness outside. The lamplight caught the silver at his temples, lending him a kind of weary dignity. Eleanor drew a breath and addressed him first.

"Captain Hale, your account places you in the bar with low lamps flickering until nearly nine. The duty roster, left open on the desk, confirms your shift. Staff saw you at the register at half past and again at closing. There's no gap unexplained. " She met his eyes, searching for defiance, but found only resignation. "You couldn't have left without being noticed. Your alibi holds. " The captain's jaw tightened, but he did not protest. The wariness in his eyes shifted to something quieter-relief, perhaps, or exhaustion. He looked away, his fingers relaxing against his sleeve. The suspicion that had clung to him for hours now slipped free, leaving only the weight of what he had witnessed, not what he had done. Eleanor turned to Dr. Mallory Finch, who stood apart, her blazer buttoned too high, hands clasped tightly before her. "Dr. Finch, your time is accounted for as well.

Beatrice Quill confirms you left her room at twenty-five past eight. The medical records show your signature at that hour, and the staff remember your voice in the corridor. No one saw you elsewhere, and your presence was required. If there was motive, it never took shape-your feelings for Sylvia were unreturned, not hidden. " Dr. Finch's composure wavered. She looked to the floor, her lips pressed together, then nodded once. The tension in her posture eased, replaced by a kind of wounded pride. Eleanor saw in a woman who had risked vulnerability, only to have it dismissed. The suspicion that had shadowed Dr. Finch faded, replaced by something more like regret. Beatrice Quill's turn came next. She sat at the edge of her seat, her silk scarf twisted in nervous hands. Eleanor's tone softened. "Miss Quill, you were in your room from eight to half-past. Dr.

Finch's record book and the duty roster confirm it. The market's shopkeeper vouched for your errands. Your whereabouts are not in doubt. Whatever argument you had with Sylvia, it was not cause enough for murder. " Beatrice's eyes glistened in the lamplight, and she let out a shaky breath. Her bravado had been spent hours ago; what remained was a battered honesty. "If envy were motive enough, I'd have more to answer for," she said, voice low. The group's gaze shifted, and for the first time, suspicion visibly departed from her. A fragile sense of relief took its place. Eleanor paused, letting the silence settle, each cleared suspect relaxing minutely as the focus drifted away from them. The air in the lounge was still tense, but its target had shifted. Eleanor's gaze landed at last on Hugo Vane. The room seemed to hold its breath.

Rain battered the windows with renewed force, and the clock's face stood implacable, unmoved. Eleanor's voice was measured, but the weight of her words pressed into the hush. "Hugo Vane, your account places you in the study from the clink of glasses until half past. The staff confirm your presence there-at least, for part of that time. But the opportunity, the means, and the knowledge of how this clock might deceive us... those are not so easily dismissed. " A flicker of fear crossed Hugo Vane's features, quickly masked by defiance. "You think I had something to do with it because I know how these things work? That's not proof, Miss Voss. That's prejudice. " Yet his gaze darted to the clock, then to Eleanor, and away again. He shifted in his seat, the confidence of his earlier protestations draining away.

Eleanor's eyes moved to each of the others, the burden of suspicion now fully shifted. She saw the cost in their faces: Captain Hale's relief, Dr. Finch's wounded dignity, Beatrice's exhaustion. The clock's testimony, the witness accounts, and the physical records had all conspired to clear them, leaving only uncertainty in the heart of the man who now stood alone. A moment of quiet fell, weighted with the understanding that the circle had closed. The clock, the records, the memories of the storm-tossed night-all pointed, not by accident, but by process of elimination. This was no trick of history, no convenient repetition. It was the slow, methodical work of watching, questioning, and refusing to be satisfied with half-truths. The tension in the lounge did not break, but changed shape, becoming watchful rather than accusatory. Eleanor's hands were steady as she closed her notebook.

"We have ruled out the innocent," she said quietly. "What remains must be faced. " Her words hung in the air, and for a long moment, no one moved. Outside, the rain slackened, leaving only the echo of water on glass and the silent, unmoving clock as witnesses to what had passed. In that hush, the group understood-some with relief, some with dread-that the reckoning was no longer a question of who, but when the truth would be spoken aloud. And in Eleanor's steady gaze, they saw that moment drawing near.

## Chapter 9: The Culprit Revealed

Late night pressed close against the wide panes of the hotel lounge, the darkness beyond broken only by the flicker of lamplight on polished brass. Eleanor Voss stood at the mantel, the scent of muddied coat and lingering smoke thick in the air, her hand resting on her notebook. The hush that had fallen after the last words of the previous hour was palpable-a waiting, weighted silence. The clock above her head, hands unmoving at ten minutes past eleven, seemed to hold its breath along with the guests. She let her eyes move from one face to another, feeling the tension coil tighter with every heartbeat. A soft shuffle of feet drew Eleanor's attention to Captain Ivor Hale, who lingered near the window, his silhouette outlined by the faint glow of the streetlamp outside.

The duty roster, left open on the side table, caught her gaze-a physical record, the inked lines confirming the bar schedule for the night in question. She crossed to it, her fingers tracing the neat handwriting. 'Captain Ivor Hale,' she said, her voice cutting through the silence, 'your alibi holds because the evidence in this roster matches the staff's statements. The timeline shows you were present in the bar from the first hush of evening until nearly nine, with witnesses confirming your presence at the register and closing. ' She realised, as she looked up at him, that no matter how she turned the facts, the timeline did not bend to suspicion. Captain Ivor Hale could not have left the bar unnoticed-not with staff and guests alike attesting to his movements.

The evidence ruled him out, and the relief in his posture was unmistakable, his arms finally uncrossing as if he could set down a burden. Eleanor's gaze shifted then to Dr. Mallory Finch, who stood apart, her blazer buttoned too high, hands clasped in front of her. The medical records lay atop the low table, Finch's signature clear at eight twenty-five. Eleanor approached, lifting the top sheet, the familiar scent of disinfectant clinging to the paper. 'Dr. Mallory Finch,' she said quietly, 'the evidence clears you because your signature in the medical log and Beatrice Quill's confirmation place you outside the lounge during the critical time. The alibi holds; the timeline does not allow you to have left unnoticed. ' As Eleanor spoke, she saw Dr. Finch's shoulders slacken, pride mingling with something like sorrow in her eyes. She realised that whatever tension had haunted Dr.

Finch was not guilt, but the pain of being suspected. The timeline was a shield, not just for the innocent, but for the wounded pride that came with suspicion. Turning, Eleanor let her attention settle on Beatrice Quill. The silk scarf at Beatrice's throat was twisted tight, her hands knotted in her lap. Near her, a folded shopping list, market receipts, and the staff's statements formed a small pile of ordinary evidence. Eleanor picked up the list, the faint scent of tea leaves and paper rising from it. 'Beatrice Quill,' she said, her tone gentler, 'the evidence clears you because the alibi holds. The market receipts and Dr. Finch's records confirm you were in your room or out running errands during the window in question. The timeline, the staff, and the medical log all rule you out. ' She could not help but wonder at the exhaustion in Beatrice's eyes as the words landed.

The suspicion that had dogged her was gone, replaced by a fragile relief. The evidence had done what words could not: it had made innocence visible, if not always comfortable. Eleanor returned to the mantel, the lounge clock looming above. The scratches along its case glinted in the lamplight-a scatter of marks, each a record of hurried hands or desperate action. She reached for the clock, her fingers brushing the cold brass, and felt the tension in the room shift. The clock's hands, unmoving since the morning, were a silent accusation. 'This clock,' she said, her voice steady, 'was meant to deceive us. It showed ten minutes past eleven, but the evidence-the coroner's report, the witness accounts, the physical timeline-proves that was not the truth. ' She let the silence linger, watching the faces before her.

Captain Ivor Hale, cleared by the evidence, now stood with arms at his sides, no longer defensive. Dr. Mallory Finch, her innocence confirmed by the timeline, looked to the floor, the weight of the day etched in her posture. Beatrice Quill, her alibi solid, pressed her hand to her scarf as if to reassure herself she was still present, still safe. The lounge itself seemed to exhale, the tension shifting as the circle of suspicion narrowed. Eleanor's gaze landed on Hugo Vane, who had maintained a studied calm throughout, yet whose eyes now darted between the clock and the others. The evidence had cleared the innocent; what remained was a question only the guilty could answer. A sudden gust of wind rattled the windowpanes, the sound sharp in the hush, and Eleanor's hand tightened on her notebook.

She let the moment hang, the air thick with the knowledge that the reckoning had not yet finished its work. The lounge, with its scattered shadows and lamplight, was no longer a place of comfort but of consequence. She moved to the low table, and in the pool of lamplight, something caught her eye-a torn piece of fabric, pale against the dark carpet, lying near the clock's base. She stooped, picking it up between thumb and forefinger, feeling the rough weave. The scrap was out of place, a remnant of struggle or hurried tampering. She held it up, letting the light catch the frayed edge. 'This,' she said softly, 'is the kind of detail that history preserves, whether or not we wish it. ' Hugo Vane's composure faltered, his gaze fixed on the fabric, then darting away. The silence pressed in, the group's attention narrowing to this new, silent witness.

Eleanor did not speak further; she let the evidence speak instead, the torn fabric a thread that would not be ignored. The room's atmosphere, charged and brittle, now carried a different weight-a sense that the story was nearly told. Eleanor caught the eyes of each guest in turn, seeing in their faces the relief of exoneration or the dread of what must come next. The consequences of the night's events settled on their shoulders, the knowledge that innocence and guilt alike had cost them something irreplaceable. Captain Ivor Hale moved first, crossing to the sideboard and pouring himself a glass of water, the tremor in his hand betraying the tension that had not yet left him. Dr. Mallory Finch gathered her records, her movements precise but weary, as though the act of being cleared had drained her more than accusation ever could.

Beatrice Quill let her scarf fall loose, her posture softening, the lines of anxiety easing from her face. Eleanor Voss glanced down at the torn fabric still clutched in her hand, then looked up to the group. 'This scrap,' she said, 'was found right beside the clock-an undeniable sign someone was close enough to tamper with it. ' Eleanor's eyes narrowed slightly as she held the fabric up again. 'Finding this here,' she said, 'means Hugo Vane must have been near the clock when it was set back to a quarter past ten. That small, overlooked scrap ties the tampering directly to this spot-and to whoever was careless enough to leave it behind. ' Eleanor remained by the mantel, the torn fabric still in her hand, her gaze never leaving Hugo Vane. The evidence had narrowed the circle, and the final reckoning was close.

But for now, the clock remained silent, the truth poised to emerge in the hours ahead. The night outside pressed against the windows, and within the lounge, history waited to be written-not by rumor or suspicion, but by the facts that could no longer be denied.
