Yet witness accounts and the physical evidence contradict your claim to have been in The Library at the critical time. The soot line on the brass latch, the timing of the fire, and the release of the bolt all point to deliberate manipulation. You alone possessed the means, the opportunity, and the motive-fear that the victim would reveal your financial scandal. Therefore, Reginald Smythe, you are charged with the murder. " Smythe's composure, so rigid until now, finally fractured. He looked at Griffin, then at each of the others, his eyes hollow.

"I did not wish for it to come to this," he said, voice raw. "The estate was on the brink. The victim threatened to expose everything-our debts, my failures. I thought I could control the outcome, buy time, perhaps frighten them into silence. But when I saw them in the room, I panicked. I stabbed them with the kitchen dagger-there was no plan for that, only fear. The mechanism, the fire, the latch... I used them to conceal what I had done, to delay discovery, to create the illusion of an impossible crime. " Griffin nodded, his expression grave. "It was not the mechanism alone that killed. The victim died by your hand, stabbed with a kitchen dagger. The brass latch, the fire, the delayed unlocking-these were but the tools you used to hide your act. The evidence is irrefutable: the ledger, the timing, the physical traces, your own knowledge and opportunity.

You will answer for this in law. " A heavy silence fell over the room as two officers, summoned from the village earlier, entered quietly and took Reginald Smythe into custody. He did not resist, only straightened his jacket and walked from the drawing room with the dignity of a man who knew his fate was sealed. The door closed behind him with a finality that left the others staring at the space where he had stood. For a long moment, no one spoke. Margaret 'Maggie' Collins wept openly, her relief at being cleared mingling with grief for the household's ruin. Evelyn Harcourt turned from the window, her face pale, eyes shining with unshed tears. "Justice," she whispered, "but at such a cost. " Charles Pembroke, voice hoarse, added, "We are none of us untouched. " Griffin offered no easy comfort, only a quiet, respectful silence.

Inspector Leonard Griffin, too, had been under the shadow of suspicion, if only by proximity and the relentless logic of the case. Yet the evidence had cleared him as well-his presence, his actions, and the transparency of his methods left no room for doubt. Inspector Leonard Griffin was cleared because his impartiality and the documented timeline confirmed he could not have committed the crime. As the late morning sunlight grew stronger, the Manor drawing room seemed both emptier and changed. The law had spoken, but the wounds left by betrayal and loss would linger. Outside, the world continued in bright, indifferent summer. Within, the survivors faced the slow work of rebuilding trust and order, each marked by the shadow of what had passed. Griffin closed his notebook at last, the case resolved, but the cost of justice etched deep into the silence that followed.

Reginald Smythe was responsible, and the evidence placed the matter beyond all reasonable doubt.
