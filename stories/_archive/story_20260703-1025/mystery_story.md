He felt the weight of the truth in his chest, a pressure that threatened to unmake him if he did not speak. He drew a breath, letting the cold, damp air settle his nerves. 'We have reached the end,' Hugo said quietly, his voice carrying in the dim room. 'The evidence is before us-all that remains is to name what it means. ' The lamplight shimmered on the clock's face, casting a warped shadow across Dr. Mallory Finch's hands. She did not look up. Beatrice Quill's pencil tapped a nervous rhythm against her notebook. 'You said you could prove it, Mr. Vane. Prove who killed Sylvia Trent. ' Her tone was brittle, but beneath it, Hugo heard the plea for certainty. He nodded, feeling the gaze of every person in the room settle on him-some with hope, others with dread. 'I can,' he replied, 'and I must. ' She turned first to Eleanor Voss. 'Eleanor Voss, your alibi is confirmed.

The hotel staff saw you in the office, and your signature appears in the ledger at intervals throughout the evening. Because the staff and the records both place you away from Sylvia Trent's room, and because you could not have slipped away unnoticed, you are cleared. ' Eleanor's posture sagged, her composure slipping at last. She pressed a hand to her lips, relief and exhaustion mingling in her eyes. Next, Hugo faced Beatrice Quill. 'Beatrice Quill, your presence in the lobby at the time of death is confirmed. The staff and several guests recall your whereabouts, and you never left their sight. Therefore, you are innocent of this crime. ' Beatrice let out a breath she had been holding, her shoulders trembling as the accusation lifted. Her notebook slipped from her grasp, falling to the rug with a soft thud.

He turned to Captain Ivor Hale, whose jaw was clenched, arms folded tight. 'Captain Ivor Hale, your alibi holds. The staff and the financial ledger confirm you were in the lobby from 9:15 PM to 10:15 PM. Because you could not have left without being noticed, you are ruled out. ' Hale's eyes closed for a moment, the relief almost painful in its intensity. He nodded, once, curtly. Hugo paused, feeling the heat of every gaze. He forced himself onward. 'And as for myself-Hugo Vane-I was in the company of the staff and other guests, my movements accounted for by multiple witnesses. Therefore, I am cleared. ' The words felt strange, spoken aloud, but necessary. The circle of suspicion had narrowed to one. She turned at last to Dr. Mallory Finch. The lamplight caught the sheen of sweat on her brow, the way her gloved hands twisted in her lap. 'Dr.

Mallory Finch,' Hugo said, his voice low but implacable, 'you are the only one whose whereabouts are not fully accounted for at the crucial time. You had access to Sylvia Trent's room, and you alone grasped how the evening's slow shadows shaped their fears would matter. ' Dr. Finch's face paled, her composure crumbling under the scrutiny. Her lips parted, but no words came. Hugo pressed on, each word a hammer blow. 'The clock was last wound at twenty minutes past nine. But that is not when Sylvia Trent died. The clock was tampered with-deliberately stopped to create a false alibi. Only someone with precise knowledge, someone accustomed to delicate mechanisms, could have left the scratch on the winding mechanism. That scratch, Dr. Finch, is the mark of a surgeon's hand. ' The silence in the Hotel parlor was absolute. Dr.

Finch's hands trembled, her eyes wide and unblinking. 'You don't understand,' she whispered, her voice raw. 'You can't know what it's like to see everything you've worked for threatened by a single word. Sylvia Trent held my fate in her hands. She threatened to expose me to the health board. I was afraid-afraid of losing everything. ' Hugo's tone softened, but he did not relent. 'You confronted her that night. The witnesses heard the argument. In a moment of fear, you strangled her. Then, knowing the clocks would be checked, you set her clock to twenty minutes past nine, creating a false alibi and buying yourself time. The evidence is unassailable. You killed Sylvia Trent. ' Dr. Finch's voice broke, her confession spilling out in a rush. 'I killed her. I was desperate. I only wanted to frighten her, to make her listen. But she would not yield.

I lost control. ' Her hands fell limp in her lap, the weight of guilt settling on her shoulders. 'I feared exposure more than anything. That fear drove me. ' The words hung in the air, a mixture of regret and defiance. A murmur rippled through the room. Eleanor Voss pressed a hand to her mouth, tears glinting in her eyes. Captain Ivor Hale stared at the floor, his fists unclenching at last. Beatrice Quill, voice unsteady, asked, 'And now? What happens to her? ' The question was not only for Hugo, but for the world beyond the Hotel parlor, for the order that had been shattered. Hugo's reply was quiet, almost gentle. 'Dr. Finch will be taken into custody. The authorities have been summoned. ' He glanced at the door, where the distant echo of footsteps in the corridor signaled the arrival of order from outside. 'Justice will follow, though it can never restore what is lost. ' Dr.

Finch rose, her movements slow and deliberate. She looked at each of them in turn-Eleanor, Beatrice, Hale, Hugo-her expression a mix of sorrow and something like relief. 'I do not ask forgiveness,' she said. 'But I hope you understand. I was not only afraid for myself. I believed I was protecting the clinic, my staff, everyone who depended on me. In the end, fear made me a murderer. ' The constable's shadow fell across the threshold, and Dr. Finch allowed herself to be led away without protest. The room seemed to expand in her absence, the tension ebbing, replaced by a hollow quiet. Eleanor Voss wept openly now, her grief for Sylvia Trent mingled with relief for herself. Captain Ivor Hale crossed to the window, staring out into the midsummer darkness, his silhouette rigid but no longer defensive. Beatrice Quill retrieved her notebook, her hands steadying as she scribbled a final note.

Hugo lingered by the clock, his reflection warped in the brass. The machinery of justice had turned, but the cost was written in every face around him. The truth had been unearthed, but it brought no comfort-only the certainty that fear, once loosed, could not be so easily tamed. She closed her eyes, listening to the whisper of wind against the glass and the slow, measured footsteps of Dr. Finch as she was led away. In the silence that followed, the Hotel parlor became a place of reckoning, suspended between the old order and whatever must come next.
