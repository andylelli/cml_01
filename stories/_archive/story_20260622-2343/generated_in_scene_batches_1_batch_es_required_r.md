" Dr. Langley's composure held, but a faint smile of gratitude flickered across her lips. Reginald Fairfax, ever the outsider, met Pembroke's gaze with a hint of defiance. "Your gambling meeting at the village inn is corroborated by both the publican and a neighbor. You could not have been at the manor when the crime occurred. You are innocent in this matter. " Reginald's relief was tempered by embarrassment, but he managed a wry grin. Pembroke faced Lady Eleanor Blackwood, his voice gentle but resolute. "Lady Eleanor, the evidence is overwhelming.

Your presence near the garden window at the exact moment of the wind gust, the ledger documenting your financial control, and the testimony of staff all point to your involvement. The clock's manipulation was possible only by someone with your access and opportunity. " Lady Eleanor's composure crumbled. Tears welled in her eyes as she confessed, her voice barely audible. "I did it for the estate, for my family's standing. He was suffering, and I could not bear to watch him fade. I thought I could spare him-and us-the shame. " Pembroke listened in silence, the gravity of his admission settling over the room. The moral ambiguity of her actions was clear; she had acted out of desperation, not malice. The inspector's duty was to uphold the law, but he could not help but feel a pang of sympathy for the woman before him.

As Lady Eleanor was led away, the remaining members of the household gathered in subdued conversation. The manor, once a bastion of order, had been forever altered by the events of the past days. Pembroke lingered by the window, reflecting on the cost of justice and the fragile nature of truth. The sun dipped lower in the sky, casting long shadows across The Drawing Room. The investigation was over, but the consequences would echo through Blackwood Manor for years to come.
