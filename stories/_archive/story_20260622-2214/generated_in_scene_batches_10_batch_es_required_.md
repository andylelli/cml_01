Langley, your medical records and the testimony of the housekeeper confirm you were in the infirmary at the critical time. The bell was rung for you at the hour, and you did not leave until nearly half past eleven. Therefore, your alibi holds, and you could not have participated in the events in the study. You are cleared. " A subtle tension eased from Dr. Langley's posture, though her gaze lingered on Lady Eleanor, sympathy and regret mingling in her eyes. Reginald Fairfax, standing near the window, met Pembroke's gaze with a mixture of defiance and resignation. "Mr. Fairfax, your alibi is confirmed by both the vicar and a woman who saw you on the lane at the time of the murder. The gambling debt you settled explains your earlier anxiety, but the witness statements place you away from Blackwood Manor Estate during the crucial minutes.

Therefore, you are innocent of this crime. " Reginald's lips parted as if to offer a sardonic retort, but he merely nodded, the relief in his posture undercut by a lingering sense of exclusion. A moment of silence followed as the inspector's attention returned to Lady Eleanor Blackwood. Her hands, usually so steady, trembled in her lap; the pallor of her face was thrown into sharp relief by the afternoon light. Pembroke's tone softened, but his words were unyielding. "Lady Eleanor, every piece of evidence-physical, testimonial, and circumstantial-points to you. A servant recalls seeing you near the garden window moments before the wind gust. The financial ledgers show a pattern of withdrawals and corrections, revealing both motive and planning. Only you had the opportunity and reason to manipulate the clock and the scene. " Lady Eleanor's composure faltered, her voice barely audible.

"I did what I thought was necessary," she whispered, her gaze fixed on the unmoving clock. "He was suffering, Inspector. His illness was not known to you, but it was real. I could not bear to watch him fade, nor to see the estate destroyed by scandal and decline. I used the wind and the clock to create a false time, to spare us all a longer agony. " The inspector's reply was gentle, but the implication was inexorable. "You opened the window to a gap of six inches at the precise moment the wind arrived, knowing it would strike the exposed pendulum arm of the clock. The mechanism reversed, winding the clock backward by exactly forty minutes before it stopped at ten minutes past eleven. This misled the household and investigators, allowing you to fabricate an alibi.

The marks on the clock's gears, the disturbed petals, and the testimony all confirm your actions. " A new detail emerged as Pembroke examined the window latch after the test: a faint trace of Lady Eleanor's perfume clung to the brass, mingling with the summer air-a subtle but undeniable signature of her presence at the scene. The evidence, now complete, left no room for doubt. Charles Wentworth's voice broke the silence, thick with emotion. "You did not have to do this, Eleanor. There were other ways-ways that did not end in this. " His words, though gentle, carried the ache of betrayal and loss. Dr. Langley pressed a hand to her mouth, her eyes shining with unshed tears. Reginald Fairfax stood apart, his expression unreadable, but the set of his jaw betrayed a grudging respect for Lady Eleanor's resolve. Pembroke stepped forward, his posture weary but resolute.

"Lady Eleanor Blackwood, you are named as the one responsible for the death and for the deliberate manipulation of the scene. The law will take its course. " Lady Eleanor rose, dignity battered but not wholly lost, and allowed herself to be escorted from the room. His final glance at the clock was one of resignation, not defiance. As The Drawing Room's door closed behind him, the atmosphere shifted-relief, sorrow, and an uneasy sense of justice mingling in the charged air. The survivors were left to reckon with the cost of truth: Charles, head bowed in grief; Dr. Langley, silent and pale; Reginald, arms folded, gaze turned to the window and the brightening summer sky beyond. Inspector Harold Pembroke lingered by the mantel, his hand once more resting on the clock face frozen at ten minutes past eleven.

He spoke quietly, for the benefit of the room and himself: "Justice is rarely clean, and never without cost. We have uncovered the truth, but it will echo through Blackwood Manor Estate for years to come. " The words hung in the air, a benediction and a warning, as the afternoon light faded and the fragile order of the manor was restored-if only in appearance. In the aftermath, The Drawing Room remained silent, the only sound the faint ticking of a distant clock elsewhere in the house. The case was closed, but the wounds it had opened would not heal swiftly. For each, the events of this summer day would linger-a reminder that even the most careful manipulation of time cannot forestall the reckoning of truth.
