Edith stood and crossed to the mantel, refusing to sit with her back to the door. She rested her hand on the brass candlestick there, fingers tapping a silent rhythm. "Too much power in too few hands is rarely wise."

Agatha's answer was cool. "Order demands responsibility. I bear it alone, as I have always done."

Millicent gathered her scores, her posture as composed as her voice. "And if the order is an illusion, what then?"

Violet's laugh was soft, almost pitying. "Then the house is built on sand."

Edith closed the ledger and set it aside. "We have a correction in your hand, exclusive access, and a timeline that cannot be reconciled. The truth must be somewhere beneath these layers."

For a moment, the only sound was the faint ticking of the clock in the corridor, its rhythm steady, indifferent to the secrets it measured. The library, lined with silent witnesses, seemed to lean in, waiting for the next piece to fall into place.

## Chapter 8: The Final Trap

"Isn't that just delightful?" Violet Radcliffe said, her voice echoing in the main hall as the late evening darkened the stained-glass windows. She perched on the edge of a velvet chair, one hand tracing the filigreed armrest as she surveyed the gathering. Edith Penhale stood at the long table, the stopped pocket watch and the manor hall clock's winding log placed side by side for all to see.

Agatha Innes, her cane balanced carefully against the oak bench, regarded the assembled evidence with a cold composure. Millicent Ashcombe stood near the hearth, her music folio clutched to her chest, while Violet watched from her seat, sardonic amusement flickering in her eyes.

Edith's voice was calm. "We will compare the times directly-quarter past seven in the evening on the victim's pocket watch, half past seven in the evening on the manor hall clock. A difference of fifteen minutes. Now, let us see what the evidence tells us."

Agatha's answer was flat. "The official time remains unchanged."

Millicent's reply was sharp. "Official times can be set by anyone with the right key."

Violet leaned forward, her smile thin. "I suppose the clocks, like the ledgers, are only as honest as the people winding them."

Edith gestured to the investigator present. "Please, confirm the times as you see them now. The pocket watch-quarter past seven in the evening. The manor hall clock-half past seven in the evening."

The detective, notebook in hand, read the dials aloud for the room. The others watched in silence, tension crackling like static in the air. Millicent's knuckles whitened on her folio, while Agatha's cane tapped softly against the floor.

Violet broke the silence, her retort a drawn-out sigh. "So, the clock and the watch are out of step. If only all our troubles were so easily measured."

Edith's dry wit surfaced. "Fifteen minutes, in this house, can be the difference between guilt and innocence."

Leonard's unmeant joke, a memory from an old rehearsal, surfaced: "If we keep arguing about time, we'll never start the music." The echo of his voice hung in the stillness.

Agatha's eyes narrowed. "This comparison proves nothing. Clocks drift. Watches stop."

Edith placed the choir school ledger on the table, pages open to the correction in Agatha's hand. "The ledger shows your correction, Rector. The clock's advance matches the discrepancy. The only person with both motive and means is you."

Violet's voice was cool. "If the time was set by the Rector, all the alibis unravel. How convenient for some."

Millicent's response was quiet but fierce. "And how damning for others."

The detective compared the entries, then looked up. "The winding log matches the pocket watch, not the hall clock. The only way for the records to agree with the official story is if the clock was set forward after the last winding."

Edith stepped back, her eyes sweeping the room. "We have the pocket watch, the hall clock, the ledger-each tells a story. But only one person controlled the narrative from the start."

Violet's sardonic laughter rippled. "Isn't it always the ones with the keys?"

A hush settled, broken only by the ticking of the hall clock-a sound now exposed as the measure of a lie. Agatha's hand trembled just once as she reached for her cane, but her voice remained steady.

Edith closed her notebook, glancing once more at the two clocks-one halted, one ticking-and at each tense face around the table. The detective finished noting the times, closing the winding log beside the stopped pocket watch-side by side, evidence of the fifteen-minute rift for all to see.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"Not beyond reason," Edith Penhale said, her voice ringing clear in the night-quiet drawing room as the others listened-Millicent Ashcombe, Violet Radcliffe, and the memory of Leonard Nettleship hovering in the hush between them. Violet's hand rested on the ledger, fingers tracing the familiar loops of her own handwriting, while Millicent's gaze lingered on the roster that had once seemed her undoing.

"You were in the practice room from seven o'clock to a quarter to eight," Edith began, nodding once to Millicent, whose relief was silent but profound. "Leonard Nettleship himself recorded your presence. There is no gap in your alibi."

Millicent's answer was flat. "Thank you." She picked at a loose thread in her shawl, eyes downcast as the weight lifted from her shoulders.

Violet pressed her lips together before speaking, her usual wry smile absent. "My handwriting matches the ledger. I was in the secretary's office the entire time." She closed the ledger, the snap of the clasp small but final.

Edith moved to the table where the choir school ledger lay open, the correction in Agatha Innes's immaculate hand plain for all to see. "The correction was made after the clock was advanced. The pocket watch-stopped at quarter past seven in the evening-matches the original winding entry, not the official time of half past seven in the evening."

Leonard's unmeant joke echoed in the hush: "Let the music speak for itself." Tonight, the music was the ticking of clocks and the turning of pages, and it left no room for doubt.

Violet shot back, her words sharp enough to slice the silence. "A pity the Rector's word was worth more than the truth."

Millicent's short reply was soft. "It was, until now."

Edith set her pen aside and looked each woman in the eye. "Agatha Innes killed Leonard Nettleship." The words, once spoken, settled over the room like a final chord.

No one moved for a heartbeat. Then Millicent exhaled, Violet looked away, and the ledger was closed, the evidence complete. The truth, so long obscured by manipulation and authority, stood revealed-not just in clocks and ledgers, but in the relief and sorrow etched on every face.

Outside, the manor's clocks continued their relentless measure, but within the drawing room, time itself seemed, at last, to be set right.

## Chapter 10: The Rector's Unimpeachable Word

The outcome was settled: Agatha Innes sat in her study, her hands folded on the desk, the heavy candlestick-once a symbol of order-now locked away as evidence. The bloodied brass, its weight undeniable, had left a visible wound on Leonard Nettleship's head, the mark of violence at odds with the quiet dignity of the room. Edith Penhale stood by the fireplace, running her finger along the cool marble before snapping her notebook shut.

Agatha pressed her lips together, her shoulders drawn tight. "It is finished, then."

"Yes." Edith's answer was flat. She set the keys to the manor buildings on the writing desk, the cold metal catching the lamplight.

Violet Radcliffe entered, her movement as careful as her words. She placed a stack of letters on the corner table-correspondence newly organized, her own debts now managed with a diligence the old Violet had never shown. She touched the ledger, then drew her hand away, as if the past might stain her fingers.

Millicent Ashcombe lingered in the doorway, a music folio clutched to her chest. Millicent arranged the music sheets for the next morning's lesson, her instructions crisp as the children gathered around, the rivals from last term glancing her way with nods of acknowledgment. She traced the edge of the piano, her touch gentle.

Leonard Nettleship's memory lingered-not in the violence of his death, but in the time he had spent coaxing music from a reluctant student, his laughter warm, his patience endless. A photograph of him as a boy, standing beside a battered piano, rested atop the mantel. The photograph stayed where it was, untouched.

Agatha's hands shook as she gathered the last of her correspondence. "There will be consequences."

Violet's reply was swift, sardonic. "There always are."

Millicent's retort was quiet: "We'll manage them."

Edith refused to sit with her back to the door, her gaze on the window where the moonlight painted a silver path across the rug. She did not speak of victory, only of order restored. The candlestick, blood now dried along its edge, sat where Edith had set it down for the constable, its dull gleam drawing every eye in the room to what could never be restored.

A child's laughter drifted from the corridor, the choir school resuming its ordinary routine. The rector's study, stripped of its secrets, was just a room again.

The music, in time, would return. The clocks would be wound. And the house would go on, measured now not by authority, but by the quiet, uncompromising tick of the truth.
