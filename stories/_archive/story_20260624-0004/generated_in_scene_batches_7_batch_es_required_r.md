Several staff members saw you in the library, and your absence was noted only for a brief moment to retrieve a telegram, which the staff also witnessed. Because your movements are corroborated throughout the crucial window, your alibi holds and you are cleared of suspicion. " The words, measured and formal, drew a visible loosening of Lady Beatrice's shoulders, though her composure did not break.

A faint exhale sounded from Eleanor Fisk, who stood near the window, her hands clasped tightly. Marlowe's gaze turned to her. "Eleanor Fisk, the laundry room logs and the signatures of the maids confirm your presence from half past eight until ten. The staff's accounts and the ledger are consistent-therefore, your alibi is confirmed and you are ruled out as a suspect. " Eleanor's eyes glistened, her relief palpable, but she did not look away from Marlowe, as if needing the reassurance to be spoken aloud. James Harrow, standing near the bookcase with his back straight as a parade ground officer, met Marlowe's eyes. She nodded. "James Harrow, your presence in the study is confirmed by multiple witnesses and the nurse's records. The logs show you were attended for your injury through the critical period, which proves your innocence.

Your alibi holds. " A subtle shift in his stance-shoulders relaxing, jaw unclenching-was the only sign of the tension leaving him. The lamplight caught the brass of the grandfather clock, drawing every eye. Marlowe allowed the silence to stretch before turning to the final matter. "As for myself, Inspector Helena Marlowe, my presence was accounted for in the company of the household and staff at all relevant intervals. The investigation's records and the corroboration of all present confirm that I could not have committed the crime. My alibi is established by the evidence. " A faint breeze rattled the window latch, and the room seemed to contract around Thomas Benton, who stood apart from the others, his hands trembling as he gripped the back of a chair. Marlowe's voice, when it came, was precise and unyielding. "Mr.

Benton, your claim to have been inside the stalls just before nine and half past nine was plausible only so long as the household believed the grandfather clock's chimes reflected the true passage of time. But the evidence tells a different story. " She stepped to the grandfather clock, her gloved fingers tracing the edge of the winding keyhole. "The mechanism shows fresh marks, and the servants recall the chimes were late that evening. The pendulum, measuring exactly thirty-four inches, should have kept perfect time. Yet the clock was found wound back by precisely forty minutes after the murder. Only someone with intimate knowledge of the clock's workings could have executed such a deception.

The victim's pocket watch, stopped at ten minutes past eleven, reflects the true moment of death-a time that falls after your supposed absence from the manor's main wing. " Marlowe's gaze was unwavering. "The murder occurred late in the evening, from the first chime of quarter to the half-hour mark nine, but the timeline was shifted by your actions. You exploited the trust placed in the manor's clocks, rewinding the mechanism to create a false sequence of events. This manipulation allowed you to claim an alibi that, under scrutiny, cannot hold. The logs from the stables contain a gap at the crucial moment, and no one can confirm your whereabouts. The servants saw no one else near the clock, and the physical evidence-the fresh scratches, the delayed chimes, the precise adjustment-points to you alone. " A shudder passed through Benton, his composure unraveling.

He looked at each face in turn-Lady Beatrice's icy resolve, Eleanor's trembling relief, James Harrow's grim acceptance-before his voice emerged, rough with defeat. "I killed him. I strangled him in the drawing room, after the household had settled for the evening. I did it to maintain control and prevent scandal. There were things coming to light that would have destroyed everything this family built. I thought... if I could spare them that, the cost would be mine to bear. " His confession was neither loud nor proud, but it carried the weight of resignation. No one moved at first. The only sound was the steady tick of the clock, indifferent to the drama it had helped conceal. Marlowe's tone softened, but she did not look away. "You tampered with time, Mr. Benton, but the truth could not be hidden.

The evidence, the altered clock, the stopped watch, and the gap in your alibi-each piece led here. You will be taken into custody. The law will weigh your actions, and the household must reckon with what has been lost. " A sob escaped Eleanor, quickly stifled by her hand. Lady Beatrice's composure fractured for a heartbeat, her eyes shining with unshed tears. James Harrow stared at the floor, his fists clenched at his sides. The cost of control, the price of silence, was written on every face. Relief mingled with grief, the knowledge that innocence had been preserved at the expense of trust. As Benton was escorted from the room, the atmosphere shifted-no longer brittle with suspicion, but heavy with the aftermath of truth. The household remained, each member changed by what had passed. Marlowe lingered by the grandfather clock, her reflection wavering in the glass.

Justice had been served, but the questions of mercy and motive would echo through the manor long after the case was closed. The spring night pressed close, silent and unyielding, as the house braced for the uncertain days to come.
