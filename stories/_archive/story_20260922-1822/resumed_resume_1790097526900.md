Edith rose, smoothing the skirt of her suit with a practiced hand. She moved to the window, resting her fingertips on the cool pane as she looked out into the night. Behind her, the others watched in silence, the weight of accusation pressing down like an extra pane of glass.

Edith's gaze fell upon the battered violin case propped in the corner, its leather scuffed and handle worn. Leonard's father had always cradled his instrument with care, as if it were something fragile and irreplaceable. The way Leonard had tried to protect the school, even as it cost him his peace.

Violet set her glass aside and stood, drawing her shawl tight. "So that's it, then. We're to accept the rector's unimpeachable word and let the clocks keep their secrets. How terribly modern."

Millicent's eyes narrowed. "Not all secrets stay hidden, Violet."

Edith turned back to the room, her voice calm but edged with steel. "The matter is not settled. Not yet. The evidence still points elsewhere, and I intend to see it through."

The grandfather clock chimed, its notes hollow in the heavy air. Agatha looked up, her gaze flinty. "By all means, Miss Penhale. Pursue your inquiry. But mind the time-it slips away from us all."

Millicent picked up a fallen sheet of music, smoothing it against her skirt. "Some of us know exactly where we were, and when. Others seem to prefer the ambiguity."

As the gathering broke up, Edith lingered by the mantel, her fingers trailing over the cold marble. She looked at the faces around her, seeing relief, defiance, and-most of all-a stubborn refusal to let go of old certainties.

The false solution hung in the air, thick as the scent of brandy and wax. But beneath it, Edith sensed the undertow of a deeper truth, waiting for its moment to break the surface.

## Chapter 7: Secrets Beneath Secrets

"Every key in the estate," Edith Penhale remarked, watching as Agatha Innes's hand hovered over the ring of brass and iron keys, her grip tightening with the weight of their authority. The night pressed in beyond the library windows, the glass fogged with condensation, but within, the world was all lamplight and the scent of old leather and polish.

Agatha set the keys down on the desk with a deliberate clatter. "I am responsible for the security of this house," she said, her voice clipped. "The clocks, the records, the archives-none are accessed without my approval."

Edith's fingers brushed the choir school ledger, her gaze lingering on a correction in Agatha's unmistakable hand beside the clock winding entry. She turned the page, the vellum smooth beneath her fingertips, her pen poised to note even the smallest irregularity. "Exclusive access is a curious thing, Rector. It ensures order-or secrecy."

Millicent Ashcombe entered quietly, her steps muffled by the heavy rug. She moved to the bookshelf, pulling out a volume with practiced ease, her lips curving in a polite, razor-edged smile. "Some people collect secrets the way others collect stamps. Or perhaps, keys."

Agatha's answer was four words: "Order is not secrecy, Miss Ashcombe."

Leonard's memory was palpable in this room-the hymnals and scores he had favored still arranged in neat stacks, a melody from the previous week's rehearsal lingering in Edith's mind as she paused by the side table. Edith paused to run her hand along the spine of a hymnal, the leather soft with age, before returning to the ledger.

Millicent's eyes flicked to the keys, then to Agatha. "It must be comforting, having such absolute control. Most of us must content ourselves with polite requests and hope for tolerance."

Agatha's jaw clenched. "Responsibility is often mistaken for privilege."

Edith's dry wit surfaced. "Not beyond reason, Rector. But one wonders if the same hand that keeps the clocks in order might also find time to adjust them-if only for the sake of order, of course."

Millicent's reply was a speech, polished and sharp. "Order is a fine thing, Miss Penhale, but history is littered with the remains of those who mistook control for justice. It takes only a moment to rewrite a ledger, to advance a clock, to leave the rest of us scrambling to catch up. The question is not who possesses the keys, but who decides who may pass through each door. That is the greater power, and the greater risk."

Edith's retort was brief: "Not always your door, Millicent."

Millicent's smile flickered, but she did not look away. She placed the book back on the shelf, her hand lingering on the wood a moment longer than necessary.

Agatha shifted in her chair, her right hand trembling as she adjusted her shawl-a tremor that Edith noted but did not remark upon. "The correction in the ledger was necessary. The time had been recorded incorrectly by a distracted chorister."

Edith tapped the page, the sound echoing in the library's hush. "A correction made in your own hand-after the death, not before. The sequence is important."

Leonard's absence was felt in the way the room seemed too large, the shadows too deep. The music that had once filled this space was replaced by the steady tick of a clock on the mantel-one that, Edith observed, matched the hall clock's time to the minute.

Millicent drew a finger along the spine of another book, her expression thoughtful. "The hand that holds the keys also holds the power to lock the truth away. Or to let it out, when it suits."

Agatha's gaze met Edith's, the challenge plain. "You are free to examine any record you wish, Miss Penhale. I have nothing to conceal."

Edith closed the ledger, placing her palm flat on its cover. "The question is not what you conceal, Rector, but what you control. The clocks, the records-the very framework of the investigation. No one else had such reach."

Millicent's jaw set, her eyes on the polished surface of the desk. "How utterly... enlightening."

Agatha turned her attention to a sheaf of correspondence, her left hand steady as she sorted through the pages. The keys remained on the desk, a silent testament to her authority.

Leonard's memory lingered here as well, in the careful arrangement of music and the scent of old scores. Edith lingered near the side table, her fingers tracing the faded titles of music scores-old hymns and Leonard's favorite pieces-finding solace in their familiar presence.

The night deepened, the library's lamplight making islands of warmth in the sea of dark. Edith stood, refusing the chair that would have placed her back to the door, and regarded the ledger, the keys, and the woman who held them all.

Edith glanced at the locked cabinet, its heavy iron key lying on the desk-Rosemere Manor's secrets, for now, kept behind its doors, but not beyond her reach. But the hand that held the keys was not infallible, and Edith Penhale was determined to prove it.

## Chapter 8: The Final Trap

"Let us settle this now," Edith Penhale said, her voice carrying clearly through the main hall, where the marble floor glowed in pools of lamp and moonlight. The hour was late, and the four remaining suspects-Agatha Innes, Millicent Ashcombe, Violet Radcliffe, and Edith herself-stood in a semi-circle before the grand table. On its polished surface, two timepieces lay side by side: the manor hall clock, wheeled in for the occasion, and Leonard Nettleship's battered pocket watch, its chain coiled like a question mark.

Agatha's lips were pressed thin, keys clinking faintly as she set down her ledger. "This is most irregular," she announced, cane tapping the tiles. "The authorities accepted my testimony-half past seven in the evening."

Edith inclined her head. "Not beyond reason, Rector. But a demonstration is in order. We have the stopped time on the victim's pocket watch-quarter past seven in the evening-and the hall clock, now showing half past seven in the evening. I propose we compare them in the presence of all."

Violet Radcliffe lounged against the arm of a chair, arms folded, her eyes reflecting candlelight. "Isn't that just delightful? Nothing like a public reckoning to remind us which side of the hour we're on. Shall we have cake after?"

Millicent Ashcombe's reply was flat, almost gentle: "We have waited long enough."

Edith gestured to the constable, who lifted the glass dome from the hall clock and set it on the table with a muffled ring. She reached for the pocket watch, holding it up for all to see. "Quarter past seven in the evening. That is the time Leonard's watch stopped." She set it beside the clock, which read half past seven in the evening. "A difference of fifteen minutes. And yet, the rector's sworn statement fixed the time of death at half past seven in the evening."

Agatha's answer was four words: "The record is official, Miss Penhale."

Violet's speech was a languid drawl, each word deliberate. "Official. Such a useful word, isn't it? I suppose if I'd been born official, my debts would have vanished by decree. But here we are-one clock, one watch, and the only witness who cannot speak for himself. I do wonder, Rector, if the clocks in your care are as honest as you claim, or just as obedient."

Millicent's retort was brief: "How utterly... enlightening. Thank you, Violet."

Edith flicked open the choir school ledger, her finger tracing the correction in Agatha's hand. "The correction to the clock's winding time was made after Leonard's death," she said, her tone sharp. "You had unique access-keys, records, authority. No one else could have advanced the clock, nor inserted a correction to fit the narrative."

Agatha's hand trembled as she leaned on her cane, but her face remained composed. "I did only what was necessary. The school-"

"The school," Edith interrupted, "is not a justification for murder."

A silence fell. The constable, glancing at the assembled group, took the pocket watch and held it to the hall clock. "The difference is plain," he said, voice firm. "Quarter past seven in the evening to half past seven in the evening-fifteen minutes. The logbook, the winding entry, the correction-all in the rector's hand."

Millicent stepped forward, her voice steady but her eyes shining. "Leonard trusted the truth to stand on its own. He would never have allowed the clock to overrule the evidence of his own watch."

Violet's gaze flickered between Agatha and the clocks. "Some of us lose time; others steal it. The difference is only a matter of who holds the keys."

Edith closed the ledger with a snap, the sound echoing in the vast hall. "We have seen the evidence. The mechanism was visible to only one. The means, the opportunity, the motive-they all point to a single hand."

Agatha's cane slipped, and for a moment she wavered, but she straightened, jaw set.

The demonstration complete, Edith turned to the others. "The matter is settled. The fifteen minutes were stolen, not lost-and the one who advanced the clock is the only one who could have done so."

The main hall, so often filled with music and laughter, was silent as the comparison ended. The truth, at last, ticked out by two clocks and witnessed by all.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"Millicent Ashcombe was in the practice room from seven o'clock to a quarter to eight," Edith Penhale stated, her voice ringing clear in the drawing room, the heavy curtains drawn tight against the night. "That alibi stands, witnessed by Leonard Nettleship himself. The roster was forged after the fact, but not by Millicent-her innocence is confirmed by both time and testimony."

Millicent sat on the edge of an ottoman, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, knuckles white as she listened. The lamp beside her cast a circle of warm light, illuminating the music folio she gripped, as if it were a lifeline.

"Violet Radcliffe," Edith continued, turning to the young heiress, "was in the secretary's office from five minutes past seven to twenty minutes to eight. The handwriting in the ledgers matches her own, and the office staff confirm her presence throughout. The inconsistency in the ledger pages was the result of fatigue, not deception."

Violet let out a breath, her posture loosening for the first time in hours. She reached into her reticule and withdrew a small, battered notebook, clutching it in both hands. Her eyes met Edith's, gratitude flickering there, though her smile was edged with exhaustion.

Leonard's absence was keenly felt, his memory present in the battered music stand that still bore the marks of his last rehearsal. Millicent glanced at the battered music stand, as if expecting Leonard's voice-earnest and resonant-to sound again, but only silence remained.

Edith moved to the mantel, lifting the choir school ledger. "The correction to the clock winding time was made by Agatha Innes. The handwriting is hers, and the keys to every building-the clocks, the records, the logbook-were in her exclusive possession."

Millicent's voice, quiet but strong, broke the silence: "So it was the rector, after all."

Edith nodded, her expression resolute. "The stopped time on the victim's pocket watch is quarter past seven in the evening. The manor hall clock shows half past seven in the evening-fifteen minutes ahead. The demonstration tonight proved that only someone with authority over the clocks and records could have orchestrated the deception."

Violet's eyes glistened as she looked at Millicent. "We spent so long doubting one another. All the while, it was the person we trusted most."

Millicent's retort was soft, almost a whisper: "How utterly... enlightening."

Edith set the ledger down with finality. "Edith's words sharpened, her gaze fixed on Agatha: "Agatha Innes killed Leonard Nettleship." The air shifted as Violet drew in a trembling breath. Edith pressed on, the tension mounting. "She advanced the clock, altered the records, and fixed the time of death to conceal her guilt. All to avoid scandal and to protect the school." Millicent's folio slipped from her hands, landing with a quiet thud. Agatha's name hung in the room, heavy and unchallenged, the revelation settling like thunder."

The words seemed to take on a weight of their own, settling over the room. Violet pressed a hand to her mouth, her other still clutching the notebook, and Millicent's grip on her folio finally loosened.

Leonard's memory returned, not as a victim, but as a man who once laughed with his choir, who believed in the power of truth to set things right. The echo of his voice-insisting the music speak-seemed to fill the silence.

Outside, the clock in the main hall chimed, marking the hour. Within the drawing room, innocence had been cleared, and for the first time since the murder, the burden of suspicion lifted from the living.

## Chapter 10: The Rector's Unimpeachable Word

"Visible evidence, Miss Penhale," the coroner said, gesturing to the heavy candlestick resting on the linen-draped table in the rector's study. Blood and bruising marked the victim's head, unmistakable even in the soft glow of the desk lamp. The candlestick's ornate base was stained and dented, flecks of blood dark against the brass-a grim testament to the violence that ended Leonard Nettleship's life.

Edith Penhale nodded, her expression composed. She placed her hand on the back of the wooden chair where she sat, refusing-still-to turn her back to the study's door. "The matter is settled," she said quietly, her voice carrying just enough for Agatha Innes, seated across the desk, to hear. "The official time of death was fixed at half past seven in the evening, but the physical evidence-the pocket watch, the wound, the bloodied weapon-told a different story."

Agatha sat upright, her hands folded tightly atop her lap, cane balanced against her knee. Her face was drawn, but her bearing did not waver. "The school would not have survived a scandal," she said, voice low. "I did what I believed was necessary."

Millicent Ashcombe, waiting in the corridor, listened as the truth was recounted. Her role, once fraught with suspicion, was written plainly on her face-a survivor, but changed. She clutched a music folio to her chest, the edges now smooth from endless handling. She had begun accepting the post of senior tutor, her presence in the choir room a reassurance to the children.

Violet Radcliffe, having received word that her debts would be managed in trust by the estate, now walked the halls with a lightness that had not been seen in months. She no longer lingered by the office door at night, instead spending her evenings in the music library, annotating scores with a new, steady hand.

Edith adjusted the reading lamp, the brass arm cool beneath her fingers. "The wound matches the candlestick. The logbook entry, the correction, all point to a deliberate manipulation of time and record. But the motive was not malice, but fear-fear of disgrace, and the loss of a legacy."

The rector's answer was four words: "That remains to be seen."

Violet's voice drifted in from the hall, a short retort to a question Millicent had posed about the future of the choir. "Isn't that just delightful?"

Leonard's memory surfaced, not tied to clocks or ledgers, but to an evening long ago when he had played a simple tune on the harpsichord for the children-an old folk melody his father used to play, fingers stumbling at first, then finding the rhythm. The laughter that filled the room then had nothing to do with time, or with death.

Agatha's cane slipped as she rose, her right hand shaking-a minor tremor that Edith, out of habit, noted but did not mention. The rector's authority, once unimpeachable, showed in the way Agatha's shoulders slumped as she glanced at the candlestick-her gaze shadowed by the knowledge of what had been lost to maintain it.

Edith gathered her notes, stacking them neatly beside the candlestick. She would remain at Rosemere for a time, seeing to the estate's transition and ensuring the music school's continuity. Her reputation, if not untouched, was burnished by the quiet thoroughness of her inquiry-a change that would follow her into her next commission.

Millicent, now senior tutor, hung a new rehearsal schedule on the notice board in the hallway, her handwriting careful and clear. Violet, her debts managed, placed fresh flowers in the choir room each Friday, a ritual of remembrance and renewal.

The study door closed behind Edith. Agatha inhaled sharply, her knuckles white on the edge of the desk; a distant shout from the grounds startled her, then another echoing in reply. Footsteps hurried past the study, the sound urgent and unsettled. Outside, the night air was cool and filled with the scent of newly cut grass.

In the great hall, the clocks resumed their ordinary duties, marking the hours with steady, measured chimes. The choir school reopened for rehearsals the following afternoon. The children's voices, imperfect but earnest, soared once more through the corridors-music speaking for itself, at last.
