## Chapter 8: Compass and Ledger

"Nora Quayle, would you mind?" Bertram Norbury's voice carried across the lamplight-soaked office, late evening drawing the world quiet around them. Nora stood by the desk, her hands resting on the battered compass as Bertram laid a blank sheet of paper beside it.

"I'd rather rebuild a gearbox," she muttered, but complied, picking up the instrument.

Bertram nodded. "Now, hold the compass as the judge did-tilted, just so." He watched as she angled it, the needle swinging wide, settling fifteen degrees off true north.

Nora's lips pressed together, the faintest flicker of irritation crossing her face. "There. Not how I'd trust a bearing."

Bertram made a note. "Now, level it out. Let's see the reading again."

She did, and the needle snapped back to true. "Difference is clear. Fifteen degrees, as you said."

Bertram's gaze was steady. "When the judge took his reading at nine o'clock, he had the casing tilted. Every witness who followed his testimony was misled by that error in the bearing."

Nora set the compass down, her fingers drumming on the desktop. "And the ledger?"

Bertram slid the book forward. "Entries between eight forty-five and a quarter to ten. Different inks, varying pressure. You wrote some after the fact, didn't you?"

Nora didn't answer, and the silence thickened.

Harriet Kestrel entered quietly, her arms full of fresh towels. "If only they knew the trouble a misplaced decimal can cause," she said, placing the towels in a neat stack. "I was cleaning from ten minutes to nine to a quarter past nine. Mrs. Wainwright and the laundry girls saw me the whole time."

Bertram nodded. "That covers your alibi, Harriet. Staff confirm your schedule and location."

Harriet's eyes shone with a bright, polite savagery. "I'm not the one who knows how to fudge an entry, Mr Norbury."

Bertram, short retort: "Nor am I, Harriet."

Montague Gaunt's battered stopwatch, resting on the desk, ticked steadily. If only the dead could offer an alibi, Bertram thought. But Montague's time was up-his battered stopwatch silenced, the office quiet.

Nora's gaze flicked to the clock on the wall. "Quarter to ten, not nine o'clock. That's your window."

Bertram nodded. "Confirmed by the compass test. The judge's reading was off by fifteen degrees-placing the suspect on the beach, when in fact you were near the office."

Nora's jaw tightened. "And the ledger?"

Bertram tapped the entries. "Some fresh, some stale." He paused, giving Nora time to react. "You altered the book to fabricate your presence, relying on the trust placed in the judge's word and your own reputation."

The clock ticked, marking the passage of time neither could reclaim.

A moment for itself: Bertram straightened the compass, wiped a smudge from the glass, and set it precisely at the center of the blotter, his military neatness asserting itself even here.

Nora's voice was low. "So you have your trick. But do you have your proof?"

Bertram's answer was simple. "Fact first, Miss Quayle. The demonstration stands."

The truth, at last, was as plain as the line between true north and false.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"We know now," Bertram Norbury said, standing in the hotel lounge beneath the glow of the crystal chandelier, "that the compass's false bearing and the altered ledger entries were both the work of one person." He held the battered compass in one hand, the hotel ledger in the other, as Gwendolyn Vance, Harriet Kestrel, and Theodora Lachlan listened in tense silence.

Theodora, her voice steady, offered the flat answer: "You mean Nora Quayle."

Bertram nodded. "She exploited the judge's habitual tilt of the compass casing, creating a fifteen-degree offset. That sleight of hand, visible in the way the compass rested just off true north, fooled even the most careful observer. But she went further-manipulating the timing of ledger entries, using different inks and pressures to create a false alibi for a quarter to ten. Witnesses believed what the record showed, and what the judge testified."

Gwendolyn's hand rested on the back of a wing chair, her posture rigid. "You're certain?"

Bertram's tone was measured. "Fact first. Nora alone had exclusive access to both the compass and the ledger. She understood the technical details-how to skew a bearing, how to replicate handwriting, how to use the trust of authority to her advantage. The timing, the altered entries, the corroboration of her presence-each step owned by her hand."

Harriet, her voice bright with polite savagery, leaned forward. "It takes a special kind of nerve to use the rules of the house against its master. To slip the altered entry among the untouched, to tuck the false bearing into the judge's morning inspection-hiding in plain sight within the tools meant to keep order. But then, some of us know what it is to be dismissed for knowing too much, don't we, Mr Norbury?"

Bertram, short retort: "You're not alone, Harriet."

Montague Gaunt's photograph, still on the mantel, caught the light in such a way that his eyes seemed to follow the proceedings-a final, unmeant joke from the man who'd so often been the cause rather than the solution.

Theodora's gloved hand traced the edge of a side table. "And the motive?"

Bertram's answer was direct. "Montague threatened to expose Nora's misconduct-her mismanagement, her past failures. The file of reprimands lay exposed at the edge of the table-proof of the career, the reputation, the years of effort Nora risked losing. When he pressed her, she struck-using the heavy paperweight, then covering her tracks with the very systems she was trusted to maintain."

A hush fell, the only sound the distant rush of surf against the dunes.

A moment for itself: Harriet, alone with the knowledge that a false accusation once cost her a position, adjusted the angle of a lamp, the light shifting gently across the carpet.

Gwendolyn's voice was soft. "She used the trust we placed in her-turned it into a weapon."

Bertram nodded. "She did. But her own expertise betrayed her in the end."

Theodora, with a faint smile, said, "That would be unfortunate-for her."

The lounge, for a moment, seemed to breathe again, the tension breaking as reality settled into its familiar patterns, the truth at last revealed.

## Chapter 10: Confrontation and Aftermath

"It's settled now," Bertram Norbury said, his voice low as the last of the night seeped into the corners of the hotel office. The heavy silence was broken only by the distant hiss of surf against the dunes, and the soft turning of ledger pages in Gwendolyn Vance's steady hands.

Gwendolyn pressed her palm to the ledger, the ink still faintly smudged from Nora Quayle's undoing. "The records will be honest, from now on," she said. Her tone was clipped, but there was a new steadiness to it, as if the collapse of old certainties had left room for something cleaner to grow.

Bertram, standing by the window, adjusted the battered compass on the sill, aligning it to true north. He no longer needed it to find his bearings. "I'll stay to see the inquiry through, but after that-the seaside is yours again, Miss Vance." His reputation, tested, was left intact; his sense of purpose finer for having weathered the storm.

Theodora Lachlan entered quietly, her silhouette framed by the moonlit dunes. She placed a sealed letter on the desk, the last of the blackmailer's threats finally set aside. "There will be no more secrets about the inheritance," she said, understatement softening the confession. Her fortune, preserved, now rested on open ground.

Harriet Kestrel, returning to the lounge, gathered the glasses from the tables, her hands moving with practiced care. Dismissed once for knowing too much, she found a quiet vindication in her continued diligence, her place among the staff secure at last.

Montague Gaunt's memory lingered, but not as a shadow; the battered stopwatch he'd left behind was wound by one of the kitchen boys, who timed the tea eggs with it every morning. No one spoke of the race he'd won by cheating, nor the scandals that trailed him like exhaust. Instead, it was the legend of his speed-a man who could never walk when he could run-that survived, retold as staff mimicked his sprint across the lobby, their laughter echoing over breakfast.

A moment for itself: Theodora paused at the edge of the carpet, stooping to retrieve a stray cufflink embedded in the pile. She turned it over in her palm, then set it beside the guest register, a small token restored to its place.

Gwendolyn closed the ledger, dust rising from its cover. "As it must be," she said, and the words seemed to settle the room.

Bertram, picking up his hat and gloves, nodded to the assembled company. "Fact first. But sometimes, when all is settled, the facts are enough."

The lamps were dimmed, the office returned to quiet order. Out on the dunes, the sand drifted in the wind, covering all traces of what had passed. The hotel, its secrets spent, resumed its ordinary life-the bell at the desk, the laughter in the lounge, the clink of glasses, the hush of the sea.
