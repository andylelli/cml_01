What works

The opening plants the right objects: Bertram dead in the cabin, the bloodied baton, gramophone log, seven-minute intermission, three-minute pose, music resuming at 4:20, and Gwendolyn’s attention to the rehearsal board. That gives the reader a clear mystery package from the start.

The best improvement is Chapter 8. Nora actually restages the silent intermission, assigns people positions, watches Gwendolyn look toward the crew door, and catches her leaving during the silence. That is much stronger than simply explaining “memory anchoring.”

The final line of Chapter 8 is also good:

“You gave them the pose, and they gave you the alibi. That’s the neatest thing you ever staged.”

That is probably the best summary of the mechanism in the whole draft.

Main problem: the time math is still confused

The draft keeps saying:

silent intermission: seven minutes
last pose: three minutes
music resumes: twenty past four
murder window: ten minutes

But if the music paused at 4:00 and the silent intermission lasted seven minutes, the music should resume at 4:07, not 4:20.

Chapter 6 also says the autopsy places death at 4:20, then says that is “within the silent intermission,” which does not work if the intermission begins at 4:00 and lasts seven minutes.

You need one locked timeline. Best version:

4:00 — gramophone stops.
4:00–4:03 — dancers hold final pose.
4:03–4:10 — silent transition continues; everyone assumes stillness continues.
4:04–4:09 — Gwendolyn slips through crew corridor, kills Bertram, returns.
4:10 — gramophone resumes.

Then delete 4:20 entirely.

Or change it to:

4:10 — silent intermission begins.
4:10–4:13 — held pose.
4:13–4:20 — concealed movement window.
4:20 — music resumes / death discovered.

Either works. The current version tries to use both.

Character Life / Relationship Richness: 7/10

This has decent character life. Harriet’s theatrical defensiveness, Desmond’s protocol anxiety, Julian’s legal caution, and Gwendolyn’s polished managerial control all create a believable ballet-company hierarchy.

Chapter 10 is also mostly genuine aftermath. Harriet’s “vindication can be exhausting,” Desmond’s softened relief, Julian’s uncertain future, and Nora’s “silence is a poor witness” line all help the cast feel less like clue-machines.

But Gwendolyn and Bertram still need one stronger personal scene. “He was destroying everything I cared for” is understandable, but generic. Give one concrete wound: he cut dancers’ wages, cancelled her choreography, threatened to dissolve the company, or publicly humiliated her.

Chapter 9 and Chapter 10 still repeat too much

Chapter 9 clears Harriet, Desmond, and Julian, which is useful. But it repeats the intermission, logs, witness statements, and Gwendolyn’s motive after Chapter 8 has already solved the case.

Chapter 10 is better, but still repeats evidence:

That should be lighter. Chapter 10 should be almost all emotional aftermath.

Overall

As written: 82/100.
