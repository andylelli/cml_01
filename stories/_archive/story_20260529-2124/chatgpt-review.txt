What is working

The tide clue is excellent. It feels more natural and memorable than some of the clock-only versions:

tide mark does not match stopped clock → clock time is false → death occurred earlier → alibis must be rechecked.

That gives the story a distinctive seaside identity.

The chain against Eastwood is also fairly strong:

clock key scratches → no authorized winding → tide contradicts clock → semaphore flash was deliberate → elevator allowed silent movement → fingerprints on clock key → footage shows Eastwood silencing victim → reenactment exposes him.

That is a complete detective structure.

Main problems
1. Too many mechanisms

The story has:

tide marks
stopped clock
scratched winding key
semaphore lamp
rooftop signal
elevator delayed-release mechanism
grease smudge
security footage
fingerprints

That is exciting, but it risks feeling overloaded. The tide + clock + semaphore are enough. The elevator can stay, but it should be simpler.

2. The victim is still unnamed

The story keeps saying “the victim.” Give the victim a name early.

For example:

Sir Malcolm Verity lay at the foot of the terrace steps, his coat dark with seawater.

This would make the case feel less abstract.

3. The murder method is unclear

How did Eastwood actually kill the victim? The story says footage shows him “silencing the victim,” but that is vague.

Add one clear line:

Eastwood struck him during the argument and pushed him down the terrace steps.

or:

Eastwood smothered him in the restricted wing, then moved the body to the terrace.

Without that, Eastwood confesses to manipulation more than murder.

4. Some clue math is messy

You mention:

“wound back by forty minutes”
“nearly three-quarters of an hour”
“two-thirds of an hour minutes”
“four decades of minutes minutes”

Keep one clean phrase:

The clock had been set back forty minutes.

Use it everywhere.

5. Pronoun errors are still bad

Examples: Linda, Sharon, Elisabeth, and Eastwood sometimes get wrong pronouns. Parkins is also sometimes referred to as “she.” These errors pull the score down heavily.

6. Ending is too report-like

The final confrontation works structurally, but lines like:

“Sharon Sheldon’s alibi was confirmed because…”

sound like generated scoring notes. Make it dialogue or narration:

“Sharon was in the dining hall,” Parkins said. “Three guests and two waiters saw her there throughout supper.”

Chapter/section notes
Discovery on the Terrace

Strong opening. The tide mark and stopped clock conflict is the best part. But it explains too much too early: “the murderer wound back…” should be discovered later.

Contradicting the Clock

Good use of tidal charts. Sharon and Elisabeth add useful tension. Some dialogue cuts off mid-sentence, which hurts polish.

Tampering in the Clock Room

The scratched key and ledger are good concrete evidence. But “after committing the killer” is a major broken phrase.

Rooftop Lamp Flash Report

The lamp signal is a nice genre clue. It adds wartime/post-war flavour. Keep it.

Decoding the Signal

Good clue development. Semaphore keys + lamp smudge make the flash feel intentional. Eastwood’s evasiveness works.

Elevator Secrets

The elevator route is clever but maybe one mechanism too many. If kept, simplify it to: Eastwood used the delayed elevator to pass the victim’s floor unseen.

Alibi Conflicts and Eliminations

Useful structurally, but too report-like. Turn alibi confirmations into sharper dialogue.

Linking Eastwood to the Evidence

This is strong: fingerprints on the winding key and security footage are decisive. But security footage may feel too modern unless the period supports it. If this is post-war 1940s/50s, use a hotel porter witness or surveillance mirror/log instead.

The Discriminating Test

Good idea: reenact tide, clock, and lamp. This is the story’s strongest set-piece. Make it the big reveal scene.

Clearing the Innocent and Culprit Confrontation

Good closure, but too much repeated alibi clearing. Shorten it and focus on Eastwood’s motive and confession.
