"I didn't want it to end this way. Dr. Finch threatened to expose something-something that would ruin me. I thought if I could buy time, I could find another way. But she wouldn't listen. " His shoulders slumped, the fight gone from his posture. "I killed her. I strangled her with her own silk scarf. I thought I was protecting my reputation-my future. I never meant for it to come to this. " The admission hung in the air, heavy with the weight of consequence.

## Chapter 10: The Revelation

The echo of the constable's departing footsteps faded into the cold hush of the hotel lobby. A pale morning light seeped through the tall windows, casting faint, wavering shadows across the marble floor. The air was thick with the scent of old polish and a lingering trace of last night's wind, which rattled the panes and left a chill that pressed into Eleanor Voss's bones. She stood at the center of the lobby, gloved hand resting lightly on the clock's casing, her breath steadying as she drew in the quiet, the tension, the finality. Around her, the others waited-Hugo Vane by the window, Captain Ivor Hale near the fireplace, Beatrice Quill perched on a settee, and Sylvia Trent rigid by the reception desk-each posture betraying exhaustion, dread, or brittle hope. The silence was taut, the only sound the muted ticking of the clock, now righted but forever suspect.

Eleanor's voice, when it came, was low but unwavering, threading through the morning's dim light. "We have circled this deception for hours, but the pattern now stands before us. The clock-once the heart of our order-became the instrument of our confusion. It was last seen accurate by multiple witnesses at ten minutes past ten. Yet the time the victim was last known to be alive, according to the tampered clock, was twenty minutes past nine. The forensic evidence, however, is clear: Dr. Finch died at a quarter past eleven. Someone used the clock to mislead us all, to buy time and scatter suspicion. " Her gaze lingered on the faces before her, pausing at Hugo Vane, whose hands had gone still, clenched tight around the edge of his notebook. A faint creak from the old floorboards drew every eye to Eleanor as she stepped away from the clock, her heels clicking softly in the hush.

"Captain Ivor Hale," she said, her tone formal, "is cleared. His alibi is verified with staff records-he did not return to The Grand Seaside Hotel until just before midnight, which proves he could not have tampered with the clock or been present at the time of Dr. Finch's death. The club's log and the porter's signature confirm it. " The captain's shoulders eased, a tremor of relief passing through his rigid stance, though his eyes lingered on Eleanor with a gratitude too heavy for words. "Beatrice Quill is also cleared," Eleanor continued, turning to the young woman who twisted a handkerchief in her lap. "Her article research shows no motive, and her presence in the lounge was witnessed by several guests at the crucial hour.

The timeline holds because her friends vouched for her until after the clock struck ten minutes past ten, which proves Beatrice Quill could not have been involved. " Beatrice's bravado crumbled, and she released a shaky breath, eyes shining with tears she refused to shed. "Sylvia Trent's location is confirmed by multiple witnesses. She was seen leaving the hotel to send a telegram, and her receipt matches the time she was away. Several guests recall her brisk exit, and a porter at the post office remembers her arrival. Therefore, Sylvia Trent is innocent-the evidence places her outside The Grand Seaside Hotel when the clock was last adjusted. " Sylvia's composure held, but a faint tremor ran through her hand as she pressed her palm flat against the counter, the burden of suspicion finally eased. Eleanor allowed herself a single, shuddering breath.

As she spoke, Beatrice Quill looked up, her voice tentative but clear: "Miss Voss, you were with us in the library the whole time, weren't you? " Sylvia nodded, confirming, "Yes, I saw you there as well-until after the body was discovered. " Eleanor met their eyes, the relief evident in her posture. "That is correct. Two witnesses saw me in the library at the time the murder could have occurred. I was present with them until well after the body was discovered. " The words were a relief and an admission-she had not been above suspicion, and the process had cost her more than she cared to show. All eyes now turned to Hugo Vane. The lamplight caught on his spectacles, glinting off the tension in his jaw. Eleanor's tone softened, but the steel beneath was unmistakable. "Hugo Vane, you alone had the means, the knowledge, and the opportunity.

You were seen near the clock before the commotion began. The scratches around the mechanism match the blade of your penknife, which I found in your coat pocket last night. You understood the workings well enough to set the hands back, to trigger the chime out of sequence, and to create a false timeline. You strangled Dr. Finch with her own silk scarf, then manipulated the clock so that suspicion would fall elsewhere. But the true time of death-confirmed at a quarter past eleven-betrayed you. The clock's testimony could be made to lie, but not the evidence of the body. " The words hung in the air, heavy as the autumnal mist beyond the windows. Hugo Vane's mask cracked. His voice, when it came, was raw. "I thought I could protect her-from a greater danger. Dr. Finch threatened to expose secrets that would have destroyed more than my reputation.

She was stubborn-wouldn't listen, wouldn't wait. I only meant to frighten her, to force her silence. But it went too far. The clock-yes, I tampered with it. I thought if I could buy time, the truth would never find me. I never wanted to kill her-God knows, I didn't. " No one spoke as the confession settled. A constable, summoned by Sylvia earlier, entered quietly and took Hugo by the arm. His resistance was gone, replaced by a hollow resignation. Hugo paused, looking to Eleanor. "You know what it's like to be desperate, don't you? To want to protect someone, even if it means crossing a line you can't uncross? " His words were not a plea, but an admission of the gray space between justice and mercy. As he was led away, the lobby seemed to grow colder, the light sharper and more pitiless. In the aftermath, silence reigned.

Beatrice Quill wept openly now, her bravado gone, her shoulders shaking. Captain Ivor Hale moved to his side, a steadying hand on his back. Sylvia Trent closed her eyes, a sigh escaping her lips, relief and sorrow mingling as she braced herself against the counter. Eleanor stood apart, watching the constable's silhouette vanish down the corridor, the finality of consequence echoing in every footstep. The Grand Seaside Hotel was changed-its guests altered, their alliances and wounds laid bare by the weight of the truth. The emotional cost pressed in. Captain Ivor Hale murmured, "The war taught us about sacrifice, but I never thought it would follow us here, into peacetime, into this place. " Beatrice Quill, voice trembling, whispered, "We all wanted something-approval, security, survival. But not this. Not blood on the floor. " Sylvia Trent, regaining her composure, drew herself up.

"The hotel will survive. We must. But we will not forget. " Eleanor lingered, her eyes on the clock. The chime now rang true, but it was no longer a comfort-only a reminder that even the most orderly mechanisms could be turned to chaos in desperate hands. Relief came, but it was tinged with regret. She wondered if she had truly brought justice, or merely exposed another kind of tragedy. The morning sun crept higher, lighting the lobby in pale gold. For a brief moment, Eleanor allowed herself to hope that the truth-however costly-might be enough. Outside, the wind carried the scent of the sea and the promise of another day. Within The Grand Seaside Hotel, the survivors gathered themselves, changed in ways they could not yet name. Eleanor turned from the clock, her own burdens heavier, but her resolve unbroken. Time, once the enemy, now ticked forward-imperfect, but honest, at last.
