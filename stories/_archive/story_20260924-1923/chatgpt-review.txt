This is a strong locked-room/coastal-tide draft, and I’d place it just below the best Blackmoor Sanctuary clock draft. It has a clearer physical “impossible room” hook than most of the recent stories, and the final reenactment is satisfying. The main weakness is that the time trick is a little muddled: the story says the “staged hour” is 3:20, the true death is 3:30, and Kestrel’s alibi runs until 3:25, but those pieces need to be stated more cleanly earlier.

I’d score it around 85/100.

What works

The opening has a genuine locked-room appeal. Katherine is dead inside a locked room, the key is still in the door, the window is latched, yet the tide has exposed a cave beneath the hotel wall. That gives the reader an immediate physical puzzle, not just an abstract alibi problem.

The clue chain is also well unified. The blueprints show a natural cave with tidal access; the tide chart marks low tide at 3:20; the window is seven feet above the rocks; the hollow foil contains the duplicate key and sketch; and the final reenactment proves Kestrel can climb, unlock, enter, and return in under ten minutes. These clues all belong to the same mechanism.

The final demonstration is one of the better recent proof scenes. Having each suspect attempt the climb is theatrical, fair, and visual. Neville’s injury, Hector’s failed climb, Bertram’s failed climb, and Desmond’s successful ascent all dramatize the deduction instead of merely explaining it.

Main issue: clarify the 3:20 / 3:25 / 3:30 logic

The intended solution appears to be:

3:20 — low tide exposes the cave.
3:25 — Desmond’s claimed alibi or recorded presence ends.
3:30 — Katherine is actually killed.
The false assumption is that the cave opportunity ended at 3:20, making Desmond seem covered by the fencing hall log.
But in reality, he had enough time after 3:25 to use the route and kill her at 3:30.

The draft says “the staged hour” was 3:20 and the true death was 3:30, which is workable, but the phrase “staged reading” sounds slightly wrong because the tide chart itself is not fake. The trick is not that the tide chart is falsified; it is that everyone over-trusts the exactness of the 3:20 low tide as if the cave were usable for only that instant.

Better wording:

“The tide chart did not lie,” Norbury said. “We did. We treated low tide as a single moment instead of a window.”

Then:

“The cave was exposed at 3:20 and remained usable long enough for Kestrel to leave the salle after 3:25, reach the window, unlock it, kill Katherine at 3:30, and return before suspicion settled.”

That would make the timing cleaner and more plausible.

The locked-room method is strong

The hidden key in the hollow foil is one of the best concrete clues. It ties the fencing world directly to the impossible-room trick: the weapon culture, Desmond’s profession, and the access mechanism all belong together.

The glove pattern on the foil is also good because it makes Desmond’s presence physical, not just circumstantial. The story introduces fresh blood, scratches, and ridges matching his gloves, then later connects those to the murder weapon and his handling of the foil.

The only thing I would sharpen is the window relocking. The method implies Desmond could unlock and lock the window from outside using the duplicate key, but it should be stated once in simple terms:

The key did not open the room door. It opened the old window latch from outside, letting Kestrel leave the room apparently sealed behind him.

That would make the locked-room solution fully satisfying.

Character Life / Relationship Richness: 8/10

This has a better social web than many of the mechanism drafts. Desmond’s loyalty to Hector, Hector’s disgrace, Neville’s injured leg and academy duty, Bertram’s gambling/bravado, and Katherine’s role in the academy all give the case a human background.

The aftermath chapter is also strong. Neville inherits responsibility for the academy, Hector is diminished by the knowledge that his schemes helped trigger the murder, Bertram’s wit softens into fatigue, and Katherine is remembered through the small detail of fresh flowers in the lounge every Monday. That gives the victim presence after death.

The strongest emotional point is Desmond’s motive: he did not only act for himself, but out of a warped loyalty to Hector Gaunt. His confession — “I protected Hector Gaunt because I owed him everything” — gives the murder more emotional shape than simple greed.

Humour / Wit: 8/10

This has good wit, especially through Bertram Vance.

His failed climb line works:

“I see why I never made the rowing team. No window's worth this.”

That is funny, character-specific, and placed in a tense scene without breaking it.

His earlier defence speech is also lively:

“My only crime is a fondness for idle gossip and a tendency to lose at cards.”

That gives him a theatrical voice and helps distinguish him from the more rigid men.

The final chapter has decent weary humour around tea, boiled fog, secrets, and scones, though the line “You could always try the bakery in town” is a little odd because the narration calls it “six words slicing through his own speech.” That phrase feels like scaffold.

Chapter 8 works better than Chapter 9

Chapter 8 is the real reveal: the reenactment, climb, key, timing, accusation, confession, and emotional response all happen there. That is good.

Chapter 9 then repeats the clearances at length. It is not as bad as some previous drafts because the emotional aftermath is present, but the evidence has already been demonstrated. The best fix is to shorten Chapter 9 by about 40%.

Keep:

Hector’s relief.
Neville’s wry line about reckless advances.
Bertram’s subdued reaction.
Desmond holding the key.
Hector’s line: “You might have trusted the law.”
Norbury’s reflection that innocence, once suspected, leaves a mark.

Cut or compress the full alibi recaps.

Chapter 10 is good aftermath

The final chapter is one of the better endings. It does not re-solve the case. It shows what remains: Desmond gone, Hector diminished, Neville taking responsibility, Bertram’s wit returning in a damaged form, Norbury restoring order, the hotel routine restarting, and Katherine remembered through fresh flowers.

The final line works:

“The tide will turn again... Let us hope the next brings less wreckage.”

That is thematically right without over-explaining the trick.

Scaffold/prose issues to remove

These lines/patterns still feel generated or awkward:

“twenty minutes past three-five” — this should be 3:25 or twenty-five past three.
“The truth remained elusive”
“the pattern forming beneath the surface”
“emotional cost of the investigation mounting”
“the facts are stubborn, but not always just”
“The room held its breath”
“The evidence is complete”
“the six words slicing through his own speech”
repeated “I have work to attend to” from multiple characters

Also, be consistent about whether the murder wound is to Katherine’s arm or fatal. The opening says a narrow puncture wound at the upper arm, but she is dead. If the foil killed her, the wound should be chest/neck, or the arm wound should be defensive and the fatal wound should be elsewhere.

Fastest fixes
Clarify that low tide at 3:20 creates a usable window, not a single instant.
Replace “twenty minutes past three-five” with 3:25 throughout.
State exactly how the duplicate key relocks the window from outside.
Fix the wound: make the arm wound defensive, or move the fatal puncture to a believable location.
Trim Chapter 9’s alibi recaps by 40%.
Keep Chapter 10’s aftermath mostly as-is.
Remove scaffold phrases like “room held its breath” and “six words slicing through his own speech.”
Overall

This is a strong upper-tier impossible-room draft. The tidal cave, hollow foil, duplicate key, and reenactment give it a memorable physical solution. It is slightly less polished than the Blackmoor Sanctuary clock story, but the core impossible-room mechanism is arguably more exciting.

As written: 85/100.

With the tide-window logic clarified, the wound fixed, and Chapter 9 trimmed, this could reach 90–92/100.
