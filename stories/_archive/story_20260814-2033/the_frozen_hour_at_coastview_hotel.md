Not at a quarter to ten, as the grandfather clock would have us believe. ' Her words landed with the quiet force of finality. For a moment, no one spoke. 'The logs are clear for the rest of you,' she said, glancing at Beatrice, Captain Ivor Hale, and Sylvia. 'Each record places you away from the lounge during the actual time of death. Mr. Vane's account, however, does not cover those crucial minutes. ' She kept her tone measured, but the implication drew a visible shudder from Beatrice, who pressed her palm to her chest, and a muttered oath from Captain Ivor Hale.

Sylvia Trent broke the tension with a laugh-sharp, almost relieved. 'So the clock's a red herring, and we've been running in circles. I suppose there's a first time for everything. As for the truth-well, we are closer than we were. ' The grandfather clock's face glimmered in the lamplight, the scratch near the wheel catching Eleanor's eye. As the group began to disperse-some in silence, some with hurried words-Eleanor lingered by the stopped clock. Her reflection in the glass was blurred by shadow and lamplight, but her resolve was clear. The truth was close, almost within reach. She pressed her palm to the cold wood, feeling the rough grain beneath her glove. The mechanism remained a mystery, but the gap in Hugo Vane's account was now a chasm, impossible to ignore. The spring night pressed in, thick with the scent of rain and the promise of answers yet to come.

## Chapter 7: Hidden Clues and Reinterpretations

Eleanor pressed her palm to the cold wood of the grandfather clock, the rough grain beneath her glove anchoring her in the hush that had fallen over the lounge. The lamplight flickered against the glass, casting blurred reflections that mingled with the gathering shadows of late evening. The air was thick with the scent of rain-damp carpet and the lingering metallic tang from earlier, while the persistent drizzle outside whispered against the windowpanes. As the others drifted away, Eleanor's resolve sharpened. She would not let the false certainty of the clock dictate the story any longer. A faint echo of footsteps in the corridor signaled Hugo Vane's approach. His silhouette, tall and composed, appeared in the doorway between the lounge and the staff corridor, the dim light outlining the sharp set of his jaw.

He paused, as if weighing whether to enter, then stepped fully into the lounge, his shoes silent on the faded rug. The spring night pressed in, cool and damp, and Eleanor was acutely aware of the tension that lingered in the air between them. She turned, notebook in hand, and regarded Hugo with a measured calm. 'You seem determined, Miss Voss,' Hugo said, his voice low, a practiced steadiness masking something brittle beneath. He moved to the sideboard, fingers trailing absently over the polished wood, but his eyes never quite met hers. 'Are you still chasing ghosts in the machinery, or have you found something more tangible? ' Eleanor offered a thin, dry smile, the kind that had unsettled many a recalcitrant pupil in her teaching days. 'Ghosts are rarely so obliging as to leave fingerprints, Mr. Vane.

But sometimes, the machinery leaves its own. ' She let the silence stretch, then added, 'I'd like to have a look at your room, if you have no objection. There are questions the lounge cannot answer. ' Hugo hesitated, a flicker of unease passing over his features. For a moment, Eleanor thought he might refuse, but then he inclined his head with a show of indifference. 'By all means. I have nothing to hide. ' The words were smooth, but his hand lingered on the velvet pouch at his side, thumb worrying the clasp. Together, they crossed the corridor, the hush broken only by the distant sound of the radio-Vera Lynn's voice, hopeful and incongruous, drifting from a staff room deeper in the hotel. Inside Hugo's room, the atmosphere shifted. The lamp cast a dim glow over the scattered clockwork tools on the desk, while the air was tinged with the faint scent of oil and brass.

The window was cracked open to the drizzle, and a chill breeze carried the spring night's dampness inside. Eleanor's eyes swept the room, noting the battered suitcase, the folded gloves, the velvet pouch. Her gaze lingered on a small chest of drawers beside the bed-unremarkable, save for the faint scratch near the handle, as if it had been opened in haste. She moved to the drawer, pausing just long enough to catch Hugo's reaction. His posture stiffened, but he said nothing. Eleanor slid the drawer open, her gloved fingers brushing against a jumble of odds and ends-a crumpled handkerchief, a pocket diary, a scattering of coins. Beneath these, half-concealed at the back, was a decorative letter opener. Its handle, inlaid with mother-of-pearl, was familiar, but it was the blade that drew her attention: the tip was slightly bent, as though it had been forced against something resistant.

She held it up to the light, turning it so the metal caught the glow. A silence stretched between them, thick with implication. 'A curious place for such an object,' Eleanor remarked, her tone mild. 'One would expect a letter opener to reside on a desk, not hidden away in a drawer. ' She watched Hugo closely, noting the way his jaw tightened. 'It's a keepsake. I seldom use it,' he replied, too quickly. His gaze flicked to the window, then back to her. 'I imply nothing, Mr. Vane. But the blade is bent. That's not the mark of routine correspondence. ' Eleanor set the opener on the desk, careful to keep her tone neutral. She recalled the wound on Dr. Mallory Finch's body-a small, ragged puncture, consistent with a narrow blade and a bent tip. The memory of blood on pale wool, the torn sleeve, pressed at the edges of her mind. Hugo's composure faltered for an instant.

He turned away, busying himself with the clockwork tools as if their arrangement demanded his full attention. 'Clocks and knives-everyone sees what they want to see. The grandfather clock, at least, is honest. It's always kept perfect time, no matter what anyone says. ' His voice was steadier now, but the words were too emphatic, a little forced. 'If you're looking for a villain, you'll have to look elsewhere. The clock's hands are fixed for a reason. Everyone saw it-at a quarter to ten. That's the only certainty we have. ' Eleanor let the assertion hang in the air, resisting the urge to challenge it outright. She glanced at the fine files and screwdrivers scattered on the desk, her mind returning to the scratch marks she had seen on the grandfather clock's escapement wheel. She recalled, too, the wire fragment-no longer than two inches-twisted and forced into place.

The marks on the clock, the scratches on the blade, the bent tip of the letter opener-all details that had seemed disparate, but now began to align in unsettling ways. She set her notebook on the desk and, with deliberate care, compared the letter opener's blade to her notes on the wound. The dimensions matched, and the bend at the tip would account for the ragged edges she had observed. The murder weapon, it seemed, had never left the hotel. It had simply been hidden in plain sight, its presence masked by routine and the illusion of order. A moment of silence stretched, broken only by the faint sound of rain and the distant strains of music from the radio. Hugo's hands trembled as he adjusted the tools, the movement betraying a nervous energy he could not quite suppress. 'You're making a great deal of a misplaced letter opener, Miss Voss. There are dozens like it in the hotel.

Anyone could have taken it, anyone could have used it. ' Eleanor regarded him for a long moment, her expression unreadable. 'Perhaps. But not everyone has your skill with clockwork, or your proximity to the lounge at the critical hour. ' She let the implication settle, then softened her tone with a touch of wryness. 'Still, I suppose even the best clocks can be made to lie, if one is clever enough. ' Hugo's mouth set like a locked door, but he said nothing. The tension in the room was palpable, the spring night pressing in with its damp chill. Eleanor gathered her notes, the letter opener, and her resolve. She had come seeking answers and found instead a new set of questions-questions that would demand a final test, a confrontation with the truth that could no longer be deferred.

As she turned to leave, the lamplight caught the edge of the letter opener, casting a sharp glint across the desk. The evidence was no longer a matter of speculation; it was tangible, weighty, and impossible to ignore. The false timeline, once so carefully constructed, was beginning to unravel. Outside, the rain eased to a whisper, and the Coastview Hotel seemed to exhale, as if bracing itself for the reckoning to come.

## Chapter 8: The Test of Truths

"We begin now," Eleanor said, her voice cutting through the hush that clung to the hotel lounge near the grandfather clock. The lamplight flickered against the glass, throwing long, wavering shadows across the faded rug. The spring night pressed cold and damp through the cracks in the blackout curtains, carrying with it the restless whisper of distant waves. In the silence, the metallic scent of the clock's exposed mechanism seemed sharper, an undercurrent beneath the rain-soaked air. Eleanor set the letter opener on the desk, its earlier glint now muted, and fixed her gaze on the others gathered in an uneasy semicircle. Captain Ivor Hale stood to attention near the window, his jaw rigid, eyes flicking from the clock to Eleanor. Beatrice Quill hovered by the sideboard, her hands smoothing her skirt in nervous repetition.

Sylvia Trent slouched by the hearth, notebook in hand, her pen stilled for once. Hugo Vane lingered nearest the clock, arms folded, his posture easy but his eyes dark with something more watchful than curiosity. Eleanor felt the weight of every expectation, every unspoken accusation, pressing in with the chill. The moment was brittle, charged. A single misstep would shatter it. Eleanor drew the fine wire fragment from her pocket and held it up in the lamplight. "This was recovered from the escapement wheel of the grandfather clock. No longer than two inches, and bent as though forced. " She let the silence settle, then turned to the open panel of the clock. The rain outside seemed to hush, as if the night itself leaned closer. She gestured for all to approach. "The question before us is not who had reason, but who had means and opportunity. Each of you will see for yourself what follows.

The wire, the marks, the tools-let them speak. " Her tone was measured, deliberate, a calm that belied the set of her shoulders. Beatrice Quill stepped forward first, her lips pressed tight. Eleanor handed the wire and invited her to compare it to the mechanism. Beatrice's hands trembled slightly as she fumbled with it, holding it against the scratches on the escapement wheel. The fit was awkward; she glanced at Eleanor, brow furrowed. "I wouldn't know where to begin," she admitted, voice thin. "I manage ledgers and staff, not clockwork. " Eleanor nodded, her eyes gentle. "The kitchen staff placed you tending the service bells when the clock stopped. The bell repair logs bear that out-the delay it caused kept you from the lounge the whole while. You're cleared, Mrs. Quill. " Captain Ivor Hale was next, his movements stiff as he took the wire.

He peered at the mechanism, then handed it back with a shake of his head. "I've spent my share of hours with navigation instruments, Miss Voss, but this-" He gestured to the clock's innards with a soldier's distaste. "-is a puzzle for another mind. " "The smoking room logs place you there from eight fifty to nine thirty," Eleanor said, her tone gentler now, "and more than one guest can vouch for you across that window. You couldn't have touched this clock. You're cleared. " Sylvia Trent accepted the wire with a sardonic smile, flipping it between her fingers. She bent over the mechanism, squinting at the scratches, then shrugged. "I can barely fix a wristwatch, let alone sabotage one of these monsters. Besides, I was in my room-four seventeen-from half past nine onward. Ask the guestbook, ask the staff. " Eleanor's smile was dry as a chalkboard. "Indeed, Miss Trent.

You're cleared as well. " Silence gathered as Eleanor turned to Hugo Vane. He did not reach for the wire. Instead his gaze lingered on the clock, on scratches so precise they could only have come from an expert's hand. Eleanor reached into the velvet pouch she had taken from his room earlier and withdrew a slender file, its tip notched and dulled. "Your hobby is well known, Mr. Vane. You have the knowledge, the access, and the tools. " Under the eyes of the other three, Eleanor set the file against the scratch on the escapement wheel. The notches lined up; the angle matched the gouge in the brass exactly. She fitted the wire fragment into the groove, and it slotted home with a small click that seemed to carry too far in the quiet room. Beatrice's hand rose to her mouth. Sylvia went still, pen forgotten. "This wire, these marks, and your file," Eleanor said. "They belong together.

No one else in this room could have left them. " A tremor passed through Hugo Vane's composure. His arms dropped to his sides, hands open, as if in surrender. "You have it all worked out, Miss Voss," he said, his voice carefully steady. "But what of motive? It's not enough to prove I stopped a clock. " His eyes darted to the others, searching for support and finding none. Eleanor's reply was soft, but every word cut with certainty. "You silenced Dr. Finch to keep her from exposing your dealings, whatever they were. She meant to make them public. The clock was to be your alibi. Instead it undid you-it showed a quarter to ten, and only a man with your skill could have made it lie so neatly. She died nearer ten minutes past ten. You've no way to account for where you were at that hour. " The room was still, the rain outside a faint memory.

Hugo's expression shifted, pride and fear warring across his features. "She would have destroyed everything," he muttered, more to himself than to the others. "I had no choice. " Eleanor straightened, some of the tension draining from her shoulders. "There is always a choice, Mr. Vane. The clock cannot absolve you now. " She turned to the others, her gaze passing over each in turn. "Captain Hale, Mrs. Quill, Miss Trent-none of you had any part in this. Only one person here had the knowledge, the motive, and the opportunity. " She laid the two readings side by side so the room could weigh them together. Either the murder had happened at a quarter to ten, as the grandfather clock showed, or someone had arranged the evidence to make it seem so. Stated plainly, the question stopped being a matter of suspicion and became something that could simply be tried.

So she tried it, there in front of them, recreating the conditions the evidence demanded and letting the room watch what came of it: the scratches on the escapement wheel, the latch where the clock had been stopped, the file from Hugo's own kit fitted to the marks, the sliver of wire that had caught in the works. She ran it once, then again, and it came out the same both times. One theory could not survive that. The other stood, plain enough that no one needed to be told which was which. Only Hugo Vane's account still depended on the version that had just failed. As the spring night deepened beyond the lounge, the air seemed to ease, the sense of expectation replaced by something quieter-relief, a little sorrow, the flat certainty of a thing finished. Hugo Vane sank into the nearest chair, the composure gone out of him entirely.

The others, each in their own way, let out a breath they hadn't known they were holding. Eleanor's dry wit surfaced at last, gentle as the first grey light at the window. "Clocks do have their secrets, it seems. But they make poor accomplices. "

## Chapter 9: Suspect Clearances

It was well past midnight when Eleanor Voss, still standing in the dim glow of the Hotel lounge, let the silence stretch. The lamplight cast long shadows across the faded rug, and the air held the chill of spring, damp and edged with the scent of rain that seeped through the cracks in the blackout curtains. The hush was broken only by the faint echo of waves against the cliff below and the muted creak of upholstery as Hugo Vane shifted in his chair, his composure unraveling in the aftermath of the test. The others hovered at the periphery, each caught between relief and the residue of tension that lingered like smoke after a storm. Eleanor's voice, when it came, was measured and low, cutting through the quiet. 'We must be thorough, for justice's sake.

Before the truth can settle, every shadow must be dispelled. ' She drew the guestbook and the stack of logs closer, her gloved fingers steady as she leafed through the pages. The paper was rough beneath her touch, the inked times standing stark against the lamplight. She turned to Captain Ivor Hale, who stood a little apart, his shoulders squared but his eyes rimmed with exhaustion. 'Captain Hale,' Eleanor began, 'your presence in the smoking room is attested by more than one guest. The logs show you entered at eight fifty and did not leave until half past nine. I have spoken with a gentleman and a woman-both recall your company, the former even remarking on your story about the Mediterranean crossing. The staff corridor logs bear your signature at nine thirty.

The murder, as we have established, occurred at ten minutes past ten-well after you left the smoking room, but your whereabouts are accounted for by the time you reached your room. There is no window in which you could have slipped away unseen. ' Captain Ivor Hale exhaled, the sound audible in the hush. His hands unclenched at his sides. 'You're certain, then? ' he asked, voice roughened by fatigue and something like hope. Eleanor nodded, her gaze steady. 'You are cleared, Captain. The evidence and testimony agree. ' A faint flush crept up his neck, and he looked away, the burden of suspicion finally lifting. The relief in his posture was unmistakable, but it was tempered by a lingering wariness, as if he feared the verdict might yet be reversed. Beatrice Quill, who had been smoothing the edge of the sideboard with nervous precision, straightened as Eleanor turned to her. 'Mrs.

Quill, the service bell mechanism was inspected this afternoon. The delay that kept you from the lounge was due to a faulty spring-confirmed by both the repair log and the testimony of a young woman in the kitchen. The bell rang for tea at nine twenty and again at nine forty-five, and you were seen by staff in the kitchen both times, occupied with the tea trolley and the menus. The logs place you away from the lounge for the entire window in which the murder occurred. ' Beatrice's composure wavered, her lips parting in a silent exhalation. 'I have always prided myself on order, Miss Voss,' she murmured, her voice brittle. 'It seems my routines have saved me, if nothing else. ' Eleanor's reply was gentle but firm. 'Order is not always the enemy of truth, Mrs. Quill.

You are cleared. ' Beatrice's shoulders sagged, and for a moment, she seemed smaller, the weight of weeks of suspicion falling away. Yet there was no triumph in her relief-only a kind of quiet, exhausted gratitude. Sylvia Trent, who had watched the proceedings with arms folded and jaw set, met Eleanor's gaze with a flicker of challenge. 'You'll find my name in the guestbook at nine thirty, and the staff logs show I was in room four seventeen until ten. The chambermaid, a woman, saw me reading by the window-she even remarked on the light from the corridor. The ledger has my signature at ten, just as the clocks in the hall showed. ' Eleanor nodded, flipping to the relevant page. 'Your presence is confirmed by both the guestbook and staff testimony. You could not have left your room, committed the act, and returned without being seen.

You are cleared, Miss Trent. ' Sylvia's mouth quirked in a wry half-smile, her posture relaxing for the first time all evening. 'I suppose that's as close to an apology as I'll get, Miss Voss. ' The tension in her voice was leavened by relief, and for a moment, the brittle edge of suspicion softened into something almost companionable. The room, once charged with accusation, now seemed to breathe again. The spring night pressed in at the windows, but inside, the air was lighter-though not yet easy. Eleanor let the silence settle, her gaze sweeping over the three she had just cleared. 'You have each borne suspicion with more grace than most. The evidence is clear: none of you could have committed this crime. The clock's false testimony, the confusion of times, the web of alibis-all have been unraveled.

Only one account remains unaccounted for. ' Hugo Vane sat with his head bowed, hands clasped between his knees. The lamplight caught the sheen of sweat at his temple. Eleanor's tone was softer now, but implacable. 'You alone possessed the skill to tamper with the grandfather clock, the opportunity to be in the lounge at the critical moment, and the motive to silence Dr. Finch. The wire fragment, the scratches on the escapement wheel, the file from your own kit-all point to you. The murder was not committed at a quarter to ten, as the clock insisted, but at ten minutes past ten. You stabbed Dr. Finch with the letter opener, then staged the scene to mislead us all. ' A tremor passed through Hugo, and for a moment, he looked as if he might speak. Instead, he pressed a hand to his mouth, eyes shining with unshed tears. 'She would have ruined me,' he whispered, voice cracking.

'She threatened to expose everything-my dealings, my debts... I tried to frighten her, but she wouldn't be frightened. I never meant-' He broke off, the words lost in the hush. Eleanor's gaze did not waver. 'You made a choice, Mr. Vane. Whatever your reasons, the cost is plain to all of us now. ' She turned to the others, her voice gentler. 'Captain Hale, Mrs. Quill, Miss Trent-you are innocent of this crime. The law will see to the rest. ' For a long moment, no one moved. The only sound was the wind outside, rattling the blackout curtains. The emotional cost of the night hung heavy in the air: relief for those cleared, sorrow for Dr. Finch, and the ruin of Hugo Vane, who sat broken in the lamplight. Eleanor gathered the guestbook and logs, her hands steady but her heart unquiet. The truth, at last, had been spoken, but justice, she knew, was never as simple as the closing of a ledger.

The spring night pressed on, and the Coastview Hotel would not soon forget the hour its certainties were undone.

## Chapter 10: The Cost of Unraveling

The hush of midnight clung to the Hotel lounge, lamplight flickering upon polished wood and the faded rug where so much had unravelled. The cold pressed in from the wind outside, rattling the blackout curtains, and the air bore the scent of spent rain mingled with the sharper tang of old brass. Eleanor Voss remained by the sideboard, her gloved hand resting on the guestbook, as the others lingered in uneasy silence. The emotional weight from earlier-relief for those cleared, sorrow for Dr. Mallory Finch, and the ruin of Hugo Vane-settled over the room like a heavy shroud, refusing to lift. Beatrice Quill gathered the guestbook and the bell logs, her hands trembling as she stacked them into a neat pile-a futile gesture of restored order. Captain Ivor Hale lingered by the hearth, staring into the cold grate, while Sylvia Trent slipped her notebook into her coat, her face set in unfamiliar restraint. Eleanor's gaze settled last on Hugo Vane. He remained motionless, shoulders hunched, eyes fixed on the floor.

In the silence, Eleanor felt the cost most keenly: the loss of Dr. Finch, the shattering of trust, and the knowledge that even the most careful order could be undone by a single desperate act. The lounge was nearly empty now, the lamplight growing thin as dawn pressed at the edges of the blackout curtains. Eleanor's final task was to close the guestbook and set it aside. The familiar weight of her notebook in her palm was oddly comforting-a small measure of control reclaimed from the chaos. As she turned from the window, she caught a last glimpse of the grandfather clock, its hands forever marking a lie. The truth, she thought, was heavier still.

She drew a slow breath, the air cool and quiet, and let herself feel, for a moment, the relief of having endured. Hugo Vane was responsible; the evidence allowed no other reading.
