Nora's gaze never wavered. "Torque says otherwise, but the evidence is plain. If the readings are off, the times are off." She tapped the ledger, her hand steady.

Bertram lifted the guest ledger, opening it to the critical pages. "With Nora's exclusive access, she could adjust the entries to fit the shifted bearings. The ink tells its own tale-entries for eight forty-five, nine o'clock, and a quarter to ten, each in a different hand and hue."

Harriet stepped back, the lamplight catching the locket at her throat. "I was cleaning rooms from ten minutes to nine to a quarter past nine. The staff saw me-laundry maid, bellboy, even Mrs. Redfern in the pantry."

Bertram nodded, ticking off alibis. "Harriet Kestrel, cleared by corroborated staff testimony for her cleaning duty-confirmed by three witnesses." He set her name aside in his pocketbook.

A hush lingered. Theodora Lachlan, her hand resting on the back of a chair, watched the compass with a thoughtful frown. "Montague Gaunt's only alibi is that he is beyond suspicion-deceased." She brushed a speck of dust from the chair's arm.

Bertram's reply was gentle: "No one stands outside the facts, not even the dead. But in this case, the facts exonerate him: he could not have acted, nor could his presence be proved."

A breeze rattled the windowpane, the shadows lengthening as the test concluded. Bertram closed the ledger and compass, setting them side by side on the desk. "The mechanism is clear. The only one with the knowledge-and the opportunity-to exploit both was Nora Quayle."

As the room settled into quiet, each person absorbed the conclusion in their own way. Harriet's posture eased, the tension in her shoulders softening. Bertram watched the compass needle, now stilled at true north, and for a moment, the memory of distant, uncertain fields returned-then faded, replaced by the certainty of the evidence before him.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"If only they knew," Harriet Kestrel said, her voice slicing the hush in the lounge as the clock struck midnight. Gwendolyn Vance sat by the fireplace, her hands folded in her lap, the flickering light sharpening the lines of her face. Theodora Lachlan stood at the window, one hand tracing the chilled glass, her gaze fixed somewhere beyond the dunes. Bertram Norbury entered, closing the door with a quiet finality.

The room was heavy with anticipation. Bertram placed the battered compass and the hotel ledger on the low table between them, their presence as unmistakable as a verdict.

Theodora's answer was flat. "What happens now?" She turned from the window, the folds of her skirt whispering against the carpet.

Bertram's gaze moved from face to face. "Now we settle the matter of guilt. The evidence is clear. The judge's compass habit-tilting the casing every time-shifted the bearing by fifteen degrees. The alibi it created was false, but only someone who knew both the judge's method and the ledger's vulnerabilities could exploit it fully."

Gwendolyn's voice was steady, almost resigned. "As it must be. The truth has a way of surfacing, in the end." She reached for a letter on the side table, her fingers brushing the edge.

Bertram continued, his tone measured. "Bertram paused, his fingers resting on the ledger. "The entries for Nora Quayle-notice the shine here, and the uneven pressure on these lines. Only someone desperate would risk fresh ink on an old page, just as only someone confident would use the judge's own compass habit to their advantage. The trail leads exactly where she needed it to-away from suspicion, and away from the office at the critical hour." She relied on the judge's authoritative-yet inaccurate-compass reading to place herself away from the scene, but in fact, multiple witnesses saw her near the office between nine and quarter to ten."

Theodora's hand dropped from the window, her posture rigid. "She used the very tools meant to protect us."

Harriet's short retort was soft, almost mournful: "She learned from the best-survival is a lesson taught by necessity." She turned a brooch in her hand, its silver gleam catching the firelight.

Bertram's reply was brisk: "But necessity is not innocence." He set the compass gently on the table.

Nora Quayle, standing by the door, did not speak. She simply closed her hand around the doorknob, her expression unreadable. The silence that followed was thick with relief, regret, and something unspoken-the knowledge that justice, at last, was being served.

Gwendolyn unfolded the letter, smoothing it on her knee. "The hotel will recover. We always do." Her voice was quiet, but unyielding.

Theodora looked down at her hands, the faint trace of scandal lingering on her skin. "That would be unfortunate-for all of us. But perhaps now, the worst is over."

Harriet, dismissed unjustly once before, looked around the room. For the first time since the murder, her posture relaxed, the edge in her voice softened. "It's not the end, but it's a start."

Bertram gathered the compass and ledger, returning them to the safe. The fire crackled, and beyond the windows, the dunes lay silent beneath the moon.

## Chapter 10: Confrontation and Aftermath

"That would be unfortunate," Theodora Lachlan remarked, as she watched the constable escort Nora Quayle from the hotel office and into the pale light spilling over the dunes. The case was closed-Nora's manipulation of the ledger and compass exposed in the full glare of evidence, her dry wit finally silenced by the weight of truth.

Gwendolyn Vance, standing by the window, exhaled slowly. She reached for the ledger, running her hand over its leather cover, no longer fearing the secrets it contained. Her rule was quieter now; as Gwendolyn moved through the office, the staff nodded to her, one young maid offering a careful smile and a respectful, "Good evening, Miss Vance." The authority she wielded felt more solid, shaped by the ordeal and the visible trust of those around her.

Bertram Norbury accepted a cup of tea from the kitchen maid, the steaming china a small comfort in hands grown used to cold facts and colder nights. He allowed himself a rare indulgence-he would remain at the hotel, not as an investigator, but as a guest, his reputation burnished rather than battered by the ordeal.

Harriet Kestrel set a vase of fresh wildflowers on the office mantel, her movements light with relief. Dismissed once without cause, she now found herself spoken to with genuine courtesy, her opinions weighed and her presence welcomed. Theodora lingered in the lounge, her voice softer when speaking with staff, the sharp edges of her social maneuvering blunted by the events.

Montague Gaunt was remembered not for his death, but for a moment years ago-standing atop his battered racer, arms raised in unearned triumph after a win that few suspected was purchased, not won. His laughter, loud and reckless, echoed in the minds of those who had known him, the memory untainted by the manner of his end.

Theodora Lachlan, after the constable's departure, paused at the office threshold. She glanced toward the corridor where two staff exchanged nervous looks, their conversation halting as she passed. The family dispute, long the subject of whispered gossip, faded behind closed doors; the sharpest teeth dulled, for now, by the night's resolution.

As the hour grew late, the hotel office returned to its ordinary rhythm. The ledger lay open, awaiting new names; the compass, at last leveled, pointed true north. Outside, the dunes whispered in the wind, bearing silent witness to secrets buried and revealed. Staff set tables for breakfast, the world righting itself, and the place-restored to its proper use-waited for the next day's sun.
