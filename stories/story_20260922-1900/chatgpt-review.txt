This is fairly solid mechanically, but it feels like a step backward in human richness and ending structure. The core clock trick is understandable, and the evidence points cleanly to Ottoline, but Chapters 9 and 10 overlap badly: Chapter 9 already reveals the culprit, while Chapter 10 is titled “The Culprit Revealed” even though the culprit has already confessed.
I’d score it around 80/100.

What works
The opening gives a clear fair-play puzzle: Beatrice is dead beside a candlestick, her pocket watch has stopped at 3:10, while the mantel clock shows 3:45. That immediately gives the reader a simple contradiction to follow.   Pasted markdown
The clock mechanism is also more coherent than several recent drafts. The detent lever, early chime, misaligned hands, glove fragment, fingerprints, altered logbook, and final timed test all belong to one mechanism. The story does not split itself between too many tricks.   Pasted markdown
The Chapter 8 test is the best part: Ambrose and Ottoline replicate the clock adjustment, proving the clock face and chime could be made to disagree. The report says the chime sounded at 3:25 while the face displayed 3:45, and Beatrice’s watch at 3:10 aligns with the true death time. That is a proper detective demonstration.   Pasted markdown
Main issue: the time logic needs one clean explanation
The draft gives these times:
pocket watch: 3:10
clock face: 3:45
chime: 3:25
chime sounded 20 minutes early
apparent time of death: around 3:45 / 4:00
actual time of death: 3:10

This can work, but the reveal needs one plain timeline. Right now the reader has to assemble it from several chapters.
Use this:
3:10 — Beatrice is killed; her pocket watch stops.
3:25 — manipulated striking train causes the manor clock to chime early.
3:45 — clock face falsely displays later time because the hands are misaligned.
4:00-ish — household movements are interpreted according to the false chime/face, confusing alibis.
Then the key deduction becomes:
The watch fixed the death at 3:10.
The clock did not prove a later death; it proved someone had tampered with the household’s sense of time.
Only Ottoline had the access, tools, and mechanical knowledge to do that.
That is strong enough without extra fog.
Ottoline’s motive is too abstract
Ottoline’s confession is basically:
“I thought I could fix everything if I could only fix the time.”

That is thematically nice, but not motive-specific enough.   Pasted markdown
Why kill Beatrice?
Possible stronger versions:
Beatrice was about to remove Ottoline from management of the lock/cottage.
Beatrice discovered Ottoline had altered estate maintenance accounts.
Beatrice was changing the will/codicil to cut Ottoline out.
Ottoline had spent years keeping Lockwood functioning and was going to be discarded.
Beatrice publicly humiliated her over the clocks and estate records.

Right now Ottoline has method, but not enough emotional pressure.
Character Life / Relationship Richness: 6/10
This is the weakest area compared with the better recent drafts.
The cast has some voice: Percival is theatrical, Frances is socially sharp, Josephine is blunt and practical, Ottoline is nervous and order-obsessed, Ambrose is methodical. But most of their scenes revolve around the clock. They need more life outside the mechanism.
The best character-life hints are Josephine sleeping in the nursery after the murder, Percival worrying about estate management, Frances arranging letters/flowers as social armor, and Ottoline’s tidy cottage/toolkit.   Pasted markdown
To improve, add two non-clue scenes:
Josephine and Ottoline arguing quietly about who actually keeps Lockwood running.
Frances and Percival discussing what Beatrice’s death means for the estate, with old resentment underneath.
Ottoline alone in the lock-keeper’s cottage, repairing something small while admitting how much she fears being made useless.

That would raise the human score.

If Percival was only in the library from 4:00 to 4:30, that does not cover the actual death time of 3:10. The sentence contradicts itself.   Pasted markdown

Overall
This is a competent clock-mystery draft with a better mechanism than the mannequin-shadow story, but it is not as emotionally alive as the best Blakeney impersonation draft. The puzzle is understandable; the human motive and final-chapter structure need work.
