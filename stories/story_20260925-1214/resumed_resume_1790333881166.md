For a moment, the gesture was all that passed for gratitude—Hector kept his eyes fixed on the matchbox, studiously avoiding a single glance toward Neville or Desmond. After a humiliating public disgrace, even the sound of one's own name could wound.

"Thank you," he said, so quietly it might have been mistaken for a breath. Montague nodded and turned away, his attention already shifting to Neville Lachlan.

Neville took the duplicate key into his palm, testing the weight. He looked at the hollow foil, then back at the detective. "If you're interested in an alibi, not a story, I have mine. I was in the fencing hall from three o'clock to a quarter to four. Twenty witnesses, none of them in need of my pension. The glove mark is old—yesterday's lesson, nothing more."

Montague paused, glancing down at the records. "You are cleared. The weapon's recent stains and handling marks do not belong to you, and the time of death—between twenty minutes past three and half past three—finds you on the other side of the building, surrounded by too many eyes to count. Your presence at the scene is an impossibility."

Neville offered a wry smile. "I take your point." He placed the duplicate key back on the desk, careful not to disturb the blueprints. He did not thank Montague, but his tone softened as he turned his cane toward the door.

Bertram Vance lingered by the window, the magazine now rolled into a tight cylinder. "Well, that's a fine mess for the outsider, isn't it?" he said, offering the words with his usual polite savagery. He turned to Montague. "My maintenance duty took me along the seaward side from ten minutes past three to twenty minutes to four—tools in hand, three staff to confirm. The rain had nearly caught me; my coat's hem was still damp when I sat down to supper. I was in my quarters the rest of the time, and my presence, such as it was, is a matter of record."

Montague nodded, ticking a box on his notepad. "You are cleared—by timing, by duty, by the absence of any evidence linking you to the weapon or to the means of entry. Your resentment is noted, but the law is not sentimental."

Bertram's eyes glittered as he unrolled the magazine, flicking through the pages without reading. He glanced at Desmond and Neville, then turned to the window, his attention fixed on the darkness beyond. "Resentment? I'd call it tradition," he said, and the words hung in the room long after he left.

Neville, standing near the doorway, delivered a speech almost to himself, the words slow and deliberate as he gripped his cane. "I've spent years believing that precision and presence are enough. But proximity isn't guilt, and absence isn't innocence. The fencing hall is a fishbowl, and I'm glad for it. At least I can say, without artifice, that my bad knee keeps me honest—and in plain view."

"Plain view helps," Montague agreed, watching the way Neville's hand trembled ever so slightly as he lifted his cane.

Katherine Quayle's presence loomed at the edge of the room—in the crispness of the blueprints, in the order of the legal folders, in the gentle discipline that haunted every corner. The will sat unopened at the desk's edge, its wax seal unbroken.

Desmond, silent, repacked his briefcase, the hollow foil and duplicate key placed with particular care atop the blueprints. He clicked the clasp shut, the finality sharper than any spoken word. The lamp cast long shadows across the carpet, drawing the lines of the hidden corridor anew.

Montague collected his notepad, his fingers lingering on the spine. "We have cleared the ground," he said, his voice steady with the weight of tradition. "What remains is motive and method—a question of who had both, and who lost more by keeping them hidden."

Desmond drew the curtains with a practiced gesture, the velvet heavy between his fingers. The room grew darker, the only light now from the lamp's small pool, illuminating the objects of law and secrecy that marked the end of this stage and the beginning of something more final.

For a moment, nothing moved except the minute hand of the carriage clock, nudging its way towards the next hour, as if timing alone could bring order to the mysteries of men, tides, and locks.

## Chapter 6: Alibis Under Scrutiny

"Let us not mistake motion for progress," Montague Norbury said, his hand steadying the billiard cue as he gestured for Hector Gaunt to approach the table. The green felt was illuminated in patches by the stained-glass pendant lamp above, and the balls stood arranged from the afternoon's play—unsettled, as if awaiting an outcome that would restore order to the room. The evening pressed against the hotel's windows, the last salt haze of daylight giving the glass a faint opalescent shimmer. Hector leaned on the cue just so, a practiced elegance, but the strain around his eyes betrayed his supposedly easy posture.

"Your whereabouts at the critical time—remind me, Mr. Gaunt?" Montague's voice was cool, the question offered as if in passing, though his gaze did not waver. Hector set the cue down beside the scoring board, the tip making a soft thud that echoed faintly in the closed space.

"Billiard room. Two fifty to three twenty five." Hector's answer was a flat recitation, the words shaped by dry wit and resignation. Montague chalked his own cue, the blue dust clinging to his fingers as he waited for more.

The windows rattled as a late breeze chased itself down the corridor. Neville Lachlan entered, his limp more pronounced after the day's exertions, his walking stick flicking a steady rhythm against the floorboards. He paused beside an armchair, adjusting the battered cushion with one hand before lowering himself carefully, the act betraying the old injury that never quite let him forget its presence. His gaze swept the room, taking in the arrangement of cues, the positions of the balls—every detail marked by habit born of years at the academy.

"Fencing hall, three o'clock to a quarter to four," Neville said before being asked, voice level. He unscrewed the cap of his flask, the metal cold in his palm as he poured a measure that caught the lamp's light on its way into the cup.

"Four witnesses confirm it," Bertram Vance called from the far end of the room, where he stood sorting through a box of spare chalks with idle precision. He tossed one to Montague, who caught it with a practiced hand. Bertram's face was composed, each movement theatrical, as if the role of outsider demanded a performance at every turn.

Montague marked his notebook, the pen scraping softly as he wrote down the time blocks: "three o'clock to a quarter to four, confirmed for Lachlan." He glanced at Hector, who unbuttoned his waistcoat with slow, deliberate movements, revealing nothing more than the shirt beneath.

"Your own time, Vance?" the detective asked.

"Ten minutes past three to twenty minutes to four, in my quarters." Bertram's response was crisp, the edge of polite savagery evident in the clipped phrasing. He tapped the box closed, the click sharp. "Staff saw me there. I am, regrettably, accounted for."

Neville shifted, his knee protesting as he crossed his leg. The sound of the joint—soft, but persistent—was a reminder of old wounds that needed no rehearsal. He spun his flask between his palms, the gesture habitual, and Montague's attention drifted to the way the silver reflected the lamplight.

"To summarize," Montague said, "Mr. Gaunt: Two fifty to three twenty five, billiard room. Mr. Lachlan: three o'clock to a quarter to four, fencing hall. Mr. Vance: ten minutes past three to twenty minutes to four, in quarters, with multiple confirmations." He closed the notebook with a soft snap, the decision as measured as a verdict read from the bench.

"Not a single one of us could have reached the cave in time, then?" Hector's voice was dry, a note of challenge lurking beneath the surface. Montague leaned on his cue, considering the pattern of the balls.

"I'll warrant it," Montague replied.

Neville rolled the flask between his hands, his manner reflective as he observed the interplay. Bertram set down the chalks and picked up a billiard ball, thumb tracing the number inlaid along its surface, the simple act filling the pause that followed.

"You have a theory to share?" Bertram asked, his voice measured and his gaze fixed on Montague, though the question was for the room. Hector did not answer at first, instead polishing the cue with a cloth as if the world depended on a spotless finish.

After half a minute, he spoke. "If we take it as settled that the weapon was found in her own room, and the window could only be accessed at twenty minutes past three, then my presence here, in full view, precludes me from the drama below. As does his," he nodded to Neville, "and his," a glance at Bertram, "unless the walls have learned to lie."

"Quite the predicament," Montague said, his eyes resting on the billiard table. The others exchanged looks, the joke passing like a ripple across the surface of uneasy alliance.

Bertram, never content to let silence reign, took up the thread with a speech that ran long enough for every cue in the rack to be handled at least once. "Consider the irony, gentlemen. The very people most in need of exoneration have the firmest alibis, marked and logged to the quarter hour. Not that anyone's likely to mistake me for a Quayle loyalist, but I do confess to a certain relief in being counted out—by time or by tide. Still, it does rob the proceedings of their proper edge. If the murderer is not in this room, he may well be in another altogether, sipping brandy and congratulating himself on navigating both the tides and the traditions of our little world. What's left for the rest of us, save to polish our reputations and wait for the next scandal?"

"Leave the polishing," Hector replied, smoothing the baize with the flat of his palm, "to the professionals."

Neville, silent for a time, drew the cue ball towards him and spun it gently across the table's edge. "That's a fair observation," he said, the words quiet but clear. His eyes met Montague's for a moment, and the detective nodded, accepting the old coach's assent as the mark of a man who had survived more than one harrowing season.

A noise from the hall—a maid's cart rattling by—reminded them all that the world outside continued its slow, indifferent progress. Montague traced a finger along the edge of the billiard table, eyes following the grain of the wood, the touch grounding him as the conversation turned.

"Mr. Gaunt, you and Miss Quayle," Montague began, "had a disagreement in the spring—her will was revised to remove you as heir?"

"Correct," Hector replied, his tone so flat it invited no elaboration. Montague made another note, the page filling with the lines of a case that seemed, for the moment, to have closed itself.

Neville rose, using his cane for leverage, the effort plain in the set of his jaw. He crossed to the sideboard and poured a measure of whisky, the bottle cool in his hand, then set it before Montague with a nod. The glass caught the light and fractured it, scattering small rainbows across the surface of the table.

"You haven't asked after Miss Quayle herself," Neville said, his tone almost an accusation. The silence that followed was not quite awkward, but it was dense with things unsaid. Montague accepted the glass.

"She was as constant as the tide," Montague said, looking into the amber depths. "And almost as difficult to direct."

Bertram snorted, rolling a ball between his thumb and forefinger. "If only the tide could be bribed, or the window swum through on a whim."

"No window to that," Neville observed, setting his glass aside with a decisive clink.

The clock in the corridor chimed three times—a reminder, as ever, of the relentless progress of time in a house that measured its fortunes by the hour. Hector watched the minute hand move, his expression unreadable, and Bertram tucked the chalk box under his arm as if it were a purse full of secrets.

The door to the fencing hall stood ajar, the smell of oiled wood and canvas wafting through as the evening crowd departed. Montague made his way there, the others following in silent procession. The hall was empty, the racked foils gleaming in their places, every mask and gauntlet hung with military precision. Neville paused to adjust a mask strap that had slipped loose, his hand lingering on the leather, the touch gentle and almost affectionate.

"Whatever else happens, the record is clear," Montague said. "Each man in his place, each hour accounted for. The rest is method and means."

Bertram, at last, could only reply, "Well, that's a fine mess." He offered up the words with a wry smile, letting them hang in the space between the lines of polished floor and the shadows that stretched along the wall.

And for a moment, the only sound was the echo of footsteps and the faint, persistent ticking of the clock in the empty corridor, marking the alibis of men who, by all appearances, could never have been at the scene—and yet, the murder had been done, leaving the answer hovering just out of reach, like the last ball refusing to drop.

## Chapter 7: Lies and Distractions

"The law is a matter of timing, Mr. Gaunt," Montague Norbury said, his hand steadying his fountain pen as he surveyed the small cluster of men in the hotel's walnut-paneled reading room. The low lamplight flickered across the surface of the polished chessboard at his elbow, pieces set in a position that suggested a match abandoned mid-contest. Hector Gaunt pressed two fingers to the back of a velvet armchair, his posture defensive but composed, a shadow thrown behind him by the gilded sconce above.

"Why not ask the real question?" Hector replied, his words clipped, eyes fixed on the edge of the desk. Neville Lachlan shifted his weight by the bookshelves, his cane resting at a lean against his thigh, and in the dimness, the silver-tipped stick caught the light as if it, too, was making an argument.

Montague slid a sheaf of correspondence across the desk, the crackle of paper drawing all eyes. "I have before me notes and letters, Mr. Kestrel, with your hand upon them. Please account for the date and the intent." Desmond Kestrel moved forward, his steps even on the carpet, and reached for the topmost envelope, turning it over in his palm with lawyerly detachment.

"Routine estate business," Desmond said, voice flat as he met Montague's gaze. The long, thin envelope bore his own wax seal, yet he did not break it, letting the others see the formal signature: D. Kestrel, Attorney.

Bertram Vance, half-sunk in a leather armchair, thumbed the edge of a battered deck of playing cards, the cards fanned and squared, never shuffled. The scent of pipe tobacco lingered about him, and a single chip—kept as a reminder—pressed a groove into his palm.

Neville, expression unreadable, stepped forward and rested his hand on the carved mantel. "Your argument stands," Neville said, the quiet observation punctuated by the soft tap of his cane against the marble.

"Would you care to explain why your personal correspondence references the tide chart, the duplicate key, and a payment from Mr. Gaunt?" Montague's words were unhurried but sharp as a foil's point. The pen in his hand tapped once, twice, against the desk.

Desmond did not blink. "Legal retainer, nothing more," he said, his tone level, but his knuckles paled as he set the unopened letter aside.

Hector answered the next, his voice more brittle: "Nothing more." He shifted his grip to the back of the chair, and Montague's gaze flicked to the glint of the ring on Hector's little finger.

"You want to explain that, Gaunt?" Neville asked, eyes fixed on the narrow gap between Hector's words and his stance. Hector's reply came short and flat: "I cannot." Bertram's mouth twitched, and he set the deck of cards down atop the hotel's ledger, as if casting a wager.

Montague withdrew a pair of gloves from his breast pocket and laid them beside the fencing foil, which had been brought for further inspection. In the hush, he picked up the weapon, turning it until the lamplight revealed the fresh stains on the guard, distinct from the older marks. He matched the pattern to a tracing on the blotter—Desmond Kestrel's own glove print, as recently confirmed by comparison.

Desmond's jaw tightened, his eyes tracing the dull sheen of the blade. "According to the records," he offered, but the words had lost some of their earlier composure. Montague let the foil rest against the chessboard, its tip coming to a soft halt between two pawns.

Neville bent to study the foil, his hand steady despite the day's fatigue. He spoke, his voice carrying the dry, observational humour honed by decades of fencing halls and missed opportunities. "Funny thing about fingerprints—they don't tell you when, only who. But the stains are fresh enough to hold the tide, and the grip's still yours, Kestrel. You can't argue with the tide—ten minutes, and the story writes itself, if you know how to read it."

Desmond's answer was a whisper, almost lost: "You're mistaken." Neville did not move, only shifted his cane so that it tapped twice against the floorboards.

Bertram, flipping a card face up and then face down, said, "Well, that's a fine mess. I trust the new management will be more generous with tips." He kept his attention fixed on the cards, sliding a glance at Hector, whose lips pressed into a thin line.

Montague adjusted his spectacles, the gesture deliberate as he returned to the stack of correspondence. The top note, written in a slanted hand, was half-concealed by a hotel bill—Desmond's expense account, annotated with times and references to "access" and "convincing delay." The evidence was of a kind that weighed more heavily than blood or stains.

Hector's hand trembled as he picked up a glass from the drinks tray, swirling the amber liquid inside. He spoke at length, the words tumbling out as if rehearsed for the court of public opinion: "If you must know, I arranged for the will to be delayed—yes, I paid Kestrel, because I stood to lose everything, and the scandal had already cost me any claim to pride. But I did not kill her. I could not have—my alibi was written and witnessed at the hotel, as you yourselves confirmed. All I wanted was a chance to revisit the ledger, to correct the unpaid entries and balance the accounts, even if only by degrees. My mistake was trusting that time could be bought, like so much else."

"Quite the predicament," Neville said, his reply succinct, as he stooped to pick up a stray chess piece from the carpet and set it on the board.

Desmond opened his attaché and withdrew a notebook, its edges worn but the pages inside crisp. He placed it, with steady hands, before Montague. "You'll find the correspondence there," he said quietly, deadpan as ever, "and the entries for the twenty minutes past three to half past three, as per the documentation."

Montague turned to the entry for the critical time. The ink was fresh, the lines clear: "Tide at lowest—access established. Duplicate key in place. Await direction per H.G." He let the others read, his finger following the neat script.

Neville's gaze lingered on Desmond, then moved to Hector. "The story's in the gaps," he remarked, his tone gentle but unyielding. "You lied about the money, Gaunt—nothing more. But the rest... the rest is action, not words."

Hector, silent now, stared into his glass as the whisky's surface stilled. A single drop fell to the floor, and he did not move to wipe it away.

Bertram, having lost or won no more than a coin toss, gathered his cards and began to shuffle them with the practiced grace of an outsider whose luck had failed once too often. He did not speak, but shuffled the deck with a slow, deliberate motion that drew a sidelong glance from Montague.

Montague rested his hand on the foil, then closed the notebook with a soft, precise click. "The next test will settle both means and motive," he said, his voice final as the closing of a case. "Until then, you will remain within call, Mr. Kestrel."

The grandfather clock in the hall struck once, low and resonant, as the men drifted to their corners—Neville testing the support of his cane, Hector pocketing an unpaid bill, Bertram flicking the cards one last time, and Desmond folding his hands atop his lapels as if the night's work were only half done.

The lamplight, never quite steady, glinted off the silvered edge of the chess clock as Montague wound it for the next match. In the hush that followed, the turning of the key was the only thing that counted—marking the minutes, and the lies, until the tide came again.

## Chapter 8: The Tide Unmasks

"Hold steady," Montague Norbury intoned, his hand pressed flat against the rain-chilled window frame of Katherine Quayle's room. He tilted his head toward the sound of the sea below, listening for the rhythm that had set the case's clock as surely as any alibi. The afternoon light was crisp, the kind that sharpens every surface: the battered trunk at the foot of the bed, the battered fencing foil on the coverlet, the glint of water far below as the tide retreated. From the open sash, a salt wind carried the briny promise that, for ten minutes at least, all secrets led seaward.

Neville Lachlan stood by the threshold, cane braced, eyes fixed on the battered tide chart Montague had nailed to the wall with a pin. Its red mark circled twenty minutes past three—a detail that had, until now, meant little more than the moment when habit became opportunity. Hector Gaunt fidgeted with the catch of his watch chain, the metal ticking against his palm as he watched Desmond Kestrel adjust his cufflinks. Bertram Vance lingered in the corner, both hands on the curved back of the writing chair, his attention divided between the detective's movements and the pale gleam of foam on the rocks below.

Montague gestured to the window. "We will settle the matter now," he said, his cadence clipped as a naval order. "The cave is open, as the tide chart predicted—twenty minutes past three. Kestrel, Gaunt—time to show us how it's done." Desmond's eyes narrowed, and Hector's mouth bent in a faint smile that showed more nerves than mirth. Montague flipped open his notebook, the paper crinkling as he ran his thumb along the edge, recording the time as the distant bell in the corridor chimed the half hour.

"A tangled situation," Hector said, smoothing the lapel of his jacket with two fingers. The movement was automatic, revealing nothing; he pressed his back to the wall, tension visible in the set of his jaw. Bertram rolled a pencil between his fingers, the gesture careless as he asked, "Who's first, then?" His voice was light but the undertone was acid—polite savagery for the occasion.

Desmond's reply was short. "I'll go," he said, and Bertram's gaze flicked from the solicitor to the open window, before returning to the group with a half-shrug.

Montague produced the fencing foil from its resting place and laid it across the narrow sill, the blade's length pointing like an accusation toward the cave below. "Observe, gentlemen," he said, "the weapon used to kill Miss Quayle was not only found in her room with fresh stains and distinct handling marks, but those marks match precisely the grip of Desmond Kestrel's gloved hand." He paused, the silence thickening as Desmond's fingers twitched, then stilled.

Bertram spoke at length, his words delivered with the assurance of a man who had long since accepted the role of outsider: "Gentlemen, we stand on the brink of revelation and the edge of the tide. I've seen great men undone by lesser evidence than this—though never with quite so little ceremony as today. Our host lies dead, our traditions bloodied, and all that's left is to see which of us can fit through a window and into history. I'll wager the sea is less forgiving than the law, but perhaps only just. If the demonstration fails, I suppose we'll have to find a taller suspect, or a more obliging tide. Either way, the rest of us remain in the audience, watching the pageant unwind with all the grace of a poorly mounted attack."

"Save your wager," Desmond answered, gripping the window frame.

Montague nodded to Hector, who approached the sash and eyed the rocks below, evaluating the drop and the angle. He set his hands on the sill, glanced at the open blueprint on the chair, and then looked back to Montague. "I could reach it, given another ten years and less weight on my conscience," he said, the irony biting, "but the record will show: at twenty minutes past three, I was in the billiard room, as confirmed by three witnesses. I have neither the means nor the motive on this account."

"Let's have it, then," Neville said, his gaze fixed on Desmond, who removed his jacket with a lawyer's precision and rolled his sleeves. The air was thick with the scent of salt and old brass. The group crowded around the window as Desmond swung a leg over the sill, gripping the frame with both hands and lowering himself with care onto the narrow ledge above the rocks. Below, the cave gaped black and wet, its mouth open in invitation or accusation, depending on the reading. Bertram knelt to check the strength of the window latch, the movement so practiced it drew a smile from Neville, whose eyes lingered on the battered carpet.

Desmond balanced, then moved, dropping the last few feet to the rocky shelf below. He vanished into the cave's mouth, the others watching in silence as his shadow was swallowed by the darkness. Montague checked his watch against the tide chart, the interval precise: "From twenty minutes past three to half past three—ten minutes, and no more, to cross the cave, enter, and exit unseen."

Inside, Neville adjusted his grip on his cane, and Hector tucked his hands behind his back, eyes following the last ripple of movement as Desmond disappeared into the corridor beneath the hotel. Bertram pushed himself upright, brushing the dust from his knees, his palms leaving faint marks on his trousers. "Some men will do anything for a client," he said, voice low. "Or, failing that, for a cause that pays out better than most."

The silence lasted until Montague gestured for the others to step back, making room as Desmond reappeared at the door to the victim's room, his shoes damp, fingers curled around the duplicate key he had withdrawn from a hollow fencing foil—the very one now resting on the sill. He placed the weapon on the table, the sound sharp in the hush, and met Montague's gaze without blinking.

Montague turned to the group. "Gentlemen, you have seen it: Desmond Kestrel alone could have entered through the tidal cave at low tide, using the duplicate key concealed in the fencing foil to open the window, and killed Katherine Quayle between twenty minutes past three and half past three." He looked directly at Desmond. "Desmond Kestrel killed Katherine Quayle." The words lingered in the air, solid as stone.

Desmond's voice was flat but clear; his eyes found the battered blueprint, and his knuckles whitened on the edge of the table as he spoke each word with the dread of a man used to verdicts. "She was about to remove me as executor, and unmask the delay I orchestrated at Hector's request." He placed a hand on the blueprints, steadying himself. "Losing the Quayle Academy would have left me with nothing but my reputation, and that was already battered in court."

Montague, not yet finished, completed the test for the record. "The killer entered through the cave at low tide, used the duplicate key hidden in the foil to unlock the window, and returned by the same route—between twenty minutes past three and half past three, the only window when the way was open." The group was silent, the proof laid out with nowhere left to run.

Bertram leaned against the desk, his voice gentle for once. "Some tides turn too quickly to outrun, Kestrel." The words were delivered with a faint regret, but he did not look away.

Desmond's hands, steady now, closed the hollow foil with a click. He did not protest further. Outside, the wind lifted, rattling the sash as the sea began to rise once more—sealing the cave, and with it, the last hope of escape.

Montague Norbury, still at the window, closed his notebook with finality. The case, as the tide, had turned. And the room, which had borne witness to so much concealment, now gave up the last of its secrets to the afternoon light.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

"All evidence has been weighed and the facts are now beyond reasonable dispute," Montague Norbury announced, his voice carrying just enough to command the attention of the small group assembled in the hotel lounge. He rested one hand on the leather armrest of his chair, the other fidgeting with the handle of his cane—a permanent tremor making the simple act more deliberate than it might otherwise have been. The late afternoon sun cut long slats of light across the carpet, catching on the gleam of the brass fire screen and the faint blue smoke from Bertram Vance's fresh-lit cigarette. At the far side of the room, the battered valise that had carried Katherine Quayle's last change of clothes now sat unclaimed, its handle turning idly beneath Neville Lachlan's absent hand.

Bertram, perched on the edge of a tufted settee, exhaled a swirl of smoke and flicked ash into a crystal tray. "Well, that's a fine mess, but at least it's no longer mine to tidy," he said, polite savagery brightening his tone for the others to hear. He leaned back, the tip of his shoe brushing the base of a brass floor lamp, as if to confirm through contact that the world beyond suspicion was solid once more.

Montague nodded, his gaze settling on Hector Gaunt. "Mr. Gaunt, the record and all witness accounts clear you. At two fifty, you entered the billiard room, departing at three twenty five. Not a soul disputes your presence, nor does the evidence allow you to enter or leave Miss Quayle's room by any means, conventional or otherwise." He paused, letting the words settle. "You are not the man we seek."

Hector received the verdict without visible relief, his hands drumming restlessly against the arm of his chair. He stared at the window, where the sky had paled to a muted blue, and let out a breath he had been holding since the morning. "I offer no apologies, only resignation," he said, dry wit failing him at the last. He pressed his palms together, their contact echoing the faint click of billiard balls in the distant, empty room.

Neville Lachlan, cane propped against the fireplace, gazed at the dying embers with an expression carved by seasons of both victory and disappointment. The battered knee that had marked his career was now rested atop a footstool, the gray wool of his trouser leg creased sharply. "I thank you for clearing my name, Norbury," he said, his tone a shade more formal than usual. He reached for the silver flask on the mantel, using its weight to anchor himself to the moment rather than the memory of accusation.

Desmond Kestrel stood by the drinks cabinet, his hand wrapped around a tumbler filled with nothing more than water. He listened as Montague continued, the words scattering tension rather than collecting it. "Mr. Vance, your station and movements from ten minutes past three to twenty minutes to four are confirmed by multiple staff and guest testimony. You are exonerated by the clock and by those who saw you, whether you knew it or not."

Bertram set his glass aside with a click, meeting Montague's eyes. "Resentment, perhaps, but not guilt," he said, his voice more subdued than usual, and the room seemed to accept the distinction with only the faintest ripple of unease. He ran his finger around the rim of his glass, the gesture both habitual and grounding.

"For the record," Montague went on, "Mr. Lachlan, you are cleared by the testimony of all present in the fencing hall, including those with no affection for either your pension or your position. Your colleagues recall you fencing with Miss Quayle in the hall, your mask never removed, for the entire ten minutes when the crime could have been done. The glove mark on the foil is explained by yesterday's lesson, the rest by routine."

Neville inclined his head, smoothing the nap of the armchair beneath his fingers. "'I see that plainly,' he said, a soft observational humour rounding the edges of an admission that might have chafed another man's pride. He picked up a matchbook from the table, turning it in his palm as if weighing the value of the small things that remained secure.

Montague paused, letting the silence fill with the muted sounds of the lounge: the clink of glass, the shuffle of cards in Bertram's restless hands, the distant cough of a porter in the corridor. At the centre of the carpet, Katherine Quayle's fencing mask rested where it had been set down, the mesh cool to the touch and untouched since the morning. Hector bent to retrieve it, turning the mask so the initials 'K.Q.', stitched into the inner lining, faced the group—a silent reminder of the discipline she'd instilled. He set it upright on the table, a gesture half in reverence, half in farewell.

"Desmond Kestrel, you have already confessed the deed and the motive, and the law will find you accordingly," Montague said, his tone measured, a judge reading out the inevitable. Desmond's lips barely moved, but the answer came—deadpan as ever—"'As the papers show,' Desmond replied." Montague's hand trembled on the cane, recording the final note in a case grown cold.

Neville, leaning forward, spoke at length, his words shaped by reflection and the need to make peace. "In the years I spent at this academy, I believed rivalry sharpened us. But suspicion blunts more than it hones. I am sorry for the hard things I said and the harder things I thought. If I keep a careful hand now, it's because time has taught me, not only this trial. I hope we may find some measure of trust in the clean sweep of daylight, now that it's come."

"Accepted," Desmond answered, his hand steady on the glass.

Montague glanced at Hector Gaunt, who regarded the room with the weary composure of a man unburdened of suspicion yet not of consequence. "You are free to go," Montague said, watching as Hector rose, crossed to the window, and pressed his forehead once against the cold glass. He stood there a moment, the city and the sea beyond reflected in the pane, before returning to the group with nothing more than a nod.

Bertram replaced the pack of cards in his breast pocket, the gesture careful, and looked at Neville. "Whatever else you may think of outsiders, at least we make dependable witnesses," he said, and this time the barb was dulled by the relief that hung in the room. Neville accepted the remark with the lift of an eyebrow, the silence between them an old, comfortable sort.

Desmond shifted, his hand returning to the briefcase he had not yet relinquished. He watched the others—one by one—as if counting them, or perhaps measuring the distance between what had been lost and what might still be carried forward. Montague closed the case file with a snap, sliding the folder into Desmond's waiting hands, its weight now a matter for the law.

"Mr. Norbury," Bertram called, his tone lighter. "May I speak freely?" Montague, hand resting atop Katherine's battered valise, offered a silent gesture of assent. Bertram's next words sketched the outline of an apology—more to the room than to any one man. "I have sometimes presumed more than my standing allowed. Let this be a lesson to me: not all entrances are open, nor all debts enforceable."

"Noted," Montague replied, turning at last to the battered mask. He lifted it, running his fingers over the wire, a tangible link to the discipline and order Katherine Quayle had insisted upon all her life.

In the quiet that followed, the survivors each found some small thing to handle: Neville folded the matchbook and slipped it into his pocket; Hector adjusted the cuffs of his jacket by the fire; Bertram returned his glass to the tray; Desmond closed his briefcase with a click. Montague drew his thumb along the knotted cane, the tremor in his hand the only movement left.

Near the door, the late sun caught the gilded letters of Katherine's nameplate—one last, silent witness to the lives she had shaped, and the order she had enforced, long after the contest had ended. The lounge, its rituals resumed, awaited the first call to tea, and for a moment, ordinary life pressed in on the edges, filling the gaps left by suspicion and fear. The room was returned to its living, and the dead—remembered only by the things they left behind.

## Chapter 10: The Last Routine

"On course, then," Montague Norbury said, laying the evening edition of the Chronicle on the hotel lounge's marble-topped table. He nodded to the barman for a glass of port, his fingers lingering on the rim of the glass as the world pressed forward, the case concluded, the tide once more indifferent to human affairs. The lounge had returned to itself in the aftermath—a low murmur of guests, the muted clink of glass, the faint brush of cards shuffled by Bertram Vance in his corner. The proof, settled the previous day with a tidal demonstration and a signed confession, hung over the room only as a memory, no longer a presence.

The velvet curtains, drawn half across the French windows, caught the last light of sunset and held it, teasing out the gold from the sea's blue. Hector Gaunt, standing at the window, straightened the lapel of his jacket with care, the gesture oddly practiced after weeks of avoiding the public eye. In the soft evening, he reached for a volume from the hotel's shelf—a battered copy of Maugham's stories—and, instead of retreating to his room, seated himself at a table in plain view of the other guests.

Bertram Vance skimmed a deck of cards across the table, the polished surface sending the top card sailing into the air, where it landed face-up—an accident, perhaps, though his eyes flickered to Hector, measuring the response. Neville Lachlan, nearby, adjusted a cushion at his lower back with a grunt, then pressed the silver-tipped cane into the pattern of the carpet as he settled into an armchair by the fire. The flames cast his profile in copper, highlighting the years that had settled into the curve of his mouth and the set of his good knee.

"Will you stay in town, Gaunt?" Montague asked, leafing past the society column with a dry smile.

"No plans," Hector replied, the answer clipped to four words. Montague refolded the paper and reached for his port, watching as the barman wiped a glass behind the counter with a towel—an ordinary gesture, no different from any other evening.

Hector marked his place in the book with a postcard, then tapped the table once for emphasis. He drew in a slow breath, the lines of disgrace softening into the ease of a man newly returned to the world's daylight. Without the shadow of accusation, his posture shifted incrementally—shoulders less tight, gaze less guarded—as if the simple act of sitting among the living were more difficult, and more meaningful, than any duel.

Neville lifted his glass to the fireplace, turning the cut crystal between his hands. "Reckon you'll take up teaching again?" he asked, the edge of observation smoothing the awkwardness. The fire popped, and he added, "Less running, more pointing," with a wry glance at his own battered knee.

"Perhaps. Or perhaps not," Hector said, the line minimal but final. Bertram placed the jack of spades facedown, a single knuckle rapping the table.

Montague, meanwhile, repositioned the heavy hotel ledger at the edge of the desk, sliding his pen between the pages where the new names had begun to appear—guests, not suspects, for the first time in days. Montague paused, surveying the neat columns of names and check-in times, lined up as precisely as any fencing bout. The tremor in his left hand was more pronounced than usual, perhaps from fatigue or the ebb of tension, but he pressed his palm to the smooth wood, anchoring himself against the day's events.

"Quite the predicament, Norbury, being out of work now the dust has settled," Bertram offered, his voice stretching to fill the space between jokes and relief, the polite savagery blunted by a look at Montague's thinning hair.

Montague, amused, replied with a speech of his own, his tone measured and laced with dry wit: "Predicament? Not quite. I find the aftermath of a solved mystery much like the calm after a well-fought storm—a chance to take stock, to mend sails and tally losses, and then to make small repairs to the order of things. Some men thrive in chaos, but I prefer the simple pleasure of restored routine—the lamp lit, the ledger closed, glasses stacked—and the knowledge, for now, that the world is as it ought to be. There are reports to file, letters to write, and, if I am lucky, a quiet evening or two with my own memories for company. The real work, I find, is ensuring that the right lessons are remembered and the wrong ones forgotten." He sipped his port, waiting for Bertram's reply.

"Send me a postcard," Bertram answered, fanning the deck.

Neville, his gaze fixed on the shifting glow in the fireplace, spoke up. "Fair enough," he murmured, his hand lingering on the carved lion's head of his cane as the firelight flickered over his face.

Montague made a note in his calendar, a small, deliberate act that seemed to reset the room's rhythm. He would remain in residence—at least for another week. Already, the academy had telephoned: a request for his guidance in revising the legal protocols for the fencing trust, to ensure no more duplicate keys found their way into hollow weapons. It was a concrete change, and he felt the satisfaction of a man whose talents were wanted for repair, not accusation.

Hector, too, had changed—less in stance than in intent. A local club's secretary stopped beside Hector, handing him a folded note and nodding, eager to have his name on their rolls, if not their letterhead. The club's secretary, passing through the lounge, pressed a note into Hector's hand; a meeting was set for the following Thursday. The offer was modest, but the look in Hector's eyes, as he folded the note, was that of a man restored to the possibility of being useful.

Across the lounge, Bertram stifled a yawn behind his hand and reached for the sherry decanter, pouring a measure with the competence of a man accustomed to pouring his own. The glass glinted, and for a moment, the memory of Katherine Quayle was present—not in the echo of her discipline, but in the way she had once corrected Bertram's grip on a decanter at a summer dinner, her remark understated and precise: "As it should be, Vance, though I would have preferred less theatricality in your pouring, if not your fencing."

Neville pressed the ledger closed, his fingers smoothing the crest embossed on its cover. The routine of the academy had already resumed—schedules posted, lessons planned, the scent of oiled leather and chalk dust drifting down the hallways as if nothing had ever disturbed them. In the common room, the battered foil, polished clean, was returned to the rack with the rest. The fencing mask, no longer a relic of suspicion, hung in its proper place near the door.

Montague Norbury, his work concluded, signed his name in the hotel's register, his hand steadying as he wrote. The hotel lounge, returned at last to ordinary use, hummed quietly—the clack of billiard balls, the muted laughter from the bar, the distant call of a porter announcing the evening post. It was, in the simplest sense, a room at peace, the contest resolved, the story closed.
