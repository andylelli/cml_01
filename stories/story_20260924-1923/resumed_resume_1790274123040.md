"If there is anything further," Neville Lachlan began, but Montague Norbury shook his head. "No. The matter is settled. The authorities will be notified, and the evidence delivered. "Sometimes the smallest misstep can turn the tide against you," he said, the words carrying a weight beyond the immediate case. He limped toward the door, pausing only to glance back at Hector Gaunt, whose own gaze never left the window. Bertram Vance hesitated, then followed, his steps unsteady.

For a moment, Montague Norbury considered speaking-offering some word of comfort or condemnation-but found nothing that would not sound hollow. In the hush that followed, the only sound was the distant echo of the sea, relentless and indifferent, as the men of the Mariner's Rest drifted apart, each carrying the weight of what had been lost and what had been spared.

## Chapter 10: What Remains After the Tide

The sound of foghorns drifted through the half-open window, muffled by the thick midsummer haze that pressed against the glass of the Hotel lounge. Gaslight flickered in the dimness, casting long, uncertain shadows over the men gathered there. Desmond Kestrel was gone-removed to the custody of the authorities, the confession he had signed already a matter for the coastal magistrate. The lounge, so often alive with rivalry and laughter, now held only the subdued hush of men reckoning with what remained. Montague Norbury stood by the hearth, the warmth barely reaching his hands, his notebook closed and set aside at last. The truth, so long withheld, had left a residue that neither the fog nor the fire could quite dispel. "So it ends," Neville Lachlan said, his voice quiet as he lowered himself into the faded settee.

The dampness from the fog still clung to his trousers, and he winced as his injured leg stretched out stiffly before him. He stared at the empty grate, the embers long since gone cold. "We held the line as best we could. Yet it seems the parry was always doomed to fail." Hector Gaunt, standing by the drinks tray, poured a measure of whisky with a hand that trembled only slightly. He did not offer it to anyone else, nor did he drink. Instead, he set the glass down and regarded the room as if seeing it for the first time. "He was desperate," Hector Gaunt said, not looking at anyone in particular. "Desmond Kestrel believed he owed me more than he owed himself. That was his first mistake." The words were clipped, but the bitterness behind them was unmistakable.

For all his polished veneer, Hector Gaunt seemed smaller now-diminished not by suspicion, but by the knowledge that his own schemes had set the final act in motion. "You might have found a better champion," Bertram Vance observed, his tone perfectly civil as he flicked a speck of ash into the empty grate. "But then, the best swordsmen rarely survive the first volley." "That will do, Mr. Vance," Montague Norbury said, the edge in his voice gentler than it might have been. He crossed to the window, drawing the heavy curtain aside to peer into the fog. The sea beyond was invisible, but the faint tang of salt and the distant pulse of waves reminded him that the world, at least, continued. "Justice is a blunt instrument, but it is the only one we possess." Desmond Kestrel's absence was a presence in itself.

The chair he had occupied was pushed back, the duplicate key left on the armrest, its brass dulled by the press of his hand. Montague Norbury picked it up, weighing it in his palm before setting it beside the cold lamp. He thought of the humiliation Kestrel had endured in court the previous year-the way he had stood, hands clasped so tightly behind his back that his knuckles whitened, refusing to meet the judge's gaze. That same posture had returned in the final hours, a man braced for a blow he knew he could not parry. "The academy will need new leadership," Neville Lachlan said, breaking the silence. "Katherine Quayle believed in tradition, but she also believed in renewal. I suppose that duty falls to me now." He did not sound triumphant; rather, the words carried the weight of a promise reluctantly inherited. He glanced once at Hector Gaunt, who gave no sign of protest.

"You've always been the better fencer," Hector Gaunt replied, his voice flat. He reached for the whisky glass, then thought better of it and left it untouched. "I have other matters to attend to." The answer was as close to a concession as he would ever offer. "If you're finished with the speeches," Bertram Vance began, launching into a rambling account of the hotel's declining tea service, the unreliability of the local telegraph, and the indignity of being suspected for so long. He gestured with a half-empty cigarette case, his words tumbling out in a rush. "One might hope, after all this, for a return to sensible hours and a hot meal that doesn't taste of boiled fog. But perhaps that's too much to expect from a place where secrets are served more readily than scones."

"You could always try the bakery in town," Bertram Vance finished, the six words slicing through his own speech and leaving the room in a brief, startled silence. Montague Norbury allowed himself a faint smile, then turned his attention to the practicalities. "The authorities will require statements. The duplicate key and the foil are secured. The guest registry will be returned to the desk." He moved to the sideboard, collecting the scattered correspondence and restoring it to its folder. The act felt oddly ceremonial-a small restoration of order in a world that had briefly spun out of control. A chambermaid entered quietly, gathering the empty glasses and righting a chair that had been knocked askew in the earlier tumult.

The scent of polish and damp wool followed her, and as she left, Montague Norbury noticed the way the routine of the hotel began to reassert itself: the fire was stoked, the lamps trimmed, the curtains drawn against the night. Life, such as it was, resumed its course. For a moment, Montague Norbury lingered by the window, remembering how Katherine Quayle used to insist on fresh flowers in the lounge every Monday, regardless of season or weather. There were none now; the vase stood empty, a small but telling absence. He wondered if anyone would remember to fill it next week, or if the memory of her presence would fade as quickly as the scent of lilies in the fog. "She always hated silence," Neville Lachlan said, almost to himself. "Said it was the enemy of progress." He stood, moving with care, and crossed to the door. "We will have to learn to speak again, I suppose."

"Some of us never stopped," Bertram Vance replied, the edge in his voice softened by fatigue. Montague Norbury gathered his notebook and hat, pausing at the threshold. "The tide will turn again," he said, voice low. "Let us hope the next brings less wreckage." He stepped into the corridor, the fog pressing close as the door swung quietly shut behind him. In the Hotel lounge, the last of the gaslight flickered, and the routine of the Mariner's Rest resumed-imperfect, altered, but enduring. The cost of truth lingered in the hush, as persistent as the midsummer fog beyond the glass.
