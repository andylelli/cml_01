It clinked, and the sound went further in the twilight than it had any right to. He did not look at Eleanor, and he did not look at Beatrice; he looked at the polished brass of the clock. His hand strayed near the winding key, fingers opening and closing, as though he could not decide whether it was himself or the timepiece that wanted steadying. Eleanor filed the gesture away. In the hours before Dr. Mallory Finch died he had kept clear of her - quit the lounge when she entered it, traded his usual bluster for a careful distance. Chance, one might have called it yesterday.

Beatrice took the edge of a velvet chair and balanced the notebook on her knee. "Let's have it, then," she said, brisk enough. "If you mean to accuse me, accuse me plainly. I've nothing to hide but a few unflattering adjectives. " Her pencil went tap, tap against the page, a rhythm that did not match the voice at all. Eleanor felt the room lean in on her. "Your alibi is strong, Miss Quill. Perhaps stronger than is comfortable. The staff place you at reception the whole evening - several of them, independently. And yet motive I cannot set aside so easily. Your pursuit of the story. Your rivalry with Dr. Finch. " Wind found the chimney and whistled in it. Hale shifted his weight and the parquet creaked under his boots. He cleared his throat, and it came out rough. "If it's motive you want, there's plenty to go round.

We all had something to lose. " Clipped, that; a man putting up a shutter. Eleanor turned to him and softened her voice, which made it worse for him rather than better. "You were seen by the clock, Captain. Alone. And you know its mechanism better than anyone here. Is there anything you would care to add? " His jaw set. "I tend the clock. Always have done. Part of keeping the place running. No crime in that. " His eyes went past her shoulder to the window and stayed there. "Look at the hands," Eleanor said. "The wear on them is uneven - more force in it than winding asks for. " She did not take her hand from the casing. "And the face reads ten minutes past eleven, which agrees with neither the guest log nor Mr. Vane's pocket watch. His watch said twenty-five past. " Fifteen minutes, and no one in the room troubled to say so aloud.

"The staff schedules and the logs are plain enough about who could reach this lounge in that window. It is not a long list. " She watched Beatrice as she said it. Beatrice's fingers closed hard on the pencil, but she did not look away. "If you're set on fitting me for the noose, Miss Voss, you'll want more than a motive and a timetable. The staff had me in their sight every minute of it. I'd have needed to be in two places at once. " A shaft of lamplight found the brass and picked out the faint grooves ringing the winding keyhole. Eleanor had noted them on the first morning and had not yet found an account of them that satisfied her. She glanced towards the Captain. He had drawn his hands behind his back and stood stiffer than before, a man making himself smaller in a room he had once dominated.

Two of the staff had spoken of his manner that day, unprompted - how he had gone quiet, how he had kept away from the doctor. Fear looked very like guilt from across a room. Eleanor had known cases where it was neither. Beatrice's dry laugh cut the tension short. "There's the scoop, isn't it. Everyone's got a motive, and hardly anybody's got the means. I'll leave it to your fine sense of justice, Miss Voss. " Lightly said. Her eyes did not match it. Eleanor answered with a wry look, because the irony deserved acknowledging. Motive she had in abundance; opportunity she had not, and every hour of the evening had a witness standing behind it. On paper the theory had held beautifully. In this room, with the woman herself two yards away, it would not stand up. The lounge cooled as the evening drew on, shadow gathering in the corners where the lamps did not reach.

Eleanor stayed at the mantel, turning the contradictions over. The clock insisting on its wrong hour. The logs, the schedules. The Captain's small betrayals of himself, and Beatrice's brittle poise. Every piece pointed somewhere, and no two pointed the same way. She thought of other cases, other certainties, and of what her certainty had cost once. To everyone else the matter was settled; the circle had closed neatly on Beatrice Quill. Eleanor could not make herself step inside it. Something was out of true - a detail she could not yet put a name to, wound tight as the mainspring behind that brass face. She looked a long while at the minute hand, at the grooves taking the lamplight, and decided she would look again in the morning, and question what everybody else had been content to accept. The solution had arrived, plausible and persuasive, and she would not sign her name to it.

Not while the clock kept its own counsel. Not while fear still moved in the eyes of a man who insisted he had nothing to hide.

## Chapter 7: Secrets and Lies

"You see this, Miss Voss? The minute hand's not sitting right," Captain Ivor Hale said, his voice low and rough as the wind rattled the Bluffside Hotel lounge windows. The room was steeped in shadow and the faint glow of lamplight, the air thick with the scent of old tobacco and a lingering metallic tang. Eleanor, standing close to the mantel, felt the cold brass beneath her fingertips as she angled the clock's face toward the light. The hour was late; outside, the storm-driven surf battered the cliffs, and the darkness pressed against the panes. She remembered the grooves catching the lamplight the night before, and now, as she peered closer, she saw it: the faintest abrasion, a telltale mark where the minute hand had been forced backward-recent, unmistakable, and not the work of routine winding.

The realization struck her with a chill sharper than the sea air: the clock had been rewound, and not by accident. A hush fell as Eleanor turned the key, feeling the resistance in the mechanism-a stiffness that bespoke recent disturbance. She paused, letting the silence settle. The others watched, their faces half-lit and wary. Beatrice Quill, perched on the edge of a velvet chair, tapped her pencil against her notebook with a staccato rhythm, her eyes flickering between Eleanor and the clock. Hugo Vane, arms folded, leaned in with a practiced nonchalance that did little to mask the tension in his jaw. Sylvia Trent hovered by the door, her hands clasped so tightly her knuckles shone pale in the dim light.

In that moment, Eleanor's mind raced through the implications: the clock's hands, once trusted, now revealed as unreliable witnesses-silently turned backward by fifty-five minutes during a brief, unnoticed interval. The lounge felt colder as Eleanor closed the glass face, the click of the latch sounding too loud in the hush. She drew a breath, steadying herself. "This clock," she said quietly, "has not kept its own time. " Her words hung in the air, and for a heartbeat no one moved. The prior theory-timelines anchored to the clock's authority-fell away, leaving only uncertainty. Hugo Vane broke the silence, voice brittle. "If the clock's been tampered with, then every alibi that leans on it is as thin as the fog outside. " He flicked open his pocket watch, the silver dial gleaming: "twenty-five minutes past eleven," he announced, pointedly ignoring the lounge clock's face.

The contradiction, once a curiosity, now became the fulcrum of suspicion. Beatrice's voice cut in, sharp as a snapped twig. "So we're all suspects again? Or only some of us? " She forced a laugh, but her eyes darted to Sylvia, then to Captain Ivor Hale. "I suppose it's convenient, isn't it, when the evidence bends to fit the story. I was at reception-everyone saw me. But if the clock's a liar, what else here can be trusted? " Her bravado rang hollow, and Eleanor caught the tremor in her hand as she scribbled a note. The journalist's ambition had always been her shield, but now it looked more like armor hastily donned. Sylvia Trent stepped forward, her voice barely above a whisper. "I checked the lounge before the body was found. The clock-" She faltered, glancing at Captain Ivor Hale. "It always showed ten minutes past eleven. I didn't question it.

I never have. " Her loyalty to the hotel, and to the Captain, was etched in every line of her posture, but now it warred with something new: fear. The cost of silence, Eleanor realized, was mounting. Sylvia's eyes lingered on the clock, and then on the Captain, as if searching for permission to speak the truth. Captain Ivor Hale stood rigid by the hearth, his face cast in shadow. For a moment, he looked every inch the naval officer-shoulders squared, chin set-but the mask slipped as Eleanor met his gaze. "I tend the clock," he said, the words heavy. "Always have. No one else knows its workings. " His hand hovered near the winding key, then dropped to his side. "If it's gone wrong, it's my fault. But I'd never-" He stopped, the sentence unfinished. The room held its breath. Eleanor watched him, noting the way his fingers curled and uncurled, a man caught between pride and dread.

Eleanor's mind reeled with the revised meaning of every clue. The guest log, timestamped at a quarter past ten, had seemed to anchor the evening-until the clock's authority was shattered. Hugo Vane's pocket watch, dismissed as a personal quirk, now emerged as the only reliable witness to time's passage. The clock, once the backbone of every alibi, was exposed as the manipulated instrument, its hands reset in silence. She saw now how each suspect's story shifted in the new light: Beatrice's alibi, ironclad but irrelevant; Sylvia's loyalty, a shield that had blinded her; Hugo's isolation, no longer suspicious but pitiable. Only Captain Ivor Hale remained with both the knowledge and the opportunity to alter the clock, his unique access now a liability.

A flicker of relief, almost comic in its timing, broke the tension-Hugo Vane, with a wry smile, quipped, "Well, if nothing else, at least my watch can finally claim superiority over hotel grandeur. " The others did not laugh, but the momentary levity loosened the room's grip, if only for a heartbeat. Eleanor allowed herself a thin smile. Even in the darkest hour, the ordinary absurdities of life persisted. The relief faded quickly as Eleanor pressed on. "We must reconsider everything," she said, her tone gentle but implacable. "If the clock's testimony cannot be trusted, the entire sequence of events must be re-examined. Captain Hale, you were alone here, with the means to access the mechanism. The evidence does not accuse, but it demands explanation. " The Captain's silence was answer enough.

Beatrice looked away, her bravado spent; Sylvia's eyes filled with tears she would not let fall. Hugo Vane stared at the clock, as if willing it to confess on his behalf. Eleanor felt the weight of betrayal settle on her shoulders-she had trusted these people, in her way, and now found herself adrift in a sea of secrets. The clues she had once assembled with confidence now shifted beneath her, each one recast in the new light of the clock's silent treachery. She looked at the minute hand, at the grooves catching the lamplight, and knew the solution was not yet in reach. But the path was clearer: the mantel clock, once the heart of the hotel's order, had become its greatest deception. And behind that deception, someone's desperate hand had moved-unseen, but not untraceable.

## Chapter 8: The Final Trap

By midnight, the Bluffside Hotel lounge was thick with anticipation, the fog outside pressing cold against the glass and dulling every lamp's glow to a mere suggestion of gold. The air was damp, laced with the scent of old tobacco and the sharper tang of brass. Eleanor Voss stood at the mantel, her gloved hand resting on the clock's casing. She let her gaze sweep the room-Beatrice quill balanced herself on the edge of a velvet chair, notebook balanced on her knee; Sylvia Trent, hands folded so tightly her knuckles gleamed; Hugo Vane, standing apart, his usual composure replaced by a wary stillness. Captain Ivor Hale lingered near the hearth, posture rigid, eyes shadowed. The silence was broken only by the distant echo of a door closing and the creak of leather as someone shifted their weight.

As Eleanor moved to the side of the mantel, her fingers brushed against a narrow seam in the paneling. She paused, curiosity piqued, and pressed gently. The panel gave way with a soft click, revealing a small cavity. Inside, she found a decorative brass letter opener, its surface marred by faint fingerprints and smudges. She held it up, the metal catching the dim light, and glanced around the room before carefully setting it aside atop the mantel. Eleanor's voice, when it came, was deliberate and steady. "We have relied on this clock," she began, "to anchor our understanding of the night Dr. Finch was killed. But the truth has hidden in plain sight. Tonight, we will test what the clock truly tells us. " She gestured for Hugo Vane to approach, and he did, producing his silver pocket watch. The dial gleamed: twenty-five minutes past eleven.

The lounge clock, unmoved, displayed ten minutes past eleven. The contradiction was stark, but Eleanor pressed on. She lifted the hotel guest log, its page open to the entry marked a quarter past ten. "This log records Mr. Vane's arrival at a quarter past ten. Yet the lounge clock insists it was ten minutes past eleven when the body was found. If the clock were honest, these times could not coexist. " Eleanor's hand hovered above the minute hand. "But if the clock's hands were turned backward-silently, deliberately-then every alibi built on its testimony collapses. " Before she could proceed, Eleanor turned to Beatrice Quill. "Miss Quill, your own account places you at reception for the entire evening. The staff schedule is clear, and multiple witnesses-several of whom I have questioned independently-attest to your presence at the desk from before ten until well after eleven.

The corridor log confirms no one saw you leave, and the hotel's routine was undisturbed. The timeline, as confirmed by the log and every staff statement, makes it impossible for you to have left reception, let alone reached the lounge unnoticed. " Beatrice's jaw tightened, but she met Eleanor's eye. "So I'm ruled out not for lack of motive, but because I was simply too visible," she said, her voice edged with something brittle. Eleanor nodded. "Your alibi holds because the evidence does not allow another possibility. " Next, Eleanor addressed Sylvia Trent. "Miss Trent, the service schedules and corridor logs place you in the staff wing for the entirety of the window in question. I have spoken with the kitchen porter and the housemaid on duty-both confirm seeing you at the linen cupboard and again at the kitchen hatch. There is not a moment unaccounted for.

The staff schedules, corroborated by witness statements, clear you completely. The timeline simply could not fit your presence in the lounge. " Sylvia's composure faltered, a tremor passing through her folded hands, but relief flickered in her eyes. Eleanor's gaze fell lastly on Hugo Vane. "Mr. Vane, your suite log shows you rang for tea at a time that places you in your room well before the murder, and the staff member who delivered your tray confirms you never left until after the commotion. The guest suite logs, along with the staff statement, rule you out. You could not have been here at the crucial moment-your alibi is as firm as the hotel's own records. " Hugo inclined his head, a shadow of his usual smile returning for a heartbeat, but his posture eased as if a burden had been lifted. Eleanor now turned to Captain Ivor Hale, her gaze unwavering.

"Captain, you alone possess the knowledge and opportunity to manipulate this clock. You have tended it for years-no one else would dare. " She pressed her gloved fingers to the glass, then, with careful precision, set the minute hand forward until it matched Hugo's pocket watch. The mechanism resisted, then yielded with a faint, grinding click-a sound that made Sylvia flinch and Beatrice draw a sharp breath. "Observe," Eleanor said, her voice low. "When the clock is advanced by fifty-five minutes, it aligns perfectly with the true time recorded on Mr. Vane's watch and the guest log. The only way the lounge clock could have shown ten minutes past eleven when it did is if someone rewound it backward by fifty-five minutes after a quarter past ten. " She let the silence settle, the implication rippling through the room. "This was not an accident.

It was a calculated act-one that only someone with access and expertise could accomplish. " Captain Ivor Hale's composure finally cracked. His jaw clenched, and for a moment he looked as if he might deny everything. But the evidence was too plain. "Aye," he said, voice rough as gravel, "I did it. I set the clock back. I thought-if I could just buy us time, keep the hotel out of scandal-" He faltered, shoulders sagging. "Dr. Finch threatened to expose what would ruin us all. I couldn't let her destroy what's left of this place, not after all these years. " Eleanor stepped forward, holding both the mantel clock and Hugo's pocket watch before the group. "Look here," she said, pointing first to the clock's hands frozen at ten minutes past eleven, then to the watch showing twenty-five minutes past eleven. "One tells us the murder happened at this time, the other insists it was later.

Both cannot be right. " She turned to Captain Hale. "And only one of you could have twisted the truth by turning the clock backward. " Eleanor carefully lifted the mantel clock from its place and, with deliberate movements, began to turn its minute hand backward right there in the lounge. The guests leaned in, watching the hands move silently counterclockwise, while Hugo held his pocket watch steady for all to see. The clock's face shifted from ten minutes past eleven back toward the earlier hour, matching the discrepancy they had uncovered. Beatrice whispered, "So it was possible all along. " Run again in front of them all, the test came out the same way twice; its result ruled out one theory and left the other standing, plain enough that the room needed only its own eyes to see it.

That left Captain Ivor Hale as the only person whose story still needed the discredited theory to be true. Eleanor's expression was grave, but not unkind. "You tried to protect the hotel, Captain, but you did so at a terrible cost. Dr. Finch is dead, and the truth cannot be wound back. " She stepped back from the mantel, her hand falling to her side. The clock, reset and honest at last, ticked on in the charged silence-a witness that could no longer be deceived. The lounge, once the heart of the hotel's order, now bore the scars of truth revealed. Outside, the fog pressed close, and inside, the cost of loyalty and the price of deception lingered in the air, unresolved as the night itself.

## Chapter 9: Clearing the Innocent

Lamplight threw long shadows across the velvet chairs of the Bluffside lounge. Night fog pressed at the windows, and the room held the cold smell of polished brass. Eleanor Voss stood at the mantel with one hand on the clock's glass, the mechanism behind it reset and honest at last, and nobody spoke. Beatrice quill balanced a steaming mug on the arm of a chair, notebook shut, fingers moving on the cover as though there were still something to write. Sylvia Trent had not come further than the doorway. Hugo Vane kept near the drinks cabinet, and his composure, so carefully maintained these three days, had worn through at the elbows. Nobody looked triumphant. They looked like people waiting for weather to break. "We have spent these hours in doubt," Eleanor said, and her voice cut the quiet cleanly.

"Each of you weighed not only for what you might have done, but for what you might have hidden. It is time to set the record straight. " She turned first to Beatrice, who lifted her chin to meet her. "Miss Quill. You were at reception, and more than one person says so. The staff schedule, the corridor logs, the statements of those on duty - they agree with each other. From before ten until well after the hour in question. There was no window in which you might have slipped away unseen. " Beatrice took her time answering, and the rapid wit she had wielded all week did not come. "So that's it. My alibi is my absolution. " The words came out brittle. She seemed smaller than she had at any point since her arrival. "I ought to thank you, Miss Voss. It's a strange sort of relief, though.

I chased the story so hard I never once considered I might become it. " She looked down, tracing the spine of the closed notebook with her thumb. "There's a cost, isn't there, to wanting the truth that badly? " Nobody answered her. Eleanor let the silence run its course before she turned to the doorway. "Miss Trent. The staff schedules and the corridor logs put you in the service wing for the whole of the window. I have spoken to the kitchen porter, and to the housemaid. He saw you at the linen cupboard; she saw you again at the kitchen hatch. There is no gap in it - not one minute in which you could have been standing in this room. " Sylvia's hands came apart, and she let out a breath she seemed to have been holding since dinner. "Thank you, Miss Voss. " The formality held, but something raw ran underneath it. "I only ever wanted the hotel to run.

To protect what little order was left to us. I thought loyalty would be enough. " Her glance went to the mantel clock, then to the patch of carpet where Captain Ivor Hale had stood earlier in the evening. "I see now that keeping quiet can do the same work as a lie. It is no easy thing, serving two masters - duty and conscience. Tonight I have learned which of them I must answer to. " Hugo Vane, who had kept to the edge of the lamplight, squared his cuffs with the ease of long practice, though the charm had gone flat in his voice. "Ruled out by virtue of isolation. There's a verdict. " The smile came and went. "The suite logs, and the girl who brought up my tea. Those are my witnesses, such as they are. I suppose there are worse fates than being left alone with oneself, but I shan't pretend it came cheap. " He stopped, and for a moment the veneer went entirely.

"There are things I've done I would rather not see aired in this room. Murder isn't among them. I'm obliged to you for seeing it, Miss Voss. " His eyes stayed on a beat longer than courtesy required. Eleanor took in all three of them. "You are cleared - not because I am fond of any of you, but because the paper and the people agree. The guest log at a quarter past ten. The staff schedules. The statements. Every one of your movements is corroborated twice over. " She glanced at the clock beneath her hand. "This was the one thing in the hotel we all trusted, and it lied to us. Your accounts have been pulled at from every side, and none of them came apart. " She let that stand a moment. "It is not always the most suspicious who are guilty, nor the most visible who are innocent.

Tonight the truth has been hard-won. " Then nothing, but the soft tick of the mantel clock, its hands set now to the true hour. Something in the room had shifted. Beatrice closed her notebook with a small snap and her shoulders came down, relief and grief arriving together. Sylvia's eyes shone; she blinked once, hard, and stood straighter than she had all night. Hugo poured himself a measure of whisky and lifted the glass an inch - to no one, to all of them. Release, then. Fragile enough. Beneath it the reckoning stayed: ambition blunted, loyalty put to the question, secrets dragged into the lamplight and left there. Eleanor watched their faces and saw the relief plainly, and beneath the relief the marks that doubt leaves. She wondered again whether justice ever put back what had been broken, or merely tidied the pieces. She turned to the mantel and let her hand rest on the cool brass.

"The clock will keep honest time now," she said, low. "Let us hope we can do the same. " The lounge, pale in the lamplight of a midsummer night, seemed to breathe out at last, the weight of accusation lifting - if only for a little while.

## Chapter 10: The Culprit Revealed

As the lamplight faded to a pale shimmer on the brass, Eleanor Voss let her hand linger on the cool mantel, the faint echo of her own words-"The clock will keep honest time now"-still suspended in the midsummer night. The Bluffside Hotel lounge, once thick with accusation, was quieter now, the fog beyond the windows thinning just enough to reveal the faint outlines of the sea. The air carried a cold tang of metal and the distant sound of wind off the cliffs, underscoring the hush that had settled over the room. No one moved at first; the clock ticked on, honest at last, and the weight of the evening pressed in, heavy as the silence after a storm. Beatrice Quill sat at the edge of a velvet chair, her notebook unopened in her lap, her posture less defiant than it had been. Eleanor found her gaze and held it.

"Miss Quill, you have been at the centre of suspicion-perhaps because you chase the truth so fiercely. Yet the evidence has spoken. I have reviewed the staff schedules and the corridor logs. More than one staff member saw you at reception from just before ten o'clock, and not one placed you anywhere near the lounge. The hotel logbook records every guest's movement, and not a single entry contradicts your presence at the desk. The timeline is simply too tight. Even if you'd wished to leave, the witnesses and the records would have exposed it. You could not have done it. " A flicker of a smile crossed Beatrice's lips, more rueful than relieved. "So I am cleared by the monotony of my own alibi, then. " She tapped her pen against the closed notebook, her voice softer than usual.

"The story I wanted to write nearly wrote me out of my own life. " The words hung in the air, and Eleanor recognized that Beatrice's ambition had become its own cage. She nodded, quietly affirming the innocence that the evidence had established. Sylvia Trent remained near the doorway, her hands clasped so tightly the knuckles blanched. Eleanor addressed her next, her tone gentle but precise. "Miss Trent, your loyalty to the hotel has never been in question, but the facts are now clear. The staff schedules place you in the service corridors during the entire window in which the crime could have occurred. The kitchen porter saw you at the linen cupboard, and the housemaid confirmed your presence at the kitchen hatch. Each statement matches the others; there is not a minute unaccounted for. The timeline, as it stands, rules you out entirely.

You could not have been in the lounge. " Sylvia's composure, so carefully maintained, was now a mask cracked at the edges. "Thank you, Miss Voss," she said, her voice trembling. "I only ever wanted the hotel to run. To protect what little order was left to us. I thought loyalty would be enough. " Her glance drifted to the patch of carpet where Captain Ivor Hale had stood. The conflict in her-between duty and truth-was etched in every line of her face. She pressed a hand to the back of a velvet chair, steadying herself. "We'll have to carry on, I suppose. There's no one left to wind the clock but me. " Hugo Vane, who had kept to the periphery throughout the ordeal, poured himself a measure of whisky from the sideboard, the sound of liquid against glass oddly loud in the quiet. Eleanor turned to him, her tone shifting to match his understated resilience. "Mr.

Vane, your suite log and the staff statement from the girl who brought your tea confirm your isolation. The guest suite logs show you never left your room until after the commotion began. The staff saw you only in your suite; your alibi is as firm as the hotel's own records. You are ruled out by the evidence, not by sentiment. " He raised the glass in a small, ironic salute to Eleanor. "Cleared by solitude-there's a verdict for the ages. I suppose I'll have to find another hotel, one with less honest clocks. " The remark was meant lightly, but his eyes betrayed a weariness that went deeper than the evening's revelations. He set the glass down untouched, his hand lingering on the polished wood. "You know, Miss Voss, I always thought the worst thing was being suspected. Turns out, it's being left behind. " Eleanor allowed herself a dry, understated smile.

"We are all left behind in some way, Mr. Vane. The difference is only in what we choose to remember. " Her gaze drifted to the mantel clock, its hands now aligned with the true hour, the grooves on the minute hand catching the light. The object that had anchored so many lies now stood as a silent witness to the cost of truth. She thought of Dr. Mallory Finch-not as the victim, but as the woman who had threatened to expose what others wished hidden. The price of honesty, Eleanor reflected, was rarely paid by the guilty alone. The room began to stir with the first signs of movement. Beatrice gathered her notebook, the pages still blank where she had meant to write the story's ending. Sylvia straightened the chairs, her motions mechanical, as if restoring order could mend what had been broken. Hugo drifted toward the window, hands in his pockets, watching the fog roll back from the cliffs.

The lounge, so recently a crucible of suspicion, felt emptied of purpose, its routines hollowed out by the night's revelations. Eleanor lingered by the mantel, her fingers tracing the faint scratches on the clock's casing. She thought of the confession Captain Ivor Hale had signed, the words that would echo through the hotel long after the uniforms had departed. There was no triumph in it-only the knowledge that justice, when it came, left scars of its own. The fragile peace restored to the Bluffside Hotel was a peace built on loss and compromise, not on certainty. As the lamplight flickered and the fog thinned to reveal the first pale hints of dawn, Eleanor let her hand fall from the clock. The room was quiet, save for the honest ticking of time, and the lives that would now have to move forward-each marked, in their own way, by the cost of truth.
