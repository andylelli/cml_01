Ottoline caught Harriet's eye, a small smile flickering at the corner of her mouth. "If we're lucky, the lions will be less trouble than the solicitors this season," she said, the jest coming at her own expense before anyone else could try.

Harriet's reply to Ottoline's hope was brief. "They bite less often." She picked up a glass paperweight from the sideboard, rolling it between her hands before setting it back exactly where it had been.

Marguerite regarded Harriet for a moment, the memory of their backstage confrontation held in the tilt of her head. "You never did forgive easily," she said, the words quiet, and neither of them looked away.

Harriet's answer, after a pause, was six words: "I never learned any other way." She turned, her gaze on the fire rather than Marguerite.

Ottoline, watching the interplay, shook her head with a rueful smile. "I once thought I'd be the one to ruin rehearsal, not anyone else," she said, her tone light as she patted the settee cushion. "Turns out, I'm only responsible for the odd tumble, not for murder."

Marguerite's laughter was brief, but genuine—her gloved hand covering her mouth as she reached for a folded program on the table, smoothing it flat as she remembered the first night of the season when Cecil Thorne had, with the faintest of smiles, handed it to her in the foyer.

Adela, rising to her feet, crossed to the window, her eyes on the gravel drive that curved toward the gate. She watched as the constable's car disappeared into the dusk, the taillights blinking twice before vanishing beyond the hedges. The world outside the manor, ordinary and indifferent, waited for no one.

Harriet, her hand on the glass, let the silence stretch. "It's strange to be grateful for being overlooked," she said, her voice almost thoughtful as she traced a circle in the condensation.

Ottoline, ever practical, rose and collected the empty cups, stacking them on the tray with care. "Better overlooked than overbooked," she quipped, glancing at the others with a grin. She carried the tray to the sideboard, setting it down with a flourish.

Marguerite, drawing her wrap about her shoulders, stood and faced Adela. "Thank you," she said, the words simple and unadorned. She reached for the silver cigarette case, hesitated, and set it down again, her eyes distant as she remembered a time when Cecil Thorne had once returned it to her after a party, his only comment a dry, "Left behind again, Miss Selwyn."

Adela nodded, her posture finally relaxed since the case had begun. She let her hand rest on the back of the chair, her mind already turning to the paperwork and the reports that would follow. The routine of her profession—the sorting, the listing, the final accounting—was a comfort she could almost welcome.

The lamp on the side table flickered as someone adjusted the shade, and for a moment, the room was golden and peaceful. The last of the day's tension ebbed away, replaced by the ordinary noises of a house settling into evening: the clink of cups, the soft shuffle of slippers on carpet, the murmured farewells as the women rose and drifted toward their own rooms.

At the door, Ottoline paused, her hand on the knob. "If anyone asks, I'll say I was feeding the lions," she joked softly, the line carrying back to the others with a note of old camaraderie.

Harriet smiled, just a little. "If anyone asks, I'll say you weren't."

Adela watched as Ottoline and Marguerite left the drawing room, their footsteps fading into the corridor. She moved to the umbrella stand, her fingers brushing the handle of Ivor's briefcase, but she left it where it was. There was nothing more to be done with it now.

The fire had burned down to embers, the brass fender warm beneath Adela's hand as she knelt to adjust it. For a moment, she allowed herself to remember Cecil Thorne, the man whose discipline kept the manor's world turning, and who, for all his sternness, had known precisely how much to say and when to say nothing at all.

Outside, the night drew in close, the fog curling about the eaves in silent witness to the case concluded. Inside, the drawing room settled into its peace, the truth at last spoken, the reckonings made, and the ordinary business of living ready, at last, to resume.

## Chapter 10: Quiet Restorations

"Let the fire go out, will you?" Marguerite Selwyn, socialite and circus patron, spoke quietly from the bedroom doorway, her wrap drawn close around her as she gazed at the gray embers in the hearth. Adela Halloway, private investigator, knelt at the grate, poker in hand, and nudged the last of the charred wood aside. Her movements were slow, deliberate—this was restoration rather than investigation, and it felt oddly restful after so many days of suspicion and calculation.

She straightened, brushed her hands on her dress, and walked to the writing desk where Cecil Thorne's ledger sat, untouched since the constables had come and gone. The desk was cleared now, the letters stacked, the clock—its hands reset and ticking once more—restored to its place of order on the mantel. Adela ran her fingers along the polished walnut and remembered the tension that had once filled the room, the weight of accusation hanging over every object. Adela had let the days grow strange with suspicion; now, she took up the ledger, turning its pages with care, resuming the work that kept the house's world in motion.

"Does it feel different, being here now?" Ottoline Fairweather, animal trainer, asked as she entered quietly, a folded linen cloth in her hand. She crossed to the window and wiped away the condensation, her reflection merging with the darkness beyond. She glanced over her shoulder at Adela, who was making a note in the ledger. Ottoline's quiet work with linen and polish brought warmth back to the room; the air no longer felt thick with secrets.

"It does," Adela replied, her voice measured. She closed the ledger and set it on the corner of the desk, her gaze lingering on the neat stack of correspondence. "The room has lost its edge." She picked up the letter opener, weighing it in her palm—a simple thing, cold and utilitarian, now stripped of menace. She remembered Cecil Thorne using it to slice open envelopes, his hands precise, his face impassive as he dealt with the day's demands.

A knock sounded at the open door. Harriet Bellamy, aerialist, stepped inside, her limp subdued by the calm of the evening. She carried a fresh log for the fire, the bark damp from the woodpile. Without a word, she set it on the hearth and used the poker to prod the embers, the gesture practical and free of ceremony. The logs would burn again tomorrow, when the ordinary rhythm of morning called for warmth.

"Are you staying on, Miss Halloway?" Harriet asked, glancing up from the hearth. She reached for a match to light the lamp by the bed. The flare of sulphur caught the edge of her hair, casting a golden glow over her face. She set the matchbox on the table and adjusted the lamp's wick.

"Not long," Adela replied. She returned the letter opener to its place in the desk drawer, closing it with a soft click. "My work here is done—at least for now." She regarded Harriet with a cool appreciation. The lamp's light settled over the room.

Marguerite moved to the wardrobe, opening it with a sigh. She withdrew a fresh shirt—one of Cecil Thorne's, crisply laundered and never worn again—and folded it into a neat square. She pressed the cloth to her face for a moment, the gesture private, and then set it atop the bureau. The business of mourning, she thought, was as much in the folding of linen as in the saying of prayers.

Ottoline, satisfied with the state of the window, crossed to the writing desk and opened the top drawer. She withdrew a stack of blank paper and a fountain pen, setting both beside the ledger. "I'll see to the animal feed logs in the morning," she said. She capped the pen and placed it on the blotter, the act as ordinary as any she had performed before the world turned inside out.

Harriet, voice softer than usual, remarked, "It's the first night in a long while the kitchen's quiet." She leaned against the mantel. The steady movement of the clock's hands drew her attention. "No one watching, no one waiting." She let her fingers rest on the clock, her touch light. The tick of the second hand was a comfort—proof that time, at least, moved forward.

Marguerite, carrying the folded shirt, stepped into the corridor. She paused at the threshold, her silhouette framed by the dim lamplight. "The staff will need guidance," she said. "Someone must continue the work Cecil began." She did not look back, but her words hung in the air, a quiet affirmation of continuity.

Adela gathered her notes, tucking them into her satchel. She paused at the desk. The proof of Ivor Yardley's guilt was settled, and her mind, at last, was free to consider the ordinary: the accounts, the correspondence, the small repairs that would restore the manor to its careful order.

Ottoline lifted the lamp and crossed to the door, her shadow stretching along the hallway floor. She set the lamp on a narrow table just outside and straightened a stack of estate bulletins. She glanced back once, catching Adela's eye, and smiled—a simple acknowledgment that, for tonight, their work was finished.

Harriet moved to the window, opening it a crack to let in the night air. The fog had lifted, revealing the faint outline of the circus tents beyond the stable yard. She watched for a moment, the quiet reassuring, before she turned back to the hearth and set the poker in its stand.

Adela, left alone for a moment, moved to the wardrobe and opened the bottom drawer. She withdrew a pair of gloves—Cecil Thorne's, the leather worn but supple—and held them in her hands. She remembered the way he had pulled them on before walking the grounds each morning, his stride purposeful. She recalled the faint scent of tobacco and the tap of his cane on the flagstones, the nod he gave to the stable boy, and the way his boots left a sharp print in the dew. She set the gloves back in the drawer, closing it gently.

Marguerite, returning from the corridor, carried a vase of fresh flowers—dahlias from the last of the manor's beds. She set them on the bedside table, arranging the stems with care. The scent was faint, but it filled the room with a reminder of the world beyond these walls, resilient and untroubled by tragedy.

Ottoline reentered, a basket of folded linens balanced on her hip. She placed it on the bed, then began to sort the sheets, humming under her breath. Each item restored to its proper place was an act of recovery, a quiet assertion that the ordinary rhythms of the house would prevail.

Adela, notebook in hand, crossed to the hall. She paused at the top of the stairs, listening to the sounds of the house settling into its night: the clink of china in the pantry, the scrape of a chair on flagstone, the whisper of wind at the casement. She let the hush settle around her, content in the knowledge that the worst was past.

Below, in the scullery, the kitchen maid rinsed a stack of cups. The heat of the water reddened her skin. She set each cup on the rack with care, the sound of porcelain on wood a small, persistent comfort.

In the stable yard, Ottoline's assistant led a horse to water, the animal's breath steaming in the chill. The routines of feeding, mucking, and bedding down began again, as they always did, regardless of tragedy or triumph inside the house.

Harriet, having gathered the last of her things, closed the bedroom door behind her. She paused in the corridor, her hand resting on the banister. She looked back once, pensive.

Adela, at the stairhead, watched the others disperse to their rooms. She set her satchel down, the weight of evidence and suspicion finally eased. The clock on the landing ticked steadily, untroubled by memory.

In the end, the house was left to its quiet work: the fire banked, the windows closed against the night, the rooms restored to their measured order. The tragedy was not forgotten, but the world—of ledgers, linens, and lamps—resumed, one ordinary act at a time.
